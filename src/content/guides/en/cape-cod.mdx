## Direct answer: take the side you can defend at the Canal, then commit to one crossing

### The one-sentence answer

On **Cape Cod** in a public FFA, the single decision that matters is not which town you
start in but which side of the **Cape Cod Canal** you end up on, because the map is not one
island and it is not one peninsula — it is three playable zones, a mainland on the west, the
Cape itself on the east, and a string of islands to the south, and the mainland and the Cape
are two separate land masses that touch water at almost every row except one. That one place is
the Canal, the narrowest water crossing on the whole map, and it is where the game forces its
only true land-versus-land fight. If you can hold the Canal line, you own the map's spine and the
rest of the board is a matter of economy and timing. If you cannot, you are a flank no matter how
many good towns surround you, and a well-positioned enemy can funnel every crossing through a
gap only a couple of tiles wide.

### What "side" means on this map

A new player who lands on the spawn screen sees thirty-one named towns — Plymouth, Falmouth,
Nantucket, Chatham, Wellfleet, and so on — and has no idea which cluster they belong to, what
the map is really made of, or where the game will force a fight. The town name is only the place
you start from; it does not tell you the strategic region. "Side" means: are you on the mainland,
on the Cape, or on an island, and which side of the Canal does that put you? The mainland and the
Cape are on opposite sides of the narrow gap, so a mainland town and a Cape town that are close
on the screen can still be on opposite Canal sides. The islands are mostly on the south sea lanes
and interact with the gap by sea rather than by land. Reading your spawn as a zone, not a town,
removes most of the confusion the thirty-one names create, and it is the step that turns the
spawn screen into a real decision.

### The three lines to write down

Before you send a second wave, write down three lines: which zone you are in, which side of the
Canal you are on, and what your first-minute priority is (hold the gap, break the gap, or open a
sea lane). A good Cape Cod opening ends the first phase with a protected core, a named Canal side,
and a genuinely separate fallback that still works if the Canal fight goes wrong. A bad one ends
with a long thin line and a hypothetical crossing that cannot answer an enemy attack. This page
turns the raw spawn list into that decision, then tells you how to play the crossing whether you
are the side that holds it or the side that has to break it, and it repeats the answer only in the
form that changes a player's decision in the live match.

## What Cape Cod actually is: three zones, two land masses

### Two main land masses, not one

The single most useful thing to understand about this map is its topology, and the topology is
boring in the best possible way. A local parse of the shipped `map4x` terrain — a 750 by 620 grid
with 143,346 land tiles — shows that the land falls into a small number of connected components,
and two of them dominate everything else: a **mainland** component of roughly 76,600 tiles on the
western and central part of the map, and a **Cape** component of roughly 48,700 tiles on the east.
These two are **separate land masses**. They do not share a land bridge across most of the map;
instead they are separated by a wide body of water — the bay and the outer Atlantic — that opens
to hundreds of tiles in the south and only narrows to a couple of tiles in one specific spot. The
rest of the land is a long thin hook that curls around the Cape and a handful of small islands,
which together form the third zone rather than a fourth major region.

### The islands and the sea

Everything else on the map is smaller: the Cape's hook, the islands in the south, and a handful
of tiny islets. That is what makes the spawn screen confusing — thirty-one names but only two
real strategic regions plus a set of islands, and the islands matter mostly as secondary
footholds and sea lanes rather than as primary power centers. When you look at the map in game,
the water is the dominant feature, not the land: the mainland is a big blob, the Cape is a long
narrow hook that curls around it, and the islands are small dots to the south. If you only take
away one mental image, take this one: a large land mass and a long thin hook, with a gap between
them that is a wide bay for most of its length and a tight canal at exactly one place. The sea is
not decoration here; it is the main connector between the two big land masses everywhere except
the Canal, so a map you can think about as "mostly water with two islands" is the right mental
model to bring into the first match.

### Why the shape matters

The consequence of the topology is that the map is naturally split into a west-to-east contest.
The mainland has more total land, which means more room to expand inland and more room to absorb
a bad opening. The Cape has less total land but a longer, more exposed coastline and a narrower
shape, which means it is more vulnerable to a flanking naval approach and more dependent on
holding the Canal to stay connected to the interior. The islands sit between and around both and
mostly decide who controls the sea lanes. None of this is a secret; it is just the shape of the
map, and reading it once before your first match removes most of the confusion that the thirty-one
spawn names create. The shape also means the map is fast in the center: because the Canal is close
to the middle-north, the two main zones meet early, so a Cape Cod FFA reaches its first real
border fight sooner than a map with a long open border, and the player who figures out their zone
first usually sets the pace for the whole match.

## The Cape Cod Canal: the one real crossing

### Where the gap is

The entire strategic identity of Cape Cod rests on one geographic fact: the mainland and the Cape
come within a couple of tiles of each other at exactly one location, and everywhere else the water
between them is wide. That narrow location is the **Cape Cod Canal**, and it is the only place
where a land expansion from one side can reach the other without first building a naval force and
crossing open water. In the terrain data, the narrowest mainland-to-Cape water gap is about two
tiles on the `map4x` grid — roughly five full-resolution tiles — running along a short vertical
stretch near the middle-north of the map, around rows 236 to 241. Everywhere to the south, the gap
between the two land masses widens dramatically into the open bay, where the distance is measured
in hundreds of tiles and a crossing means a real naval operation, not a land push. The contrast is
the whole point: one tight gap and one wide bay.

### What the gap is worth

This is the map's version of a strait, and it plays exactly like the chokepoint logic you already
know from other maps. Whoever holds the Canal controls the pace of the whole game, because the
side on the other side of it has exactly one overland way in. If you hold the Canal, you can choose
when to let anyone cross and you can concentrate your entire defense on a two-tile gap instead of
a hundred-mile coastline. If you do not hold it, every expansion you want toward the other side is
a decision point: send a land force into a known funnel, or build ships and risk a sea crossing
where the enemy can screen you. The Canal also creates a clean defensive line, because the gap is
so narrow that a single well-placed row of structures or a defended border segment can block
overland movement at a fraction of the cost of defending the open coast. That is why the map
rewards the player who figures out their Canal side early.

### The wide bay that goes around it

The wide bay matters because it is the escape route and the flank route at the same time. The side
that holds the Canal is not safe just by holding the narrow gap, because the enemy can go around
through the bay by sea and attack the holder's coast from the side that was not being watched. The
side that has to break the Canal can choose to avoid the narrow gap entirely and run a sea crossing
through the bay, which costs ships but sidesteps the defender's concentrated line. So the Canal is
never the only fight; it is the center of the fight, and the bay is the flank. Read [Strait of
Gibraltar openings](/guides/strait-of-gibraltar-openings/) for the general chokepoint logic,
because Cape Cod applies the same rule on a smaller, faster board. A player who wins the Canal but
leaves the bay wide open usually still loses the map, and a player who screens the bay but ignores
the gap usually gives the enemy the interior for free. The two lines have to be played together.

## The mainland side: twelve towns and a roomy interior

### Where the mainland towns sit

Twelve of the thirty-one spawns sit on the mainland component, and they form the map's most
forgiving opening. The mainland towns — Plymouth, Taunton, Brockton, Rochester, Bridgewater, New
Bedford, South Carver, Marshfield, Mattapoisett, Wareham, Manomet, and Bourne — cluster across the
western and central land mass, from the far north-west corner down to the mid-south coast. Because
the mainland is the larger of the two components, a mainland start gives you the most inland room:
there is a lot of land behind you, a lot of neutral territory to absorb, and a lot of room to build
a compact core before anyone forces a border fight. The north-west towns sit far from the Canal and
have the longest, most protected interior, while the mid-south towns like Wareham, Manomet, and
Bourne sit closer to the bay and to the southern end of the Canal line.

### The mainland's strength

The mainland's strength is interior depth and the Canal defense. From a mainland start you can
expand inland first, build up a real economy, and only then look at the Canal as a line to hold. A
town like **Taunton** or **Brockton** in the north-west corner has a long, protected interior behind
it and is far from the Canal, so the first several minutes are about building, not fighting. A town
like **Bourne** or **Manomet** sits closer to the bay, so it feels more naval from minute one but
still has the full mainland behind it. If you are bad at naval play or you simply want a slow,
defensible start, the mainland is the safe side, and the safety comes from the room to build a
reserve before the Canal becomes a live fight. The player who uses that room well can afford to
lose the first skirmish and still come out on top because the interior kept paying.

### The mainland's risk

The downside of the mainland is that it is the big, obvious target. The Cape side has exactly one
overland way into the mainland, and the islands flank the coast, so a coordinated enemy can
pressure you from the Canal and the sea at the same time. The rule for a mainland start is to earn
the Canal before you spend it: use your interior depth to build a reserve, place a Port if your
coast supports it, and only then commit to holding or attacking the narrow gap. If you expand
inland and ignore the Canal while an enemy builds up there, you trade your safety for a position
you cannot keep. The mainland is the map's safe side, but safe is relative: it is safe against a
sloppy enemy and dangerous against one who concentrates on the one thing that actually connects the
two halves. For the general discipline of building a compact, defensible core before pushing, read
[Tall versus wide](/guides/tall-vs-wide/).

## The Cape side: ten towns on a long, exposed hook

### Where the Cape towns sit

Ten spawns sit on the Cape component — the long, thin hook that curls out to the east and north.
They are Falmouth, Sandwich, Mashpee, Barnstable, Dennis, Chatham, Orleans, Eastham, Wellfleet, and
Provincetown, and they run from the Cape's base near the Canal out to the northern tip at
Provincetown. The Cape start is the opposite of the mainland start: less interior room, a longer and
more exposed coastline, and a shape that is easy to flank from the sea. If you like naval play,
long coastal fronts, and being the side that has to actively defend a long line, the Cape is your
side. If you want a slow build with a big safe interior, the Cape will feel exposed and cramped.
The base towns sit close to the mainland and the Canal, while the north towns sit far out on the
hook with almost nothing behind them.

### The Cape's strength

The Cape's defining feature is that it is a hook, not a blob. Every Cape town is close to the water,
and the land narrows as you go north, so a Cape start has a shorter, more dangerous expansion than
a mainland start. **Falmouth** and **Sandwich** are near the base of the Cape, close to the Canal and
the mainland, so they are the most connected and the most contested: an enemy across the Canal can
reach them quickly overland. **Provincetown** at the north tip is the furthest, most isolated, and
most naval of all the Cape starts: it has a long coast, little interior, and its main interactions
are with the sea and with the islands. The middle Cape towns — **Barnstable**, **Dennis**,
**Chatham**, **Orleans**, **Eastham** — form the Cape's spine and are where most of the
Cape-versus-mainland fighting over the Canal and the eastern coast actually happens. The Cape holds
the eastern ocean, which is real power on a water-heavy map.

### The Cape's risk

The Cape start trades interior safety for two things: a dominant position on the eastern coastline
and, critically, the option to be the side that **breaks** the Canal rather than holds it. If you
are on the Cape and the mainland side is the one that wants to come out to you, then the Cape side
gets to defend the narrow gap and the mainland side pays the cost of the chokepoint assault. But the
Cape's long coast is also its vulnerability: a naval screen along the hook can strangle a Cape
economy that depends on the sea, and because the land narrows, there is no deep interior to fall
back into when the front breaks. Read [Island defense](/guides/island-defense/) for how to screen a
long coast with transports and a defense post, because the Cape coastline is exactly the long,
exposed coast that page is about. The Cape is the map's aggressive side: less safe, more exposed,
but the side that controls the eastern ocean and can dictate when the Canal breaks.

## The island starts: nine footholds and the sea lanes

### Where the islands sit

Nine of the thirty-one spawns are island or islet starts, and they form the third zone of the map:
Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, Siasconset, Nantucket, Naushon, Nashawena, Nomans Land, Tuckernuck, and the
larger island bodies they sit on. These range from two real islands — **Nantucket** (with Siasconset
near it) and the Martha's Vineyard body (with Edgartown and Oak Bluffs on it) — to a handful of tiny
islets like Naushon, Nashawena, Nomans Land, and Tuckernuck. An island start is a completely
different game from a mainland or Cape start: you have very little land, your entire economy depends
on the sea, and your survival depends on keeping at least one sea lane open. The islands sit mostly
in the south of the map, strung between the mainland, the Cape, and the open Atlantic, and they
decide who controls the southern sea lanes.

### The island's strength

An island start is the highest-risk, highest-flexibility option on the map. You have almost no
interior, so you cannot fall back; you are on the coast from the first second, so a naval screen
against you is immediate; but you are also in the middle of the water, which means you can hit both
the mainland and the Cape coastlines and you can contest the sea lanes that the other two zones
depend on. A start on a real island with two spawns and enough land to support a Port and a defense
post is the strongest of the island options, because it has economic depth behind the sea lane. A
start on a tiny islet like **Tuckernuck** or **Nomans Land** is a pure sea-lane probe with almost no
economic depth behind it: it is a forward position, not a home, and it only makes sense as a
screen or a stepping stone toward a bigger island or a coastal target. The island's strength is
flexibility, and its weakness is that there is nowhere to retreat to.

### The island's risk

The rule for an island start is to own a sea lane before you own anything else, because you cannot
afford to lose it. If your single sea lane is blocked, you are trapped and lose on the clock, and
because the islands sit in the south between the two main zones, a blockaded island can become an
isolated dot that the mainland and Cape both ignore. Read [Transport landings](/guides/transport-landings/)
for how to run a crossing that keeps a fallback lane open, and read [Trade vs piracy](/guides/trade-vs-piracy/)
for how to keep a sea route paying instead of burning it. The islands are not a comfortable opening;
they are a choice to play the map's naval game from the inside. If you pick an island, commit to the
sea, keep one lane open, and treat your tiny land base as a forward position, not a home. The
island player who keeps a lane alive controls the south of the map; the one whose lane closes has
already lost.

## The first minute, by zone

### Mainland first minute

The first minute on Cape Cod is about converting your spawn into a zone identity, and it is fast
because the map is small and the Canal is close. For a **mainland** start, expand inland first.
Take the neutral land directly behind your town before you look at the water, build your starting
City to capacity, and place a Port only if your coast reaches one without exposing the core. By the
end of the first minute you should have a compact two-or-three-pocket core, a named Canal side, and
a Port plan that is checked, not bought blindly. The mainland's first minute is a building minute;
the fighting comes later, and the player who wastes the early interior on a coastal gamble gives
away the map's slow-burn advantage. A north-west town can afford to be slow; a mid-south town should
be a little faster because it sits closer to the Canal line.

### Cape first minute

For a **Cape** start, secure your coastal line and name the Canal. Because the Cape is narrow,
expand along the hook rather than into open water, and decide in the first minute whether you will
defend the Canal gap or push toward it. Place a defense post on the gap if you intend to hold it,
and screen the eastern coast if you intend to make the enemy come to you. The Cape's first minute is
a positioning minute: you are deciding who pays for the chokepoint. The base towns near Falmouth
should be looking at the gap immediately, while a north town like Provincetown should be looking at
the north sea lane and the islands. The difference is that a Cape player is always one step closer
to the fight than a mainland player, so the Cape's first minute is where the map's tempo gets set.

### Island first minute

For an **island** start, open and hold a sea lane. Place a Port and a defense post on your one good
coast, screen the lane with a Warship, and keep one alternative landing alive. The island's first
minute is a survival minute: if you cannot keep a lane open, you have no opening at all. A real-island
start has the room to do this properly, while a tiny islet start has to move fast because it has
almost no land to defend the lane from. The island player's first-minute output is a lane, not a
position.

### The three-line output

Whichever zone you are in, the first-minute output is the same three lines: which side of the Canal
you are on, what you are defending, and what your fallback is if the Canal fight goes wrong. Write
those three lines down in chat or in your own head before you send a second wave. A start that
produces those three lines cleanly is a start that can be played; a start that cannot name them is a
start that is improvising, and on a fast map like Cape Cod, improvising is how the map gets lost. The
first minute is not long, but it is the minute, and the three lines are the whole of it.

## Playing the Canal: crossing and defending

### If you hold the Canal

The Canal is where the map is decided, and it is a classic chokepoint with a narrow gap, a
defensive line, and one overland route. The two sides play it differently, and the difference is
mostly about who has the cost. If you **hold the Canal** — you are on the mainland and the Cape
wants to come to you, or vice versa — your job is to make the gap more expensive to cross than it
is worth crossing. Build a defensive line on your side of the gap, a row of structures or a
defended border segment that spans the narrowest tiles, and keep a reserve force able to arrive at
the gap faster than the attacker can replace a lost wave. Because the gap is only a couple of tiles
wide, a well-placed defense blocks overland movement at a small cost, which is the whole point of
holding it. Screen the sea lanes on the wide side so the enemy cannot simply go around by boat, and
time your attack on their flanks for the moment they overcommit a large force into the funnel. Read
[Defense posts](/guides/defense-posts/) for the cost and range of the structure you anchor the line
with.

### If you break the Canal

If you **break the Canal** — you need to cross the narrow gap to reach the other side — your job is
to make the crossing survivable instead of suicidal. Do not throw a single all-in wave into the
funnel; send a probe to measure the defender's response, then commit a force sized to absorb that
response. Bring a Warship to screen the wide-bay alternative so the defender cannot reinforce your
crossing by sea, and keep a reserve so that if the first wave is stopped, the second one still
exists. A broken Canal gives you the other side's interior, so a successful break is worth a real
investment, but a failed one leaves you thin on your own side and exposed to a counterattack. Read
[Annexation and enclosure](/guides/annexation-enclosure/) for how to close a pocket around a
conquered neck without overextending the line. The breaker's discipline is the same as any
chokepoint assault: measure first, commit second, and never go in with no reserve.

### Tempo is the meta

The meta-game of the Canal is tempo. The holder controls when crossings happen; the breaker
controls where they happen by choosing between the narrow gap and a wider sea crossing. Win the
tempo and you win the map, because the Canal is the only line that connects the two big land
masses and every other fight on the board feeds into it. A holder who lets the breaker pick the
timing and the place is a holder in name only. The practical read is to always be the side that
decides the pace: if you hold, force the breaker to spend on the gap or go around the bay; if you
break, choose the moment when the holder's reserve is committed elsewhere. The Canal fight is short,
but it is the long fight of the map compressed into a few minutes, and the player who treats it as
a tempo problem rather than a single skirmish usually takes the map.

## Failures, counters, and exit signals

### The two root mistakes

Cape Cod has a small number of sharp failure modes, and they all trace back to the same two
mistakes: ignoring the Canal, or ignoring the sea. The first failure is the mainland player who
builds a beautiful interior and never looks at the Canal, only to find the Cape side holding the
narrow gap and dictating the whole game. The counter is to name your Canal side in the first minute
and spend a little Gold early to establish a line there before it is too late to do so cheaply. The
second failure is the Cape or island player who has a sea lane that quietly closes while they are
busy fighting on land; the counter is to keep one alternative lane open at all times and to screen
it with a Warship so the closure is visible, not silent. Both mistakes come from the same root:
treating one line as the only line.

### The overcommit and the flank

The third failure is the overcommit: sending a large force into the Canal funnel without a reserve,
so that a stopped wave leaves you unable to continue on your own side. The counter is the
probe-then-commit pattern from the previous section. The fourth is the flank ignore: holding the
Canal but leaving the wide-bay coast unguarded, so the enemy goes around by sea and attacks your
economy from the side you were not watching. The counter is a minimal screen on the wide lane even
when you are busy at the gap. The overcommit and the flank are the two ways a player who is clearly
in control still loses the map, and both are avoidable with a reserve and a screen. The player who
keeps a reserve and a screen rarely loses a map they are winning, because the two classic
reversals are both blocked before they start.

### Exit signals

Exit signals tell you when a zone plan has stopped working and you should switch. End a mainland
plan when the Canal is held by a stronger human and you cannot break it cheaply. End a Cape plan
when your long coast is being blockaded and your economy is no longer feeding the front. End an
island plan the moment your last sea lane is at risk, because an island has no interior to fall
back to. When a plan exits, write the new primary and fallback before sending another wave; a
Cape Cod match that keeps replacing a failed plan with another failed plan is the most common way
the map gets lost. Read [Threat assessment](/guides/threat-assessment/) for how to scan the whole
board and pick the next target once the Canal is settled. Knowing when to stop is as important as
knowing where to start, and the exit signal is the line that separates a plan from a habit.

## Version limits and what changes when a new release lands

### What is verified

This page is verified to the upstream commit that added Cape Cod — PR #5071, commit `3223573e8`,
shipped in the **v0.34.0** release — and to the shipped `map4x` manifest as of 2026-10-10:
thirty-one spawn nations, a 750 by 620 `map4x` grid, and 143,346 land tiles, with a local terrain
parse confirming the two main land components and the narrow Canal gap. The spawn names, the
three-zone split, the two main land masses, and the Canal as the single narrow crossing are the
stable facts. If a future version changes none of those, the core of this page still holds: pick
your zone, name your Canal side, and play the gap. The verified facts are the ones you can rely on
from match to match, and they are the part of the map that does not move under your feet.

### What is not stable

The things that make a specific match different are not stable, and you should re-check them when
a new version lands. Map assets, rotation weights, the Canal's exact width in a future terrain
revision, Nation behavior, and naval prices can all change between releases, and any of those
changes can move which side of the Canal is the better one. Mode changes matter just as much: in a
random-spawn lobby you cannot preselect a town, so you must re-run the per-zone first-minute plan
after placement instead of assuming a chosen start; in a room with a different map size, the
distance numbers and the Canal's relative importance shift, so re-read the board rather than
scaling a remembered timestamp; and in a team room, a teammate can hold a different side of the
Canal, which changes the calculus from a single line-hold into coordinated two-sided control. For
shared positioning read [Team spawn formation](/guides/team-spawn-formation/).

### The four-line note

The map also appears in the map list with a multiplayer frequency that affects how often you meet
it in public play, so a rotation weight change can change how much you should study it, and a new
version can change the spawn screen itself. Before you queue, make a four-line note: your zone,
your side of the Canal, your defense or attack plan at the gap, and your exit signal. After the
