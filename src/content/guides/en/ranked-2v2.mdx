Ranked 2v2 is OpenFront's two-per-team competitive ladder, added in v0.33.0. You
play a ranked match against one partner, on a separate ladder with its own
leaderboard, in a mode that adds real cooperative elements that 1v1 and ordinary
Team play do not have. If you treat 2v2 like a 1v1 match you just add a friend
to, you will lose matches you should win. The mode has a fixed contract: four
players, two teams of two, a 60-second post-selection immunity window, Gold and
Troop donation between teammates, a 50% chance of a compact map, and a finish that
ends the moment one team is wiped. This guide tells you exactly what changes, how
to pick and vet a partner, how to divide the map and the first attack, when to
donate, and how to read the three ways a 2v2 game can end.

## What Ranked 2v2 actually changes versus 1v1

The single most important thing to internalize is that Ranked 2v2 is not "1v1 with
a friend." It is a different mode with its own matchmaking, its own ladder, its
own leaderboard tab, and mechanics that only exist here. When you queue 2v2 the
game fills four players across two teams of two, and you are matched with a
partner rather than a full opposing field. That partner relationship changes
nearly every decision you would make solo: you can now coordinate an attack
target, you can split the economy so neither of you is forced to contest the
same pocket, and you can transfer Gold and Troops to keep both lanes alive.

The mode also disables Nations for the match. In 1v1 and Team play you choose a
Nation that grants a specific unit or economic bonus, and the rest of your build
is designed around that bonus. In 2v2 the Nations selection is off, so you do not
get that starting bonus and you cannot build your opening around a Nation-specific
unit. Instead your opening is a pure, symmetric build: both of you start from the
same base kit, and the difference comes down to map control, lane timing, and how
you coordinate with your partner. That symmetry is a feature for the player who
understands it, because it removes the "which Nation is best in a specific map"
guesswork and turns the match into a contest of execution.

The map itself is also different. There is a 50% chance you load onto a compact
map, which means a smaller playable area with tighter pockets and a shorter
distance between your spawn and the enemy spawn. Compact 2v2 games carry 100
background bots and a 10-minute timer, while non-compact games carry 400 bots and
a 15-minute timer. So you must be ready to play two different games: a fast,
close-quarters brawl on the compact map, and a longer, more territorial game on
the standard map. The compact maps used are Australia at 40%, Iceland at 20%,
Asia at 20%, and EuropeClassic at 20%, and their tight layouts mean your partner
and the enemy pair are all within striking range almost immediately. If you want
a refresher on the general map and territory vocabulary that 2v2 builds on, the
[first match guide](/guides/first-match/) walks through spawn choices and the
opening expansion that this mode then compresses.

One more consequence of the symmetry: because Nations are off, the enemy pair is
running the same base kit as you, so you cannot use a Nation mismatch to hide a
bad opening. If your opening is weak on a map you have not played, the enemy pair
will feel the same weakness but you will not have a bonus to offset it, which
means the map you load onto matters more in 2v2 than it does in a Nation-enabled
mode. Reading the compact-map pool and knowing which of the four compact maps you
are most comfortable on is part of preparing for the ladder, not an optional
extra.

## Choosing and vetting a partner before you queue

The community's loudest 2v2 complaint is the partner problem. Players repeatedly
ask where to find a teammate, complain that they never get one, and wonder
whether queuing with a known friend is worth it compared to waiting for a random
partner. The v0.33.0 release made it possible to choose a 2v2 teammate before
queueing, which means you are not forced to accept a random stranger if you have
a fixed partner. If you have someone you regularly play with, always queue with
them and lock the partnership before the match starts. A pre-arranged partner who
already knows your habits is worth far more than a higher-rated stranger, because
the value of 2v2 comes from coordination, and coordination only exists if you have
played together before.

If you are queueing for a random partner, vet them using the data you can see
before the game. The unofficial OFstats leaderboard publishes a separate ranked
board that is weighted by games played and a separate team-wins board that counts
team wins for everyone. Look at a candidate partner's games played and win rate
before the queue: a player who has played a large sample of ranked matches and
holds a reasonable win rate is a safer partner than a player with a few matches
and a volatile rating. You can also look at their team-wins record to confirm they
actually contribute to team games rather than only surviving alongside a strong
teammate. Treat a very low games-played count or a team-wins rate that is far
below their ranked win rate as a warning that the player is not reliable in the
cooperative half of the mode, and consider waiting for a different partner rather
than queueing blind. The goal is a partner who can hold a lane independently,
because the moment you are tied up in a fight, your partner is on their own.

A practical default if you cannot vet in advance is to favor the partner with the
more stable, recent sample over the one with the higher peak rating, because a
high peak can come from a short hot streak that does not survive a real queue,
while a steady mid-level record over many games usually means the player holds a
lane when it matters. If two candidates are close, prefer the one whose win rate
is consistent week to week rather than the one that swung hard either direction,
because a swinging record usually means a player who is changing builds or
positions and will not be predictable as a lane holder for you.

## The 60-second window and the two ways it ends

Every 2v2 match opens with a 60-second immunity window after selection. For the
first 60 seconds after you pick your spawn, neither of your teammates can be hit
by the enemy pair, so the opening is a protected period rather than an immediate
skirmish. You must use that window to do two things at once: establish a safe
economic base and decide the lane split with your partner. Because the window
protects everyone, the enemy pair is also spending it safely, which means the
first real contact happens roughly 60 seconds after selection rather than
instantly. That changes your opening economy compared to a normal game: you do
not need to panic-expand in the first few seconds, but you do need to be expanding
and committed to a lane by the time the window closes, because the first fight
arrives the moment the immunity ends.

The mode also has two hard failure states you must understand before you queue.
First, if fewer than all four players have spawned by the end of the selection
phase, the game is cancelled as a whole: it never starts, it never counts as
ranked, and nobody gets a result. So a 2v2 that drops before selection finishes
gives you no ladder movement at all, which is why a stable partner and a queue
you commit to matter. Second, and this is the one that decides most 2v2 games,
the game ends the instant exactly one team is still standing. The moment both of
you or both of your opponents are eliminated, the match is over. That means 2v2
does not wait for a percentage-of-land lead or a timer in most real games; the
percentage and the 10/15-minute timer are fallbacks for the rare game where
neither team is fully wiped. Understanding that a single team wipe ends the game
immediately is the core strategic fact of the mode, and it shapes every decision
below. Treat the 60-second window as the one period where you can set up safely,
because after it closes you will be fighting for the wipe while trying not to get
wiped yourself.

A useful mental model for the window is that it is a build-and-commit period, not
a fight period. You should use the full 60 seconds to get your income going and
your lane position set, then be ready to commit the moment it drops. The error
that loses here is either committing too early while still protected (wasting the
window by fighting when you should be building) or committing too late (losing the
first exchange because you were still undecided when the immunity lifted). Because
both teams have the same 60 seconds and the same symmetric kit, the team that
decides its split and its target before the window ends has a real structural
advantage, since the undecided team is reacting while the decided team is
executing.

## Splitting lanes: the first attack target and the pocket

The most common 2v2 mistake is both players expanding into the same pocket, which
leaves one of you without a clean economic position and both of you exposed to
the enemy pair. Instead you should divide the map before you spend a single unit.
The standard split is an attacker and a laner: one of you takes the contested,
aggressive pocket closest to the enemy spawn and is responsible for the first
attack and the fight, while the other takes the safer side pocket and is
responsible for the economy and for reinforcing when the attacker is in trouble.
Which of you takes which role should be decided by your own strengths and by the
map: the more mechanically aggressive player takes the attack lane, and the more
economically disciplined player takes the income lane.

The division is not only about two pockets; it is about the attack target. When
the 60-second window is about to close, the attacker must already have a target
picked: a specific enemy unit or building that, if destroyed, wins the early
fight. You and your partner should agree on that target before the window ends, so
that the moment immunity drops both of you are moving toward the same objective
rather than reacting. If you both attack the same target you double your damage
and can kill it before the enemy pair can defend it, but that requires the lane
split to be clean so the enemy pair cannot simply split their defense against
your concentrated push. A clean lane split plus a pre-agreed target during the
window is the single biggest win condition in early 2v2, because it converts your
cooperative advantage into a concrete first-blood edge before either economy is
fully built. If your partner picks a target and you ignore it, you split your own
damage and the enemy pair wins the exchange, so discipline on the agreed target
is as important as the map split itself.

When you decide who takes the attack lane and who takes the income lane, match the
role to the player who would win the moment the other is busy. The attacker is
exposed and fighting, so the income player must be able to hold their pocket and
protect the base without help for a long stretch; the income player is safe and
building, so they can afford to reinforce the attacker with a donation when the
fight turns. If you put the more fragile, aggressive player on the income lane and
the more defensive player attacking, you will invert the roles and lose the early
fight, because the defender will get caught defending a pocket they are not built
for. The role assignment is therefore not arbitrary; it is a direct expression of
who in the pair is better at holding ground and who is better at winning an
exchange, and getting it right is the precondition for the pre-agreed target to
actually work.

## Donation: when Gold and Troops transfer wins

Ranked 2v2 enables both Gold donation and Troop donation between teammates, and
this is the mechanical advantage that makes 2v2 different from a casual team
match. The core idea is that you and your partner share one effective economy:
whenever one of you is behind and the other is ahead, the ahead player can send
Gold or Troops to the behind player so that the lane is not lost. The tactical
rule is to donate to whoever is holding the lane you actually want to keep. If
the attacker is losing a fight and the income lane is strong, the income player
donates Gold to keep the attacker's economy going so they can rebuild and re-push.
If the income lane is under pressure and the attacker is already winning, the
attacker donates Troops to help the income player hold the lane and protect the
base.

The discipline that wins is not donating constantly; it is donating at the moment
a lane is about to flip. Constant small donations spread your economy thin and
leave both lanes underbuilt, while a decisive donation at the moment of danger
turns a lost lane into a held one. You should watch both lanes' relative
economic health continuously, and the player who is ahead is the one who makes
the donation call, because they have the surplus to spare. A common losing
pattern in 2v2 is both players donating to each other in a death spiral where
neither lane is ever strong, which means neither lane can win. The fix is a
clear, standing rule you agree on before the match: the lane that is losing takes
the donation, the lane that is winning keeps building, and you only donate enough
to change the lane's outcome, not so much that the donor's own lane collapses.
Treat donation as a targeted intervention, not a continuous tax, and remember
that a donation made too early to save a lane that was already lost only wastes
the donor's own strength, so the call is about saving a lane that is still
winnable, not burying the donor to rescue a hopeless one.

The timing of the donation call also matters: you should make it at the moment the
losing lane is about to flip, not so early that the losing lane recovers on its
own and you have donated nothing, and not so late that the lane is already lost
and the donation only slows the collapse. A good heuristic is to watch the
relative troop and Gold gap between the two lanes and make the donation call when
the gap starts to close in the losing lane's favor against the enemy pressure,
which is the window where a single transfer actually changes the outcome. If the
gap is still wide in the losing lane's favor you are too early, and if the losing
lane has already lost a key unit you are too late; the in-between moment is where
a well-sized donation is the difference between a held lane and a lost one.

## The three ways a 2v2 game ends and how to force yours

You already know the elimination end: the game stops the instant exactly one
team remains, so a full wipe of the enemy pair is an immediate victory and a full
wipe of your own pair is an immediate defeat. The other two endings are the
percentage finish and the timer finish, and both only trigger when neither team
has been fully eliminated by the time the match runs out. The timer is 10
minutes on a compact map and 15 minutes on a standard map, and at the timer the
team with the higher percentage of land wins. The percentage finish can also come
from a map-wide land threshold being crossed before the timer, which ends the game
with the leading team winning. Knowing all three endings tells you how to manage
the middle of the game rather than just reacting to fights as they happen.

In the middle of a 2v2, once the first fight is decided and both teams are
alive, your job is to push toward whichever finish you are best positioned to
take. If you have the kill advantage and the enemy pair is repeatedly down to one
player, you are positioned for the elimination end and you should keep pressuring
the downed player's lane to complete the wipe. If you do not have a clean kill
advantage but you have the larger territory, you are positioned for the
percentage end and you should stop overcommitting to fights that could wipe you
out and instead consolidate land so you lead at the timer. The losing move is to
ignore which finish you are actually set up for: a team that has the territory
lead but keeps all-in fighting until it gets wiped converts a percentage win into
an elimination loss. Read the state of both lanes and the timer, decide which of
the three endings you are realistically fighting toward, and play the rest of the
match to force that specific ending. If you want more on endgame land management
that applies to the percentage finish, the endgame coverage in the [nation and
alliance decisions guide](/guides/nation-alliance-decisions/) covers the same
land-percentage reasoning in a longer form.

A common mistake in the middle game is treating the timer as a countdown to lose
rather than a threshold to reach. The timer only decides the match if neither team
is eliminated, so its value is that it sets a deadline by which you must be leading
on territory if you do not have the kill advantage. Reading the timer together
with the current territory lead tells you which ending is real: if you are ahead
on land with several minutes left, the percentage finish is the realistic target
and you should play to extend the lead; if you are behind on land but have the
kill advantage, the elimination finish is the realistic target and you should play
to convert the kill lead into a wipe. Confusing the two, leading on territory but
chasing a wipe you cannot complete, or leading on kills but giving up land you
cannot recover, is how teams lose 2v2 games they had the structural edge to win.

## Failure modes and the counterplay for each

The most common 2v2 failure is the overlapping-lane loss: both players expand
into the same pocket, one is left with a bad position, and the enemy pair
concentrates on the exposed player. The counterplay is the lane split you agreed
on before the window closes, and if it breaks down mid-game you should immediately
re-divide rather than continue fighting from overlapping positions. The second
common failure is the donation death spiral, where both players keep donating to
each other so neither lane is ever strong enough to win. The counterplay is the
standing rule that only the losing lane receives a donation and only enough to
flip that lane, so the donor's lane never collapses. The third failure is
ignoring the elimination end state: a team that is ahead on territory but keeps
all-in fighting until it is wiped out converts a percentage win into an
immediate loss. The counterplay is to recognize when you have a territory lead and
stop overcommitting, consolidating land instead of chasing kills you do not need.
Each of these is a specific, fixable behavior rather than a talent gap, which is
why reviewing your own 2v2 losses against this list is the fastest way to climb
the ladder.

The fourth failure is queueing with an unvetted random partner and losing the
cooperative half of the mode to bad coordination. The counterplay is the vetting
routine from the partner section: check the candidate's games played and win
rate and their team-wins contribution before you commit to the queue, and prefer
a known partner whenever one is available. The fifth failure is treating a
compact 2v2 like a standard 2v2. Compact maps are fast and close, with 100 bots
and a 10-minute timer, so the economy pays back sooner and the first fight
decides more of the game; a standard 2v2 with 400 bots and a 15-minute timer is
longer and rewards patient territorial play. The counterplay is to read the map
type the moment it loads and commit to the matching tempo: aggressive and fast on
compact, patient and territorial on standard. If you play the wrong tempo, you
will be either over-extended on a standard map or underbuilt on a compact one,
and both of those lose.

A sixth, subtler failure is reviewing 2v2 losses using 1v1 instincts: noticing
that you lost a fight but not asking whether the loss came from a lane split that
broke down, a donation that was undersized, or a tempo that did not match the map.
Because the elimination end is so fast in 2v2, a single bad call early can decide
the match before your usual 1v1 strengths have time to matter, so the review habit
has to be about the cooperative decisions, not just the mechanical ones. If you
catch yourself repeatedly losing for the same reason, write that reason down and
tie it to one of the five failure modes above; most 2v2 losses map cleanly to one
of them, and naming the specific mode is what turns a loss into a fixable habit.

## Mode and map adjustments, plus how to track your ladder

Ranked 2v2 has its own leaderboard tab separate from the 1v1 ladder, so your 2v2
record and rating are tracked independently of your 1v1 record. This means you
can climb 2v2 without touching your 1v1 rating, and a strong 2v2 partner can
raise both of your standings on the 2v2 board specifically. When you review
your 2v2 performance, look at the 2v2 leaderboard and the team-wins data, not
the 1v1 board, because that is the only data that reflects how you are actually
playing the mode. If your 2v2 win rate is lower than your 1v1 win rate, the gap
is almost always coordination rather than solo skill, and the fixes are the
lane-split and donation routines in this guide rather than general aim or build
knowledge.

The practical ladder routine is: pick a vetted partner, lock them before the
queue, agree on the lane roles and the first-attack target during the selection
window, donate only to the losing lane, read which of the three endings you are
set up for and force that ending, and after the match review your own lane and
donation decisions against the failure list above. Compact maps reward an
aggressive tempo and an early first-blood push, standard maps reward a patient
territorial tempo and a clean donation plan, and both reward the same two
cooperative behaviors: a clean lane split and a decisive, targeted donation.
Get those two right and the rest of the mode, the immunity window, the compact
map chance, the donation economy, and the elimination finish, all work in your
favor instead of against you.

The final adjustment is reading the tempo before you commit to a build. On the
compact maps, the 10-minute timer and 100 bots mean the game resolves faster and
the first fight carries more weight, so you should favor the aggressive lane split
and the early first-blood push. On the standard maps, the 15-minute timer and 400
bots mean the game is longer and the territory lead compounds more slowly, so you
should favor the patient split and the clean donation plan that holds lanes over
time. Switching the tempo to match the map is the last skill that ties the mode
together, and it is the one most new 2v2 players skip, which is why they lose
compact games by being too slow and standard games by being too aggressive.
