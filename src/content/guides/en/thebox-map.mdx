The Box is the waterless arcade map that runs at a 210-player lobby since the v0.35.0 rework: a 2048-by-2048 land board of 4,194,304 tiles, 13 unflagged arcade nations, and a 210-player FFA ceiling. It has been in the game since v0.30.0, the forced 75% water-nukes modifier since v0.34.0-beta1, and the 210-player lobby since v0.35.0 removed the old 125-player cap. Every lobby rolls water nukes at 75%, so the craters they leave are the only ocean — and the only way to build ports and boats. The match comes down to fighting for the first crater, choosing the 210 board over a compact one, and playing a land war that quietly becomes a naval one.

## Version boundary: when the map landed, and when the lobby changed

The version boundary on The Box is the first thing to pin because it separates the map's original geometry from the lobby-size change that made its current 210-player figure real. The Box is not a new v0.35 map: its manifest and 13 arcade nations have been in the game since v0.30.0, the first release to ship the map-generator asset set. At v0.30.0 the board was a pure land arena — 2048-by-2048, 4,194,304 land tiles, 13 unflagged arcade nations — with no forced modifier at all. The forced `isWaterNukes:75` modifier was added in v0.34.0-beta1, the first tag where every Box lobby rolls for water nukes. The v0.35.0 release did not introduce the map; it changed the lobby formula. The v0.35.0 release body states it plainly: "The 125-player cap is gone. Lobby size now comes from the map, so The Box fits 210." Before v0.35.0, the lobby size was capped at 125 players regardless of the map's land-tile count; after v0.35.0, the land-tile count drives the lobby size, and The Box's 4,194,304 land tiles round the formula to a 210 base. The same release doubled The Box's playlist frequency from 3 to 6, so the map appears twice as often in the multiplayer queue. By v0.35.9, the latest formal release, the geometry is unchanged: the same 2048-by-2048 canvas, the same 4,194,304 land tiles, the same 13 arcade nations, and the same forced `isWaterNukes:75` modifier. The map's in-game translation key is `map.thebox`, so the name you see in the lobby list resolves to exactly this manifest. A video that describes The Box from before v0.35.0 is describing the same board with a 125-player cap — the geometry is identical, only the lobby size and frequency changed.

Every number here is taken from the v0.35.9 tag source, not from the beta or from community recollection. The manifest at that tag reports 4,194,304 land tiles out of a 2048-by-2048 canvas, which is a 100 percent land fraction — the entire board is land and there is no coastline, no river, and no lake tile anywhere on it at the start of the match. The same manifest reports 13 nations, none of them carrying a real national flag, so the board is a set of arcade role names rather than a civic layer the way Rio's 30 Brazilian names are. The map ships the pre-baked resolutions alongside the full board, and the full 2048-by-2048 canvas is the one that matters because it is the one the lobby formula reads when it computes the player count. The map is tagged with the "arcade" category, which is the group the game sorts it into when you filter lobbies by map type.

The practical consequence of pinning to v0.35.9 is that any guide or video that quotes a Box lobby from the v0.35 betas may be off by a formula change, and the only safe statement is the one that comes from the final tag. This guide does not carry over any Box-specific number from an earlier build, and the version boundary is the reason the lobby table below uses the v0.35.9 formula exactly as it is written in the tag source. If a future v0.35.x patch moves the land count or the formula, the numbers in this guide will need to be re-derived, and the version line in this frontmatter is the marker that tells you which build they were derived from. Until then, the v0.35.9 tag is the reference, and every claim in the body is traceable to the manifest, the lobby formula, or the release body at that tag. That is the same version-pinning discipline the other v0.35 map guides use, and it is the reason the next section can state the lobby size as a hard number rather than a range.

## The waterless geometry: a board with no coastline at all

The single most important physical fact on The Box is that it has no water, and that changes the whole game compared to every other map. The manifest reports 4,194,304 land tiles on a 2048-by-2048 canvas, and 2048 times 2048 is exactly 4,194,304, so the land fraction is 100 percent and there is no water tile anywhere on the board. Every other map has a coastline, a river, an island, or a lake, and that water is where ports sit, where boats launch, and where a navy is built. The Box has none of it at the start, so the opening of the match is a pure land war: every player expands over land, builds factories and land units, and fights for tiles the way the land-combat and annexation guides describe, but there is no water to cross, no island to take, and no naval route to contest.

The consequence is that the early game on The Box has no ports and no boats by default, because a port has to sit on water and there is no water to put it on. That is the load-bearing fact of the map, and it is the reason the map feels completely different from a normal arcade board. You do not get a navy until the board changes, and the board only changes in one way: the forced water-nukes modifier. Every Box lobby rolls a 75 percent chance that water nukes are active, and when a water nuke lands it converts the destroyed land into water, leaving a crater that is a real body of water on the map. That crater is the first ocean the lobby has ever seen, and it is the first place a port can exist. So the water that every normal map gives you for free is, on The Box, the thing the whole match is fighting over, and the timing and location of the first crater is the single most important event of the game. The water-nukes guide covers how the conversion and reclamation work in general, and the port-versus-factory guide covers what a port is worth once you have the water to put it on.

The second geometric fact is that the board is a plain square with no internal features to break it up. There are no rivers to drain, no bay to pivot around, no strait to choke, and no island chain to control. The map is an open flat of land from corner to corner, and the only asymmetry that exists at the start is the nation spawn positions, which scatter the 13 arcade names across the board. That openness is a double-edged fact: it means there are no natural choke points to defend, so early fights are open-field and there is no terrain to hide behind, but it also means that the first crater the water nukes carve out immediately becomes the focal point of the map, because it is the only feature on the board. Before the crater there is nothing to contest except land tiles, and after the crater there is a body of water that every player is now reaching for. The map-strategy guide covers the general logic of how open boards play, and the choose-your-spawn guide covers how to read the spawn scatter before the countdown ends. The point of this section is that The Box starts as a pure land arena with no coastline, and the entire naval half of the game is generated by the water nukes rather than handed to you at spawn.

## The lobby size the land formula hands you

The single most important number on this map is the lobby size, because it decides how many players you actually face and how dense the board is. The live formula in the v0.35.9 tag source, in the file that handles the multiplayer playlist, derives a base player count from the land-tile count with the expression that takes the land tiles divided by one million times fifty, rounds to the nearest five, and floors at five. For The Box's 4,194,304 land tiles, that expression evaluates to a base of 210, because 4.194304 million times 50 is 209.7 and rounding to the nearest five gives 210. That base of 210 is the large-FFA number, and it is the ceiling for every other figure on this map, which is the first thing to understand before you look at the rest of the table.

From that base, the game builds three rolled tiers: the large tier is the base itself at 210, the medium tier is the base times 0.75 rounded to the nearest five, which is 160, and the small tier is the base times 0.5 rounded to the nearest five, which is 105. The roll is 30 percent toward large, 30 percent toward medium, and 40 percent toward small, so a 105-player board is the most common lobby you will actually land in. Free-for-all mode uses the rolled tier directly, which gives you the 210, 160, and 105 figures. Two-team mode is where the cap matters: the formula multiplies the base by 1.5 and rounds up, but then clips that result at the large-FFA tier of 210. So a 210 base gives a team figure of min(ceil(315), 210), which is 210, and the 105 small tier gives min(ceil(157.5), 210), which is 158 after the two-player-even rounding. That is the counterintuitive result: on this map, two-team mode does not produce a bigger board than free-for-all, it produces a board capped at the same 210. Compact mode then shrinks each figure to a quarter, floored at a minimum of three players, which gives 52, 40, and 26 in FFA and 52, 52, and 39 in teams.

| The Box | Large (30%) | Medium (30%) | Small (40%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| FFA | 210 | 160 | 105 |
| 2-Team | 210 | 210 | 158 |
| Compact FFA | 52 | 40 | 26 |
| Compact 2-Team | 52 | 52 | 39 |

> **Note on the 2-Team rows:** The Box's manifest defines no fixed team spawn zones (`teamGameSpawnAreas` is an optional field and is absent on this map), so two-team mode here falls back to random spawns with no pre-placed team territories. The 210 / 158 figures are the player-count ceilings, not a statement that the map carves out a team home area — each side still races to the same centre crater from a random start.

The reason the cap clips the 1.5× multiplier is that the multiplier is applied to the base and then compared against the large-FFA tier, and on a map whose base is already 210, the multiplier's result (315) is always above the 210 ceiling. On a smaller map the multiplier would not be clipped, but on The Box it is, which means the team-mode board is bounded by the same 210 that bounds the FFA board. This is the single most-misquoted number on the map, and it is the reason a guide that tells you "team mode gives you 420 players on The Box" is wrong for this map. The full derivation, with the formula written out and the rounding rules explained, lives in the [lobby-size guide](/guides/lobby-size-map-driven/), which is the reference for how the land-tile count becomes a player count across every map in the game. The table above is the Box-specific instance of that derivation, pinned to the v0.35.9 tag, and it is the number set that every scenario in this guide is built on. If you want to understand why a 210-player board is the right size for a 13-nation map, the [map-strategy guide](/guides/map-strategy/) covers the general density logic, and the compact-mode guide covers how the quarter-shrink works and when you should deliberately queue a compact board instead of a full one.

## The nation layer: 13 arcade roles with no flags

The nation layer on The Box is the reason the map feels different from a generic flag-pool map: every one of the 13 spawn names is a self-described arcade role, and none of them carries a real national flag, so the board presents a cast of characters rather than a map of countries. There is no civic layer the way Rio's 30 Brazilian names are, and there is no geographic meaning to the names. The 13 names are King of the Corner, Suspicious Ally, Evan The Dev, Middle Defender, Punch Merchant, Nuke Thrower, Fullsender, Factory Builder, Front Manager, Box Fighter, Cage Liberator, Train Trader, and Non-peaceful Bot, and each one is a hint at the kind of play the role invites rather than a place on a map. The consequence of a 13-name, unflagged board is that the nation layer is a character roster rather than a military one, and the strategic question is not "which country is stronger" but "which of these 13 roles do I take and which do I leave for someone else."

The 13 names scatter across the 2048-by-2048 board at spawn, and the scatter is what makes the opening feel open and even, because there is no dense half and no thin half the way Rio's 16-versus-11 west/east split creates. On a waterless board the spawn scatter is the only asymmetry at the start, so where you land relative to your neighbours matters more than it does on a map with a fixed geography. King of the Corner and Box Fighter are the names that read as aggressive, Suspicious Ally and Fullsender read as players who will betray, Middle Defender and Factory Builder read as players who will turtle, and Nuke Thrower is the name that reads as the player most likely to be the one who drops the first water nuke and creates the first crater. The names are flavour, not mechanics, but in a 13-nation board where every player is visible to every other player, the flavour is the first read you get on how the table is going to play, and it is worth a second before you commit to a direction.

The density of the nation layer relative to the player count is the second key number, and it is low. With 13 names and a player range of 105 to 210, the average FFA board is roughly 8 to 16 players per name, which means the nation name is far from a meaningful grouping on a full board: a 210-player lobby has more than ten players sharing each role name on average. That is the opposite of Rio, where the 30 names are nearly as numerous as the players, and it is the reason The Box feels like a scrum rather than a nation-based campaign. The compact board changes the ratio to roughly two to four players per name, which is still low but is the mode where the 13-name layout starts to read as a character roster rather than a crowd label. The population-growth guide covers how the nation layer fills up over the course of a match and what an unclaimed name is worth in the midgame, and the [lobby-size guide](/guides/lobby-size-map-driven/) covers how the player count is derived from the board. The point of this section is that the nation layer on The Box is a real, countable, 13-name arcade cast with no flags and no geography, and the scatter of those 13 names across an open 2048-by-2048 board is the only asymmetry you have to read before the countdown ends.

## The forced water nukes: the only water source on the map

The forced modifier on The Box is `isWaterNukes:75`, and it is the single mechanic that separates this map from every other waterless or coastal board. In the v0.35.9 tag source, a forced modifier entry is parsed by splitting the key from a percentage, and that percentage is the per-lobby roll chance. So `isWaterNukes:75` means that every Box lobby makes a roll, and 75 percent of the time the roll succeeds and water nukes are active for the whole match, while 25 percent of the time the roll fails and water nukes never happen. That roll is the defining uncertainty of the map, because it decides whether the game becomes a land-war-plus-naval game or a pure land war that never gets a single drop of water. When the roll succeeds, the first water nuke that lands converts its destroyed land into water and leaves a crater, and that crater is the first and, early in the game, only body of water on the entire 2048-by-2048 board.

The mechanics of the conversion are the same water-nukes rules that the [water-nukes guide](/guides/water-nukes/) documents, and they matter here because the water they create is the only water available. A water nuke converts unowned land to water after a reclaim check: if a tile is re-conquered before the conversion flush it can stay land, and the crater edge is smoothed rather than ragged, so the new shoreline is an irregular body of water rather than a pixel-scatter. On a normal map that converted water is one more patch of a coastline you already had, but on The Box it is the coastline, because there was no coastline before it. A port has to sit on water to launch boats and trade ships, so the first crater is the first possible port, and the port is the first possible navy. Every boat, every trade ship, and every naval unit on the map traces back to a water nuke that carved the water it sails on, which means the player who controls the crater controls the navy of the match.

The 75 percent roll also has a hard strategic consequence: in the 25 percent of lobbies where water nukes never activate, The Box stays 100 percent land for the entire match, and the navy that the rest of this guide describes never exists. That is a real branch, not a footnote. In a no-nukes lobby the map plays as a pure land annexation game from spawn to end, and every naval counter, every port decision, and every trade-route plan is void, so a player who pre-commits to a navy plan on The Box is gambling on the 75 percent roll and should treat the first minutes as the window to confirm whether the roll landed. The [map-strategy guide](/guides/map-strategy/) covers how to read a board before you commit, and the [list-lobbies guide](/guides/list-lobbies/) covers how to see the modifier badge on a lobby before you join, which on The Box is the one pre-join signal that tells you whether the navy half of the game is on the table at all. The point of this section is that the forced water nukes are not just another modifier on The Box; they are the water source, and the whole map is built around the question of when the first crater drops and who reaches it first.

## Decision framework: pick your board and your crater before the first nuke

The decision that decides a Box match is the one you make before the first water nuke lands: which board you are on, which direction you are expanding, and which crater you are reaching for. The framework has three layers, and you work them in order. The first layer is the board, and the choice is between the full 210-player FFA, the capped 210 two-team board, and the compact 52-player quarter board. Free-for-all on The Box is a 105-to-210-player scrum over an open land board, which is a density game where the first crater is a free-for-all scramble. Two-team on The Box is a capped 210-player board with the same land, which is a side-selection game where your team and the other team race to the crater. If you want to play the scramble and pick your own direction, queue FFA. If you want a two-team race where the navy is a team asset, queue two-team. If you want a readable 13-name character game, queue compact. The [map-strategy guide](/guides/map-strategy/) and the [lobby-size guide](/guides/lobby-size-map-driven/) cover the general logic of when density versus side-selection is the better game for your playstyle.

The second layer is direction, and it applies before the crater exists. Because the board is open and waterless at spawn, your early expansion is a pure land push, and the direction you pick is the direction you will have to fight to defend once the crater appears. The three sensible directions are toward the centre, where the crater is most likely to form and where the fight is sharpest, toward a corner, where the King-of-the-Corner name reads as the archetype and the land is the most defensible, and along a side, where you keep one edge open as a retreat route. The choose-your-spawn guide covers the spawn-choice decision tree, and the land-combat guide covers how to hold a land border once you have pushed. The third layer is the crater, and it is the layer that only exists once the roll has landed and a nuke has fired. At that point every player has a target: the body of water that just appeared, and the question is whether you race to the crater to build the first port and claim the first navy, or you hold your land and contest the approach to the crater so the enemy cannot reach it. The [water-nukes guide](/guides/water-nukes/) and the [port-versus-factory guide](/guides/port-vs-factory/) are the two references that decide the crater layer.

The framework resolves to a simple rule: pick the board that matches your preferred game (scramble versus team-race versus character game), pick the direction that matches your preferred geometry (centre fight versus corner hold versus side retreat), and once the crater appears, pick whether you race for the port or contest the approach. The first nuke is the hard deadline, and the crater it leaves is the asset that everyone else is now contesting, so the framework is a pre-nuke decision, not a mid-match one. The scenarios below apply this framework to two specific boards, and they show how the same three layers resolve differently on a full 210-player FFA and on a compact 52-player board. The map-strategy guide is the general reference for the framework, and the compact-mode guide covers when a compact board is the better choice for the same map.

## Scenario one: a full 210-player FFA on the open centre

The first scenario is a full 210-player free-for-all, which is the densest board the map produces and the one that most clearly shows what a waterless 2048-by-2048 board does at maximum density. You spawn into one of the 13 scatter positions on the open land, the board is at the large-FFA tier of 210 players, and the player-to-name ratio is 210 divided by 13, which is roughly 16 players per name on average. The practical consequence is that the nation names are effectively crowd labels on this board: you are not playing a 13-player nation campaign, you are one of 210 players on an open land arena, and the name you rolled is flavour rather than a grouping. Your first decision is the direction of your land push, because there is no water and no coastline to give you a default direction, and the centre of the board is the place where the first crater is most likely to form.

If you push toward the centre, you are buying the fight: you will be near the first crater, you will be in the sharpest scramble of the match, and you will be the player most likely to either build the first port or get crushed trying to reach it. The cost is that at 210 players the centre is crowded enough that your land push meets two or three other players within the first few minutes, and the early game is a three-way land fight before any water exists. If you hold a corner instead, you are buying defence: the corner is the most defensible geometry on an open board because you have two edges behind you, and the King-of-the-Corner name reads as the archetype of that play. The cost is that you are far from the centre, and reaching the first crater means crossing contested land while the centre players are already on it. The early-expansion-rhythm and land-combat guides cover the timing and the border-hold for both branches.

The midgame of this scenario is the first crater. By the ten-minute mark, the centre is consolidated by a few large land blocs, the corners are held by turtle players, and the first water nuke has dropped somewhere on the board, leaving an irregular body of water that everyone is now reaching for. The crater is the pivot: whoever builds the first port on it controls the first navy, and the rest of the 210 players are either racing to that water or contesting the land between themselves and it. At 210 players the board is dense enough that the last few unclaimed land regions are worth more than the early ones, and the player who holds the crater at the twenty-minute mark is the player who controls the endgame, because the navy built on that water can reach every corner of the open board. This scenario is the density game, and it is the one you play when you want the scramble and the first-nuke fight rather than a readable character game.

## Scenario two: the compact 52-player board

The second scenario is a compact board at the 52-player large tier, which is the most readable board the map produces and the one that most clearly shows what the 13-name layer does when the player count drops to a quarter. The compact tier is a quarter of the full figure, floored at three players, so the compact FFA board is 52, 40, and 26 across the three rolled tiers, and the compact two-team board is 52, 52, and 39. In the 52-player compact FFA, the player-to-name ratio is 52 divided by 13, which is four players per name on average, and that is the mode where the 13-name arcade roster starts to read as a cast of characters rather than a crowd. You spawn into one of the 13 roles, and the first decision is not "which of 210 players am I competing with" but "which of the other three players sharing my name do I want to avoid, and which crater am I reaching for."

In the compact board the open land is the same 2048-by-2048 geometry, but the density is low enough that your land push is a two- or three-player fight rather than a 210-player scrum, and the directions resolve differently. Pushing toward the centre is still the sharpest play, but at 52 players the centre fight is a handful of players rather than a dozen, so the first crater becomes a small group scramble where the player who reaches it first and builds the port has a real, durable lead. Holding a corner is still the defensible play, and at 52 players the corner hold is more viable because the turtle has only a handful of neighbours to worry about rather than a dozen. The compact-mode guide covers the quarter-shrink in detail and when a compact board is the better choice for the same map, and the [map-size-compact-mode guide](/guides/map-size-compact-mode/) covers the general logic of how the resolution changes the board.

The midgame of this scenario is the same first crater, but it plays as a character game rather than a scrum. By the ten-minute mark, a few of the 13 roles have consolidated into small land blocs, the first water nuke has dropped, and the crater is the pivot that the handful of remaining players are reaching for. Because there are only 52 players, the last few unclaimed land regions are contested by a small group, and the player who builds the first port on the crater has a navy that faces a handful of opponents rather than a hundred. The endgame of this scenario is the character game itself: at 52 players, the board is open enough that the 13 roles read as distinct players with distinct plans, and the player who holds the crater at the twenty-minute mark is the player who controls the endgame, because the navy built on that water is the only navy and it faces a small, readable field. This scenario is the character game at quarter density, and it is the one you play when you want a readable Box rather than a 210-player scrum.

## Failure modes and opponent counterplay

The failure modes on The Box are specific, and they cluster around three traps that the waterless geometry and the 75 percent roll create. The first trap is the navy-gambling trap: you pre-commit to a navy plan before you have confirmed that the water-nukes roll landed, and the roll fails, so the map stays 100 percent land and the navy you were planning never exists. The counter is to treat the first minutes as a confirmation window: watch for the modifier badge before you join via the [list-lobbies guide](/guides/list-lobbies/), and once in the game confirm that a water nuke is actually available before you build toward the water, because a port built with no water under it is a port that never launches. The early-expansion-rhythm guide covers the timing of confirming a plan before you commit.

The second trap is the centre-crowding trap in a full 210-player FFA: you push toward the centre to reach the first crater, but the density is high enough that you meet a dozen players in the first few minutes and lose the land fight before any water exists, leaving you with neither the crater nor a defensible border. The counter is to hold a corner or a side instead and let the centre players fight over the crater, then contest the land between the crater and your hold once the navy is built, because the navy that is built on the crater has to reach you over land and your defensible geometry is the asset that makes the approach costly. The [stack-vs-spread guide](/guides/stack-vs-spread/) covers the general logic of when a dense board favours spreading out over stacking up.

The third trap is the port-timing trap: the first crater drops, you race to it, and by the time you arrive a faster player has already built the first port and claimed the first navy, so you are now chasing a navy that is already built and protected. The counter is to contest the approach rather than race the last player: build toward the crater early so you are one of the first to reach the water, and if you are clearly not going to be first, redirect to the land between the crater and the next region so you control the approach instead of the port itself. The [port-versus-factory guide](/guides/port-vs-factory/) covers when a port is worth the investment and when a factory is the better use of the same gold, and the water-nukes guide covers the reclamation check that can keep a tile as land if you re-conquer it before the conversion flush.

The opponent counterplay on The Box is the crater. Whatever your failure mode, the opponent's counter is the body of water the nuke left, because the crater is the port, the port is the navy, and the navy is the endgame. If you lose the centre, the opponent takes the crater and controls the navy. If you lose the port race, the opponent's navy reaches you over the open land and your border is the only thing between you and being swept. If you lose the no-nukes roll, the opponent who confirmed early is playing the land war with a plan and you are playing it blind. The [water-nukes guide](/guides/water-nukes/), the [port-versus-factory guide](/guides/port-vs-factory/), and the land-combat guide are the three references that cover the crater counter in detail. The point of this section is that the three failure modes are specific, the counters are specific, and the opponent's counter is always the crater. If you understand the three traps and the crater pivot, you understand the map.

## Mode and map adjustments

The mode and map adjustments on The Box are the specific changes that make each mode work, and they are the final layer of the framework. In free-for-all mode, the adjustment is the density management: at 210 players, you manage the open land board by holding a defensible geometry and contesting the approach to the crater rather than racing the scrum, and at 105 players, you manage the same board by pushing toward the centre where the density is low enough that the first-nuke fight is winnable. The density management is the FFA adjustment, and it is the reason the FFA board is a scramble game rather than a side-selection game. In two-team mode, the adjustment is the side selection: at the capped 210 board, your team and the other team race to the crater as team assets, and the navy becomes a shared asset that the team coordinates, so the side selection is the team adjustment and it is the reason the team board is a race game rather than a scramble game. The team-spawn-formation guide covers the side-selection logic, and the team-economy-space guide covers the economy management that the team race requires.
