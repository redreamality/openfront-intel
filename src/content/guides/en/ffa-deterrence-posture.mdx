## Direct answer: price yourself as a target, then buy the threat

The FFA midgame question is not "how much stronger can I get?" It is "how expensive do I make it to attack me?" Your neighbours choose a target by comparing four inputs they can see or estimate: your banked Gold, the ready tubes of any Missile Silo you own, the visible troop strength on your border, and how exposed your expansion is. When you are ahead, those four inputs usually read as *an easy, profitable hit*, which is exactly why the biggest player keeps getting bombed. The fix is a deliberate **deterrence posture**: stop spending banked Gold on visible expansion, keep at least one Silo tube ready, and hold enough Gold to refill it at the current MIRV price. The posture is a live commitment, not a purchase, because the threat only exists while a tube is actually ready and you can afford to reload it.

In one line: **hold the Gold, keep one tube ready, and stop the expansion that makes you readable**, then re-price the ladder every few minutes until the bank can no longer refill a tube. That is the entire decision in four verbs — hold, keep, stop, re-price — and each one is cheaper than the alternative, which is to keep expanding and hand your neighbours the strike they were already planning. The posture is a *subtraction* of visible activity, not an addition of buildings, which is why it works for a behind player with a thin economy and for an ahead player with a full one at the same time: both of them are stopping something they would otherwise spend, and the Gold they do not spend is the Gold that makes the threat real. The cost is the income you give up, and the return is the change in the neighbours' calculation from "strike this one first" to "striking this one is a gamble."

This guide prices that trade against the v34 ladders — the shared MIRV counter, the fixed Silo build, and the SAM ring — so you can run the decision as arithmetic instead of instinct. Read the [MIRV guide](/guides/mirv/) for the attack and defense timing this posture relies on, the [Threat Assessment guide](/guides/threat-assessment/) for whole-board target selection from the attacker's side, and the [Attack Ratio guide](/guides/attack-ratio/) for the troop-side of the same call, because the posture is the *perception* side of the decision and the ratio is the *combat* side, and the two only agree when you keep them in sync. The sections that follow turn the four inputs into a target score you can read, price the layers against each other with the v34 numbers, run the decision as a four-step loop, work two concrete scenarios — behind and ahead — list the failure modes that turn a sound posture into a losing one, and draw the version boundary that keeps the posture honest about which numbers it is allowed to use.

## What deterrence posture means in FFA

A deterrence posture is a way of running your economy so that the cost of attacking you, as your neighbours calculate it, is higher than the cost of attacking someone else. It is not a build order and it is not a troop plan; it is a *perception* you create on the map and then maintain. In Free-For-All every neighbour is a potential attacker with a partial view of you, and each of them is quietly asking the same question: "if I strike this player right now, do I win the trade and survive the retaliation?" The posture changes the answer from yes to maybe, and a maybe is enough. The player who is the most *readable* target — the one with the clearest border, the largest visible stack, and no visible nuclear threat — is the player who gets hit first and repeatedly, even when that player is the strongest on the map.

This is a decision about *who you make yourself be to the rest of the map*, and it competes directly with the instinct to keep expanding. Every Gold you hold back for a bank is a City, Port, or Factory you are not building. Every ready Silo tube is a standing cost, because keeping it ready means you are spending Gold on reloads instead of on income. The posture therefore only works if you can sustain it: the bank has to refill from real income, and the threat has to stay live. A player who spends the last of their Gold on a final expansion and is left with a Silo that cannot reload has not built a posture, they have built a *decoration* — an expensive building that the map reads as "this one is about to run out." The difference between a posture and a decoration is sustainability, and the rest of this guide is about making that sustainability a calculable choice instead of a mood you keep.

The reason this matters more in FFA than in a one-versus-one nuclear duel is that the audience is multiple. In a two-player match there is one neighbour deciding, and you can tailor the posture to that one read. In FFA there are several neighbours, several partial views, and several different calculations running at the same time, so a posture that reads as expensive to one neighbour can still read as free to another if you have over-exposed yourself on that neighbour's border. The goal is to be *unattractive to the group*, not just to the one you are most worried about, and that is why the posture is measured against every visible neighbour at once rather than against a single rival. The next sections turn that principle into a target score you can actually read, a cost table you can price with, a decision sequence you can run every match, and the failure modes that turn a sound posture into a losing one.

## The four inputs of a target score

Before you change anything, you need to read yourself the way your neighbours read you. The community phrases this as "how do the other players see me right now?" and the useful move is to build a four-part **target score** for yourself and then check it against each visible neighbour. The four inputs are not equally hard to read, and they are not equally cheap to change, so the first skill is knowing which lever moves the score the most for the least Gold.

**Banked Gold** is the input your neighbours can see directly on the leaderboard and the one that most changes their calculation. A large banked Gold total is not a sign of weakness; in FFA it is a sign of a player who can commit suddenly, which is precisely what a cautious attacker does not want. A small bank with a large visible economy reads as a player who has already spent their options and is vulnerable to a timed strike. The banked number is the single most important signal, which is why the posture starts by *raising* the number you show rather than by hiding it. You do not need to misreport anything; you just need to stop spending it on visible expansion while you are in the posture.

**Ready Silo tubes** is the input that converts a bank into a *nuclear* read. Owning a Missile Silo costs a fixed 1,000,000 Gold and takes 100 ticks to construct, but the deterrent value is not the building, it is the *ready tube*. A Silo with a ready tube means an attacker must price in a live nuclear retaliation; a Silo that is reloading means the window is open. Upgrades reduce reload time, which is why an upgraded Silo is a more durable deterrent than a base one. The key discipline is to keep at least one tube ready at all times while in the posture, because the threat that a neighbour can see on the map is the ready state, not the potential. If your Silo is always mid-reload, the map reads it the same as if you did not own one.

**Visible troop strength** is the input that makes or breaks the posture from the military side. A player with a ready Silo and a bank, but a huge visible stack on the border, still reads as *the* target, because the attacker sees a cheap military entry point even if the nuclear read is there. The posture wants your border to look *defensible but not inviting*: enough troops to make a probe costly, not so many that you look like the prize. This is the over-push failure mode in reverse — the community warns that over-pushing makes neighbours out-scale you, and the same logic applies to your visible strength: a maximal visible stack attracts the strike that the posture was supposed to prevent. You want the neighbours to think "hitting this one is a gamble," not "hitting this one is free."

**Territory exposure** is the input about *where* you are, not *how strong* you are. A compact core with a clean border is harder to strike than an over-extended line with a soft flank, even at equal troop strength. Expansion that pushes into a neighbour's likely attack line is exposure; expansion that keeps you inside a defensible ring is not. The posture therefore pairs the bank-and-Silo read with a *shape* change: you stop the over-extended expansion that created the exposure, and you keep the compact core that your Silo and bank can actually defend. The four inputs together are the score, and the next section prices what it costs to move each of them in the direction you want.

## Costing the posture with the v34 ladders

The posture is a trade between two uses of the same Gold pool, and the v34 ladders let you price that trade instead of guessing it. On one side is **visible expansion**, the economic buildings that grow your income and your troop cap. A City, a Port, and a Factory all start at 125,000 Gold and climb on a shared ladder of `min(1,000,000, 2^n × 125,000)`, where `n` is the number of existing ports and factories. That means your first few economic buildings are cheap and your later ones get expensive fast, which is why the early game is the natural time to build the economy and the midgame is the natural time to *stop* and bank. On the other side is **the nuclear threat**, which is the Missile Silo plus the weapons it launches.

The Silo itself is a flat 1,000,000 Gold with a 100-tick construction time, and upgrades reduce reload time, so an upgraded Silo is the durable version of the threat. The weapons it launches are where the real deterrence price lives, and they are the numbers a neighbour is actually calculating against. The MIRV price ladder is shared across the whole match: the next carrier costs 25,000,000 Gold plus 15,000,000 Gold for every MIRV already launched in the match, and every launch by *any* player advances the counter for everyone. At zero prior launches the first three carriers cost 25M, 40M, and 55M, or 120M together. That shared counter is the heart of the posture, because it means a banked reserve that can afford two or three carriers is not just your own threat, it is a *shared deterrent* that raises the price of the ladder for every other player too.

The defensive side of the read is the SAM launcher, which your neighbours also price in when they decide whether a strike will land. A SAM launcher starts at 1,500,000 Gold and climbs on `min(3,000,000, (n+1) × 1,500,000)`, with a 90-tick cooldown per slot and range scaling from 70 to 150 tiles by level. A SAM ring does not guarantee interception, but it changes the attacker's calculation from "the nuke lands" to "the nuke might not land, and I have to spend time building the strike anyway." The posture is therefore a *stack* of three priced layers: the banked Gold that signals you can commit, the ready Silo tube that makes the retaliation real, and the SAM coverage that makes the incoming strike uncertain. The table below prices those layers against the expansion they compete with, so you can see exactly what giving up a rung of economic growth buys you in deterrence.

|---|---|---|---|
| Banked Gold (held, not spent) | 0 extra; opportunity cost of 1 rung of the 125k × 2^n ladder | "This one can commit suddenly" — a cautious attacker avoids the sudden commit | Income must refill the bank; stop spending on visible expansion |

The way to use the table is to pick the layer that moves your target score the most for the least Gold and the least lost income. For a player who is behind, that is usually the banked Gold plus one ready Silo tube, because it is the cheapest way to go from "the easy hit" to "the gamble." For a player who is ahead, that is usually the MIRV bank plus the SAM ring, because the goal is to make the leader's position *defensive*, not to keep expanding a position that already invites the strike. The cost is always the income you gave up, and the next section is the income check that decides whether you can actually afford the layer you picked.

## Decision framework: read, price, commit, stop

The posture is not a one-time switch, it is a loop you run every few minutes of game time, and the loop has four steps. The first step is **read**, and it is the target score from the previous section: for each visible neighbour, what do your four inputs look like to them, and which neighbour would you attack if you were them? You are not trying to outguess one specific rival; you are trying to be the player nobody picks first, which means checking the score against *all* visible neighbours at once and fixing the worst read. The neighbour who sees the cheapest entry point into you is the one you are currently making yourself look like, and that is the one to change.

The second step is **price**, using the ladders from the previous section. For the layer that would move your worst read, what does it cost, and what income are you giving up to hold it? A ready Silo tube is a standing reload cost at the current MIRV price, and a bank you are not spending is a City or Port you are not building. The price step is where the posture can quietly fail, because a layer that looks strong on the map can be bleeding Gold you do not have. If the layer you picked costs more income than your economy produces, you are not in a posture, you are in a *leak*, and the right move is to pick a cheaper layer or to abandon the posture and convert back to expansion before the leak empties your bank.

The third step is **commit**, and it is the actual change you make: stop the specific expansion that created the worst exposure, hold the Gold, and bring a Silo tube to ready. The commit is a *subtraction*, not an addition, which is the part most players miss. You are not building a second Silo or a third SAM ring; you are *removing* the visible over-extension and *holding* the Gold that was going to buy it. The subtraction is what moves the read from "easy hit" to "gamble," because the neighbour's calculation is about what they can take, and a player who is not pushing out has less to take and a longer, more exposed line of approach. The commit is also where the troop discipline lands: you keep enough troops to make a probe costly without making a maximal visible stack, which is the border that looks defensible but not inviting.

The fourth step is **stop**, and it is the exit condition that separates a posture from a losing one. You hold the posture while the map still reads you as the expensive target, and you stop it when the bank can no longer refill a ready tube at the current ladder price, or when a neighbour has committed so hard to another target that the read on you has reset. The stop is not "the game is over," it is "the posture has served its purpose and the next phase is either the expansion you deferred or the nuclear commitment you were saving for." A player who stops the posture at the right moment converts the bank into the endgame economy or the endgame weapons; a player who holds the posture too long, past the point where the bank refills, sits on Gold that is no longer deterring anyone and loses the income race to the neighbours who kept expanding. The four steps are a loop, not a list, and the next section runs the first two of them in a concrete behind-the-curve scenario.

## Scenario: behind and out-eco'd, using the posture to freeze the leader

The first scenario is the player who is *behind*. You are in a FFA match, and you are the smaller of the three players still standing: the two bigger neighbours have been expanding steadily, your bank is thin, your troop stack is smaller than theirs, and your border is the soft flank that both of them can see. This is the exact position the community describes as "you are being out-eco'd," and the instinct is to panic-expand — build the next City, push the border, try to catch up by growing fast. That instinct is the one that keeps the behind player losing, because a behind player who over-expands is a behind player who makes themselves the *most* readable target, and the bigger neighbours then coordinate the strike that finishes the gap.

The posture moves this in the opposite direction. You stop the panic expansion, and you make two changes you can afford with a thin economy. First, you hold whatever Gold you have instead of spending it on a visible City, and you let the bank be the *highest* number on the board relative to your troop strength, because that is the read that says "this small one can still commit." A bigger player with a smaller bank relative to their size is the more attractive target, and you do not have to be the biggest to stop being the most attractive. Second, you bring one Silo tube to ready. You do not need a big nuclear arsenal to make the posture work; you need the *threat* to be real, and a single ready tube with a bank behind it is enough to change the neighbour's calculation from "strike the small one first" to "striking the small one is a gamble that might raise the ladder and cost me a tube."

The numbers make the choice concrete. Assume your bank is 30M and you have one ready Silo tube. A neighbour considering a strike on you is not just calculating the troops they lose; they are calculating that if they strike and you retaliate, the MIRV ladder moves, and the next carrier for *everyone* gets more expensive. At zero prior launches that next carrier is 25M; at one prior launch it is 40M; at two it is 55M. Your one ready tube is the launch that moves the counter, and a neighbour with a 20M bank suddenly cannot afford the retaliation you are threatening. The posture does not require you to be able to launch three carriers; it requires you to make the *first* retaliation credible, which a single ready tube plus a 30M bank does. The assumption in this scenario is that the two bigger neighbours are the ones with the larger banks and the larger stacks, and that your income is roughly keeping pace with theirs even though your total is smaller, which is the condition under which the posture is sustainable rather than a leak.

The failure mode to watch in this scenario is the *refill check*. If your income is not actually keeping pace — if the bigger neighbours' economy is genuinely producing more Gold than yours — then holding the bank is not a posture, it is a slow bleed, and the right move is to convert back to expansion or to commit the bank to a single nuclear strike before it empties. The posture works for the behind player because it freezes the leader's advantage long enough for your income to catch up, but it only works if the freeze is *sustainable*. The exit condition here is specific: you hold the posture until your bank has refilled to the point where you can afford to launch the carrier you have been threatening, and then you either launch it or convert the bank into the endgame economy. The next scenario is the mirror image: the player who is ahead and has to keep the posture from turning their own lead into a target.

## Scenario: ahead and the crown target, using the ladder to deny the strike

The second scenario is the player who is *ahead*. You are the biggest player on the map, you own the most territory, your troop stack is the largest, and your bank is the thinnest, because you have been spending it on the expansion that got you here. This is the crown-target position, and the community is blunt about it: "having the crown is like having a target on your back." The instinct for the ahead player is to keep expanding and to let the stack do the talking, and that is the instinct that gets the ahead player hydrogen-bombed "to oblivion" even when they have the bigger stack, because the bigger stack is exactly what the neighbours are pricing in as the reward for the strike.

The posture for the ahead player is different from the posture for the behind player, and the difference is the *goal*. The behind player is trying to stop being the easy hit; the ahead player is trying to make the *leader's position defensive* rather than the leader's position attractive. You stop the expansion that is making you the most readable target, and you convert the bank you would have spent on another City into a **MIRV bank** that can afford two or three carriers. The MIRV ladder is the tool here, because a bank that can afford multiple carriers is a *shared* deterrent: every carrier you launch raises the price for every other player, which means the neighbour who was planning to strike you now has to price in the ladder *you* are moving, not just the ladder the rest of the map is moving. The community's "throw a bunch of MIRVs to drive up the cost" is exactly this, and it is the ahead player's version of the posture.

The numbers make the choice concrete. Assume your bank is 120M and the ladder is at zero prior launches. You can afford the first three carriers — 25M, 40M, and 55M — which together cost 120M, the entire bank. Launching all three is not the plan; the plan is to *hold* the bank at a level where the neighbour knows you can launch two or three, and to launch one when a neighbour commits hard enough to make the retaliation worth it. A neighbour with a 30M bank who is planning to strike you is pricing in that if they strike and you launch one carrier, the next carrier for *them* jumps to 40M, and a second launch to 55M, which their bank cannot afford. The posture does not require you to actually launch three; it requires you to hold the bank at a level where the *threat* of launching two or three is real, which a 120M bank at zero prior launches does. The assumption in this scenario is that your income is still producing enough Gold to keep the bank at or above the two-carrier threshold even while you are not expanding, which is the condition under which the ahead-player posture is sustainable.

The second layer of the ahead-player posture is the **SAM ring**, because the ahead player is the one the neighbours are actually trying to strike, and the SAM ring is what makes the incoming strike uncertain. A SAM launcher starts at 1,500,000 Gold and climbs on the `(n+1) × 1,500,000` ladder capped at 3,000,000, with a 90-tick cooldown and range scaling from 70 to 150 tiles, so a ring of a few launchers around the core changes the neighbour's calculation from "the nuke lands and I take the crown" to "the nuke might not land, and I have to spend time building the strike while the ahead player's income keeps running." The SAM ring is the defensive expression of the posture for the ahead player, and the next section is the failure modes that turn either of these postures into a losing one, because the failure modes are the same on both sides of the board even though the goal is different.

## Failure modes and counter-moves

The posture has three failure modes that appear on both sides of the board, and each one has a counter-move that is usually cheaper than the posture itself if you catch it early. The first failure mode is **over-pushing so the neighbours out-scale you**, and it is the most common one because it looks like the posture's opposite, which is why it is easy to miss. A player in the posture who keeps a visible stack that is *too* big is not in the posture at all; they are in the posture *and* still making themselves the easy hit, because the neighbour's calculation is dominated by the visible entry point, not by the nuclear read. The counter-move is the subtraction the commit step is supposed to make: remove the visible stack to the level that makes a probe costly, not the level that makes you the prize, and check the target score again after the subtraction. If the score is still "the easy hit" after you have subtracted the stack, the problem is the bank, not the troops, and you need to raise the bank relative to the stack, which is a Gold-holding change, not a troop change.

The second failure mode is **spending the last banked Gold so the Silo has no refill**, and it is the one that turns a posture into a decoration. A player who holds the posture, then at the last moment spends the bank on a final expansion, is left with a Silo that cannot reload at the current ladder price, and the map reads that as "this one is about to run out," which is the most attractive target on the board. The counter-move is the *refill check* from the scenarios: before you hold the posture, verify that your income can refill the bank to the level you need, and before you spend any banked Gold, verify that the remaining bank can still refill a ready tube at the current MIRV price. If the answer is no, you are not in a posture, you are in a leak, and the right move is to abandon the posture and convert back to expansion before the leak empties the bank, not to hold on and hope the income catches up. The refill check is the difference between a posture and a decoration, and it is the check that the ahead player and the behind player both run, even though the bank levels are different.

The third failure mode is **treating the posture as a purchase rather than a live commitment**, and it is the conceptual one that the other two are symptoms of. A player who builds a Silo and a bank and then leaves them alone, expecting the posture to work on its own, is treating it as a purchase, and a purchase does not deter, because the threat only exists while the tube is ready and the bank is full. The counter-move is the loop from the decision framework: run the read, price, commit, and stop steps every few minutes of game time, and treat the posture as a *state you maintain* rather than a *thing you built*. The state is maintained by the bank refilling from income, the tube staying ready from reload Gold, and the expansion staying compact so the exposure does not reset. The next section is the mode adjustment, because the failure modes above are FFA-specific, and the posture changes when the audience is an alliance or a team rather than a set of independent neighbours.

## Mode and map adjustments: FFA versus Nations and team

The posture above is the FFA version, where the audience is a set of independent neighbours each running their own partial calculation, and the mode adjustment is about what changes when the audience is not independent. In **Nations mode**, the neighbours are grouped into alliances, and the target score is not "which single player is the easy hit," it is "which *alliance* is the easy hit," which means the posture has to be read against the alliance's combined bank and combined Silo tubes, not against one player's numbers. The MIRV ladder is still shared across the whole match, so a single launch by one member of an alliance advances the counter for everyone, including the attacking alliance, which means the deterrence price of a single ready tube is *higher* in Nations than in FFA, because the launch that moves the counter costs the attacking alliance the same ladder price as it costs the defending one. The posture for a Nations player is therefore to keep the *alliance's* bank at a level where a retaliation is affordable, even if your personal bank is thin, because the alliance's combined bank is the number the attacking alliance is pricing in, and a single ready tube from any member is the threat that changes that price.

In **team mode**, the adjustment is the opposite, because the audience includes your own teammates, and the target score has a teammate component: the question is not just "how do the enemies see me," it is "how do the enemies see *us*, and how do my teammates see *my* contribution." The posture for a team player is to keep the team's combined bank and the team's ready Silo tubes at a level where the team is the expensive target, and to avoid the individual over-push that makes *you* the easy hit even when the team as a whole is fine. The community's over-push warning is sharper in team mode, because an individual who over-pushes is not just making themselves the easy hit, they are making themselves the easy hit *and* slowing the team's troop regeneration, which is the two-fold cost the community describes. The posture for the team player is the same read, price, commit, and stop loop, run against the team's combined target score rather than the individual's, and the stop condition is the same: the posture is held while the team reads as the expensive target and stopped when the team's bank can no longer refill a ready tube at the current ladder price.

The **map adjustment** is the shape side of the mode adjustment, and it is the same in all three modes: the posture pairs the bank-and-Silo read with a *shape* change that reduces exposure. On a map with open water, the exposure is the coastline, and the posture is to keep the compact core away from the coast that a Warship screen can reach, which is the transport-landing threat the [Transport Landings guide](/guides/transport-landings/) owns. On a map with a tight core and long borders, the exposure is the border line, and the posture is to keep the line short and the troops distributed so a probe is costly but a maximal stack is not visible. The map change is a *shape* decision, not a Gold decision, and it is the part of the posture that costs no Gold at all, which is why it is the first lever to pull in any mode, because it moves the target score without touching the bank. The next section is the reading and evidence method, because the posture is only as good as the read, and the read has to be updated as the match changes.

## Reading the posture and the evidence method

The posture is a read, and a read has to be updated, which is why the evidence method is the last step of the loop. The evidence method is the same method the [Replay Review guide](/guides/replay-review/) uses for the whole match, applied to the posture specifically: after every strike, every launch, and every expansion by a neighbour, you re-run the read step and check whether the target score has changed. A neighbour who spent their bank on a City has a *lower* bank and a *lower* target score, which means the posture you were running against them is now over-priced and you can convert some of the held Gold back to expansion. A neighbour who launched a MIRV has moved the ladder, which means the posture you were running *with* is now cheaper to hold, because the next carrier for everyone is more expensive, and the threat your bank represents has just increased in value. The evidence method is the part of the loop that catches the *change*, because the posture is not a static state, it is a state that has to be re-priced every time the board changes.

The three evidence labels are the same as the broader method: **observed**, **inferred**, or **unknown**. A neighbour's bank is observed, because it is on the leaderboard. A neighbour's ready Silo tube is observed if you can see the Silo, inferred if you know they built it but cannot see the reload state, and unknown if you do not know they have one at all. The posture is only as good as the *worst* of the three labels on the neighbour you are most worried about, because a neighbour whose Silo state is unknown is a neighbour you cannot price, and an unpriced neighbour is a neighbour who can strike you without the ladder moving, which is the strike the posture was supposed to prevent. The evidence method is therefore a *check on the read*, and the read is a *check on the posture*, and the posture is a *check on the decision*, and the loop closes when the decision is the one you would make if you were the neighbour.

The final piece of the evidence method is the **stop condition** from the decision framework, because the posture is only complete when you know when to stop it. The stop condition is the specific number that tells you the posture has served its purpose: the bank has refilled to the level where you can afford the carrier you have been threatening, or a neighbour has committed so hard to another target that the read on you has reset. A player who stops the posture at the right moment converts the bank into the endgame economy or the endgame weapons, and a player who holds the posture too long sits on Gold that is no longer deterring anyone. The stop condition is the number that turns the posture from a *mood* into a *decision*, and it is the number that the ahead player and the behind player both set before they enter the posture, because a posture without a stop condition is a leak that does not know it is a leak. The next section is the version boundary, because the posture is priced against the v34 ladders, and the version boundary is the line between what the posture can rely on and what it cannot.

## Version boundary and what this guide does not own

This guide is priced against the **v34** release line, with the current authority being the official `v0.34.20` release and the stable v34 feature base at `v0.34.0`. The nuclear cost numbers — the MIRV ladder of 25,000,000 Gold plus 15,000,000 per prior launch, the Missile Silo at a fixed 1,000,000 with 100-tick construction and upgrade-reduced reload, and the SAM launcher at 1,500,000 to 3,000,000 with a 90-tick cooldown and 70 to 150 tile range — are anchored to the v34 release and tagged source, and the structure cost ladders are read from the site's generated structure database refreshed from the exact v34 tag. The version boundary is important because the posture is a *cost* decision, and the cost changes when the version changes: a MIRV ladder that was flat in a beta experiment was reverted before the stable release, which means the posture's value is the *shared* ladder, not a flat price, and a beta-era number is not the number the posture should be priced against.

The guide does not own the **mechanics** of the posture. It does not reteach how to build a Missile Silo, how to place a SAM launcher, how to launch a MIRV, or how to read the leaderboard's Gold Income columns, because those are the mechanics the [MIRV guide](/guides/mirv/), the [In-Game Gold Rates guide](/guides/in-game-gold-rates/), and the [Threat Assessment guide](/guides/threat-assessment/) own. It does not reteach the troop ratio math, because the [Attack Ratio guide](/guides/attack-ratio/) owns the troop-side of the same decision, and the posture is the *perception* side of the decision, not the *combat* side. It does not reteach the endgame, because the [Winning Overtime guide](/guides/winning-overtime/) owns the shrinking-territory threshold and the next-30-second crossing, and the posture is the *midgame* decision that prepares for the endgame, not the endgame decision itself. The version boundary is the line between what the posture can rely on — the v34 ladders and the shared MIRV counter — and what it cannot, which is any beta-era number, any mechanic owned by another guide, and any endgame threshold owned by the overtime guide.

The posture is a *midgame* decision, and the version boundary is the line between the midgame it is priced against and the endgame it prepares for. The midgame is where the bank is still refilling, the Silo is still building, and the ladder is still low, which is where the posture is cheapest to hold and most effective at changing the read. The endgame is where the bank is committed, the Silo is firing, and the ladder is high, which is where the posture has either succeeded in making the ahead player defensive or failed in keeping the behind player from being the easy hit. The version boundary is the line between those two, and it is the line that the stop condition from the decision framework sits on: the posture is held in the midgame and stopped at the line, and the endgame is the phase the stop condition converts the bank into. The posture is a decision about the midgame, priced against the v34 ladders, and the version boundary is the line that keeps it honest about which phase it is in and which numbers it is allowed to use.
