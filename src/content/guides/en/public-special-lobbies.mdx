## Direct answer: what decides a special lobby

Every public special lobby is assembled by the server in three rolls you can predict, even if you can't control. First, the modifier-count roll picks how many badges the lobby gets: the array `[1,1,1,2,2,2,2,2,3,3]` means 30% of special lobbies get exactly one modifier, 50% get two, and 20% get three — the cap of three has applied since v0.30.0. Second, each modifier is drawn from a 42-ticket weighted pool, where gold multiplier holds the most tickets (6 of 42, about 14.3% per draw) and the rarest badges — hard nations, alliances disabled, nukes disabled, SAMs disabled, and peace time — hold one ticket each (about 2.4% per draw). Third, a set of exclusivity and mode rules strips impossible combinations: you will never see two starting-gold tiers in one lobby, never hard nations with 25M gold, and never nukes-disabled with water nukes.

The queue itself keeps up to six lobbies scheduled per type (FFA, team, special) at all times, so special lobbies cycle continuously; your wait is measured in lobby countdowns, not in hours. Every seventh scheduled public game is additionally "trusted", capped at 25 players, and ineligible for the crowded modifier. The doomsday clock, when present, always starts with a 10-minute combat-only grace before any pressure ramps, and the four speeds (slow, normal, fast, veryfast) drive the bar to its 35% ceiling at 45, 35, 25, and 15 minutes respectively — team-mode lobbies climb a raised rung ladder but share the same ceilings and timings. Knowing these rules converts the lobby list from a lottery into a menu: you can estimate the odds of every badge, rule out combinations that cannot exist, and set personal join/no-join filters before the countdown ends.

None of the three rolls are visible before they happen — the lobby card is the reveal — so the practical skill is not prediction but fast evaluation: when a new special scrolls into the list, you have one countdown to classify its badges, check the exclusivity rules, read the player cap, and decide. Players who treat the card as trivia waste the queue; players who treat it as a decision input get to play the specials that actually train the skill they came to practice.

This matters more than it looks because the special queue is the only public queue where the match's identity is random at the lobby level. A normal FFA lobby always starts with the same default rules; a normal team lobby is always the same team structure. A special lobby can change the economy (gold tiers, gold multiplier), the map (compact, random spawn), the meta (nukes disabled, water nukes, SAMs disabled), the population (crowded), the endgame (doomsday clock), and the team structure (HvN, duos through 7-teams) — and it can combine several of those at once. So the decision to join a special is not "do I want to play OpenFront" but "which version of OpenFront do I want to play right now, and does this lobby's card describe that version?" The answer requires reading the card fluently, which is exactly what the next sections build. This guide walks through the full pool, the odds math, the exclusivity map, and a decision framework with concrete scenarios so you stop joining the first special that appears. All mechanics below are verified against the v0.34.17 server code and the release changelog for the versions that changed each rule.

## The queue: how lobbies are built and how long you actually wait

The master server runs one scheduling loop per lobby type. For each type — FFA, team, and special — it always keeps the next lobby armed with a start countdown, and as long as the queue holds fewer than six lobbies it schedules another from the map playlist. In practice this means the three queues run in parallel and independently: a special lobby being created does not pause the FFA queue, and vice versa. If you filter the lobby list to special games, you are usually looking at two or three queued specials with live countdowns, and the newest one is the one whose modifiers were just rolled.

A third queue fact is worth knowing before you sit in the list: lobbies do not pause because they are slow to fill. A special lobby that misses its start time with too few players simply expires and the next countdown takes over, so an unpopular combination (the community consistently reports ports-adjacent and alliance-disabled combos filling slowly) costs you that whole countdown of queue time with no compensation. The counter-strategy is to treat the visible countdown as your decision clock: join the lobby whose badges and player cap you want when it still has time to fill, rather than waiting for a perfect roll that may never come.

Filling speed is itself a signal you can read. A lobby at 20/125 with three minutes left is telling you the badges are unpopular — usually because they slow the early game (crowded at full capacity, nukes disabled, alliances disabled) or change the map in a way most players do not want to learn mid-queue. A lobby at 45/85 with two minutes left is healthy. A lobby that hits its cap early is the strongest signal that the combination matches the current player base's preferences, and joining an early-filling lobby also buys you a margin: if the game starts a minute early because everyone is ready, you are in. The inverse is a trap: an underfilled lobby often means the countdown is about to expire, and the "one more perfect lobby" you are waiting for is statistically the next one to expire.

Two practical queue habits follow. First, filter your lobby list to special games before the countdown starts, not after — the list refreshes continuously and the newest entry is the one with the freshest roll. Second, keep a running mental count of how many specials have expired while you wait; at two or three expirations in a row, the badge you are hunting is rarer than your patience, and the queue time you are spending is now worth more than the session you are trying to earn.

## Trusted lobbies and map-forced modifiers: the two overrides you must read

The third structural layer in the queue is the trusted game. Every seventh scheduled public game of any type is flagged trusted; a trusted lobby is capped at 25 players instead of the usual cap, and the crowded modifier is excluded from its roll because crowded exists specifically to raise the lobby toward the 125-player tier. You recognize a trusted lobby by its low player cap showing in the lobby card; if the countdown shows 25 as the max, you are about to join a smaller, faster-paced game where every opponent is individually visible and the endgame compresses toward the last few minutes. Trusted lobbies are not a different modifier pool — they run the same 42 tickets — but the 25-player cap changes how every badge plays, because the same gold tier or the same doomsday clock operates against a field of a quarter the size. If you are learning a modifier, a trusted lobby is the cleanest environment to learn it in: fewer players means fewer variables, and the faster pace means you hit the endgame before your attention budget runs out.

The second override is map-forced modifiers. Each map's definition can declare modifiers that are always on for that map — or on with a stated percentage chance — and it can also declare modifiers that are never rolled there. Islands maps, for example, cannot roll random spawn. When a map forces a modifier that the roll also produces, the forced copy wins and the roll result is dropped. This matters for your decision in two directions. First, a Falkland lobby with a forced modifier plus two rolled modifiers can end up with a combination the raw pool odds would understate: the forced badge is guaranteed to be there no matter what the roll says. Second, when the roll also produces what the map forces, the forced copy wins and the roll is wasted, so a count roll that "produced" three modifiers can still show a lobby with only two distinct badges. The pool odds in the next section assume no map involvement, so on maps that force or disable modifiers the real distribution shifts: a forced badge is guaranteed present, and a disabled badge is absent no matter what the roll says. The practical move is to read the lobby card's forced-modifier row before you trust the table, because the table is the baseline and the map is the correction. Getting this order right — map first, then badge — is the single highest-leverage reading habit in the special queue, because it is the only habit that protects you from both the guaranteed-forced badge and the silently-wasted roll slot at the same time.

## The modifier pool and the real odds of each badge

The special pool holds 42 weighted tickets. Here is the full breakdown with per-draw odds, followed by the more useful number: the chance that a badge appears at all in the lobby you join (which depends on the count roll and on which other badges were already taken):

| Modifier (badge) | Tickets | Per-draw odds | Odds of appearing in your lobby* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gold multiplier (2x gold) | 6 | 14.3% | ~26.8% |
| Random spawn | 4 | 9.5% | ~20.8% |
| Compact map | 4 | 9.5% | ~20.8% |
| Water nukes | 4 | 9.5% | ~20.8% |
| Doomsday clock | 4 | 9.5% | ~20.8% |
| 5M starting gold | 4 | 9.5% | ~20.8% |
| 25M starting gold | 3 | 7.1% | ~17.9% |
| Crowded (big lobby) | 2 | 4.8% | ~12.7% |
| 1M starting gold | 2 | 4.8% | ~12.7% |
| Hard nations | 1 | 2.4% | ~8.7% |
| Alliances disabled | 1 | 2.4% | ~8.7% |
| Nukes disabled | 1 | 2.4% | ~8.7% |
| SAMs disabled | 1 | 2.4% | ~8.7% |
| Peace time (4 min no-PVP) | 1 | 2.4% | ~8.7% |

\* Appearance odds assume a uniform draw: a one-modifier lobby (30%) contributes its per-draw odds, a two-modifier lobby (50%) contributes roughly twice the per-draw odds for non-exclusive badges, and a three-modifier lobby (20%) roughly three times. The pool weights trace to the community favorite-modifier poll that reshaped them in v0.30.5 — which is why gold multiplier is the single most common badge and why the five single-ticket badges form the rare tier.

Two reading tips. First, "per-draw" and "appearing" differ because of exclusivity: with three gold tiers taking 9 tickets of the 42, a second draw in a lobby that already rolled a gold tier has 33 of 42 viable tickets, so a specific second badge lands slightly under 2x its per-draw odds in two-modifier lobbies; the table uses the cleaner uniform approximation, which slightly overstates gold-tier odds and slightly understates the rare badges. Second, the doomsday clock badge hides a second roll: once the modifier is drawn, one of four speeds (slow, normal, fast, veryfast) is picked uniformly per game, a rotation added in v0.34.1 — so the badge tells you the endgame pressure mechanic is on, but not its pacing; check the lobby's speed label before assuming it plays like a normal clock.

A third reading tip concerns the rare tier specifically. The five single-ticket badges (hard nations, alliances disabled, nukes disabled, SAMs disabled, peace time) share the same 8.7% appearance odds, but their practical scarcity is not identical because of exclusivity with the more common badges: nukes disabled is mutually exclusive with water nukes (a 20.8% badge), so in lobbies where water nukes already rolled, the nukes-disabled ticket is dead and its effective odds fall further. If you are filtering for one of the five rare badges, expect to see it roughly once every dozen specials on a good day and once every forty on a bad one — the uniform approximation says about 1 in 11.5, and exclusivity plus the count roll pull the real number toward the less frequent end.

For planning purposes, the pool also gives you a stable ordering of "how often should I expect to see this": gold multiplier (26.8%) is the baseline "common"; the 9.5% tier (random spawn, compact, water nukes, doomsday, 5M gold) is "frequent, roughly one in five lobbies"; the 4.8-7.1% tier (25M gold, crowded, 1M gold) is "uncommon, worth a short wait"; and the 2.4% tier is "rare, do not wait for it". That four-band framing is what the scenario section below uses when it tells you to stop hunting.

## Exclusivity and mode rules: which combinations can never appear

The raw pool produces impossible pairs; the exclusivity rules remove them before the lobby is shown. The full verified rule set, in v0.34.17, is smaller than most players assume:

- The three starting-gold tiers (1M, 5M, 25M) are pairwise exclusive. A lobby has at most one gold tier, and 25M additionally excludes hard nations.
- Nukes disabled is exclusive with both SAMs disabled and water nukes. These three form one "nuke family": a special lobby can contain at most one of them. (Water nukes is in the family only for the nukes-disabled pairing — water nukes and SAMs disabled can appear together.)
- Team special games never roll hard nations — the badge is FFA-only.
- Duo, trio, and quads team specials never roll random spawn.
- FourIslands team specials never roll gold multiplier, because the map is too prone to long stalemates at doubled gold.
- Special team maps never roll nukes disabled, because cross-water attacks become nearly impossible and the result is extreme warship spam.
- Humans-vs-Nations specials never roll 25M starting gold and never roll peace time — nations have no PVP immunity, so a no-PVP window has no effect and 25M gold distorts the nation AI economy.
- Trusted lobbies (25-player cap) never roll crowded, since crowded's whole job is to push the lobby to the big tier.

Beyond exclusivity, mode changes the team-count roll for team specials: the pool weights 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7-team configs at 10 tickets each, Duos at 5, Trios and Quads at 7.5 each, and Humans-vs-Nations at 20 — so HvN is roughly one in five team specials, which is why it feels like the "default" team special. Maps that declare a preferred special team count use it 75% of the time instead of the pool. Two practical consequences: if you want HvN specifically, team specials are the only place it rolls and you should expect it about every fifth team lobby; and if you want a pure random-spread FFA special, avoid duo/trio/quads team lobbies entirely because they structurally cannot carry the random-spawn badge.

A final structural note: map-disabled modifiers interact with exclusivity. If a map disables a badge that the exclusivity rules already limited, the effective odds drop further; the reverse (a map forcing a badge) raises the odds above the table, because the forced badge is guaranteed present whether or not the roll produced it. Always read the lobby card's forced-modifier row before applying the table — the table is the baseline, the map is the correction, and the forced row is the one cell of the lobby card that can move your odds above or below every number you just computed.

## Decision framework: which lobby to join, with scenarios

Treat the lobby list as a menu and run every candidate through four filters in order. Filter 1 — intent: what do you want to practice? If it is economy, join a gold-tier or gold-multiplier lobby. If it is endgame survival, join a doomsday lobby and read its speed. If it is open-map reading, avoid compact and avoid crowded (crowded lobbies at 85-125 players bury your map-read under noise). Filter 2 — odds sanity: if the badge you wanted is single-ticket (the 2.4% tier), do not burn queue time hunting it; it averages about one lobby in forty. Filter 3 — cap and pace: a 25-player trusted lobby runs the same modifiers in a much faster, more personal game; a crowded lobby inverts the early game entirely (more players, slower map coverage, later first contact). Filter 4 — exclusivity check: verify the lobby's badges do not contain a combination you know cannot exist; if one does, you are looking at a forced-map situation and should re-read which map forced what.

Scenario A — you have five minutes to kill and want a high-pace FFA special. The list shows: (1) Compact + 5M gold, 45/85 players, 1:40 left; (2) 25M gold + random spawn, 20/25 (trusted), 2:10 left; (3) Water nukes + SAMs disabled, 30/125, 3:30 left. Pick (2): trusted means the game starts soon, 25M gold plus random spawn is a pure economy-and-luck test you can finish within your window, and 25M gold is the second-most-weighted gold tier (17.9% appearance odds) so you are not gambling on a rare badge. Reject (1) — compact plus 5M gold in a nearly full 85-player lobby is a war-of-attrition game that outlasts five minutes. Reject (3) — water nukes changes the naval economy and SAMs disabled changes air defense; that combination is a research game, not a quick game, and it is already underfilled at 30/125, so the countdown is likely to expire on you.

Scenario B — you are a mid-ranked player who keeps losing specials and suspects the modifiers are the problem. Do not change your skill; change your selection. The failure pattern behind most "I lost because of the modifier" reports is joining a rare-badge lobby (hard nations, nukes disabled) without knowing what it does to the opening. Rule: only join a single-ticket badge lobby if you can name its opening implication. Hard nations doubles the nation army threat, so your opening must buy time against AI before it turns to PVP. Nukes disabled removes the nuke trade entirely and makes SAM placement the first investment of the game. If you cannot articulate that in ten seconds, pick the two-modifier lobby with gold-tier or compact instead — those are the badges with the most player knowledge behind them, so the lobby's skill signal is cleaner.

## Failure modes and counterplay

The most common special-lobby failure is queue-stall on a rare combo: you filter for hard nations or nukes disabled, sit through three or four expiring countdowns, and lose more time than a normal game would have cost. Counterplay: set a hard budget of two expiring countdowns; after the second, join whatever viable special is next regardless of badge, because the information gained from playing any special beats the information gained from watching a fourth empty one.

Second failure: misreading the doomsday speed. The badge does not tell you the pacing — slow, normal, fast, and veryfast all carry the same icon — and a veryfast clock compresses the entire endgame window to a fraction of normal. If the lobby card shows the speed, weigh it against your recovery skill; if you are weak at territory recovery, filter out fast and veryfast before the first wave arrives rather than discovering it during the first skull. The doomsday-clock guide ([doomsday clock](/guides/doomsday-clock/)) on this site breaks down each speed's exact timing, and it is worth reading before you commit to a clock lobby.

Third failure: the crowded trap. Crowded raises the lobby toward 125 players, which sounds exciting but changes three things: your gold-per-tile economy (more players competing for the same map), your visibility (the map is genuinely hard to track at that population), and your endgame (doomsday-style share targets are measured against a field with twice the players). If your target is rank progress rather than spectacle, crowded is a negative-EV badge for most players; join it to learn map tracking, not to win.

Fourth failure: trusting the badge over the map. A badge is a default; the map can override or force. If a map forces water nukes, the naval economy applies whether or not the lobby's roll produced it, and your land-first opening is already wrong before the first shot. Counterplay: read the map name first, then the badges, then the player cap. That order — map, badges, cap — is the same order the lobby card presents them for a reason.

Fifth failure: assuming a bad roll means a bad game. A 1M-gold lobby (12.7% appearance odds) punishes slow early expansion but rewards the player who buys the first warship on schedule; an alliances-disabled lobby (8.7%) converts a team-mode lobby into a hard-FFA one and is the single best practice environment for FFA players queued into team specials. Every badge has a player who wins more with it; the question to ask is whether that player is you this session.

Sixth failure: skipping the doomsday-speed read in a clock lobby because the game "still has time". Every speed starts with the same 10-minute combat-only grace, but the bar's trajectory diverges hard after that: normal reaches 35% at 35 minutes, slow at 45, fast at 25, and veryfast at just 15 — so a veryfast lobby applies full endgame pressure at half the time a normal lobby does, and the damage ramp a skulled side takes per second climbs with the bar either way. If you are using a doomsday lobby to practice recovery, deliberately choose slow or normal speed from the lobby list; using a veryfast lobby to learn recovery trains the exact panic pattern you are trying to eliminate. The counter-rule generalizes: a modifier is a tool, and a tool you cannot name is a hazard, not a training aid — join the special where the badge matches a skill you already have, and treat the rare-badge lobbies as experiments you schedule deliberately rather than accidents you fall into.

A final cross-check failure: joining a special on a map you have never played. The map's forced and disabled modifiers silently reshape the badge set, and on an unfamiliar map you are evaluating two unknowns at once — the map and the modifiers — which doubles the chance that your opening is wrong for reasons the badge row does not show. If the map is new, either join a special on a map you know, or join the new-map special with the expectation that the first game is a map-learning game, not a rank game, and weight your badge choice toward the familiar (a gold tier you understand beats a rare badge you do not).

## Mode and map adjustments

FFA specials: the full pool applies except the team-count roll does not exist; your decision space is the 14 badges and the map. FFA players should also remember that random spawn is the most common structural change in the queue (20.8% appearance odds), so an FFA opening that assumes a fixed spawn order is only reusable in about four of every five specials. If you want to practice your standard opening, filter out random spawn explicitly.

Team specials: the modifier pool is identical, but three badges are removed structurally (hard nations, and random spawn in duo/trio/quads) and the team count itself is rolled from its own weighted pool — 60% of team specials land on one of the six standard team counts, 20% on HvN, and the remaining 20% split between duos and trios/quads. If your team role is economy, join gold-tier team specials and ignore crowded; if your role is map control, crowded team specials are the best training ground because the population raises the value of every contested choke.

Humans-vs-Nations specials: the two exclusions (no 25M gold, no peace time) mean HvN lobbies always have a working PVP economy and an immediate threat; treat the first two minutes as nation-defense planning, not as scouting. If you see an HvN lobby with gold multiplier, it is a high-temperature lobby — the nations have the same doubled-gold opening you do — and the pace is roughly 20% faster than a plain HvN game.

Map adjustments: ocean maps amplify water nukes (the badge's whole point is a naval economy that only exists across water, which the [water nukes](/guides/water-nukes/) guide on this site covers in depth), island maps cannot roll random spawn and tend to force their own spawn logic, and the FourIslands team map is the one map where gold multiplier is structurally removed from team specials. Compact is a map-size change, not a modifier in the economic sense: it shortens travel times, which accelerates the first contact and makes the gold tiers slightly less forgiving, because the window to bank early gold before the first fight closes sooner. When two badges interact — compact plus 25M gold is the sharpest combination in the pool — expect the lobby to decide in the first ten minutes and plan your opening accordingly.

The map name is also your first hint about the lobby's character before you read any badge. Large ocean maps with scattered landmasses reward water nukes and punish land-heavy openings; single-continent maps with dense ports make the normal nuke economy the default and make nukes disabled a much sharper change; and the crowded tier only makes sense on maps with enough territory to actually host a large population. If a lobby card shows an unfamiliar map, the map page on this site lists the forced and disabled modifiers for it — reading that one line before joining is cheaper than learning the map's overrides in your first (possibly lost) game. The map-strategy guide ([map strategy](/guides/map-strategy/)) collects the per-map openings that pair well with each of these structural shifts, so checking it before a new-map special saves you the cost of a blind opening.

One adjustment is worth memorizing: the interaction between map-forced modifiers and the three-modifier cap. The server applies at most three modifiers total, and map-forced modifiers count inside that cap rather than stacking beyond it. So on a map that forces water nukes, one of your three slots is already taken by the map, and the remaining badges come from fewer draws than the table assumes — which is why a forced-modifier lobby can look like it has "only" two rolled badges even when the count roll produced three, and why the odds of seeing a specific second badge are lower than the table says.

## Putting it together: a pre-join checklist

Run this checklist top to bottom on every special lobby card, in under ten seconds. One: read the map name and the forced-modifier row; the map sets the floor, and a forced badge has already spent one of the three slots the roll can fill, so the rest of the card has less randomness left than it looks. Two: read the badges and check the exclusivity map in your head — gold tier, nuke family, HvN pair, trusted/crowded — if a badge combination is impossible, the card is telling you the map forced something; re-read the forced row before deciding it is a data error. Three: read the player cap; 25 means trusted and fast, 125 means crowded and noisy, anything else is a normal fill, and the cap is a pace signal more than a difficulty signal. Four: read the doomsday speed if the clock badge is present; fast and veryfast are different games entirely, with 25- and 15-minute ceilings instead of the 35- and 45-minute defaults, and the speed label is the only place the lobby tells you which one you are about to play. Five: run the four filters — intent, odds sanity, cap and pace, exclusivity — and decide join or skip before the next lobby scrolls in; the decision should take seconds, not a countdown's worth of thinking, because the queue will hand you the next special regardless of what you do.

The strategic payoff of the checklist is not finding better lobbies; it is refusing worse ones faster. The queue generates specials continuously, six deep at all times, so the supply of joinable specials is effectively unlimited and the only scarce resource is your queue time. A player who joins every second viable special and plays it fully outqueues a player who watches for a perfect roll that averages one in forty lobbies.

Three habits make the checklist stick. First, practice the five reads in the lobby list while idle — classify every special that scrolls by without joining, out loud if you can — so that by the time you actually want to play, the reads are automatic instead of deliberate. Second, keep a two-count rule for rare badges: two expiring countdowns on a hunt for a single-ticket badge ends the hunt, full stop; the third expiration would cost more queue time than the expected value of the badge justifies. Third, match the lobby to the session's goal, not to the moment's impulse: a ranked-push session wants clean-signal lobbies (gold tiers, compact, standard clocks), a practice session wants the weird ones (nukes disabled, HvN, veryfast clock), and a casual session can take whatever fills. The modifiers are the game's randomizer; your filter is your counter to it. Master the pool odds, respect the exclusivity rules, and the special queue stops being a lottery and starts being a menu you actually know how to order from.
