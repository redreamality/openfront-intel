**Direct answer.** Your FFA rating moves with the result against a field OFstats expected, so the climb is about (1) only queueing games that count, (2) winning against the strongest field you can realistically beat, and (3) when you lose, placing as high as you can by banking structures and gold so the placement adjustment costs you less. In the early high-uncertainty window a single win can be worth a +166 median; a 3-player lobby is worth zero.

## The gain-per-game model: what actually moves your number

The first decision to internalize is that your rating is not a score you grind up with a fixed point value. There is no hidden "+20 for a win" you can collect the same way every match. OFstats updates the number from three inputs at once: the outcome (you won or you did not), the strength of the field you faced (were you expected to win, by how much, and how were the others rated), and your current uncertainty (the ± around your point estimate). A win against a field the model thought would beat you moves the number up by a lot; a win against a field it thought was far weaker moves it up by a little; a loss against a field it thought was far weaker costs a lot. The point is that the *size* of the swing is a property of the match, not a fixed reward, and you control which matches you step into and how you play them.

This is the whole decision model for climbing. The rating change on the win screen is the actual score of the game, and it is the difference between your rating before and after. A positive change means the match produced more evidence that you are stronger than the model previously thought; a negative change is the opposite. Two games with the same win/loss can produce very different changes, which is why "win rate" alone is a poor guide to how fast you are climbing. A climber who wins most games against the weakest available lobbies and loses occasionally to stronger fields will climb far slower than a climber who wins a smaller share of games but wins the ones the model priced against them and loses only to fields above them. The habit that follows is to read the rating change and the expected percentage after every match, not the win/loss line, and to treat a big positive change against a hard field as the most valuable result of the block.

The reason this matters for the season is that the three inputs are not static across your ladder. Your outcome input you can only partly control; the field input is a choice you make by reading the lobby before you lock in; and the uncertainty input drifts on its own as you play rated games. So the climb is not "win more" but "make each rated game carry the most rating it can, and skip the games that carry the least." That reframing is what turns the ladder from a streak-chasing activity into a resource allocation problem, and every later section is an instance of that single decision: which rated games do you play, and how do you play the ones you play so they move your point estimate in the right direction by the largest amount available.

## The high-uncertainty window is the biggest rating-gain window

The single most valuable window on the ladder is when your uncertainty is wide, and Season 1 made that window unusually generous for everyone at once. When a new season starts, OFstats re-baselines the field and widens every player's uncertainty, described as "as if they had been away 20 days," so the first games after the reset move a player more than the same games mid-season. The effect is large and quantified: for a player in the high-uncertainty band (a ± of 130 or more, regardless of their point estimate), a win with a win chance under 1% has a median change of +166.0, while a loss in that same band costs a median of only -5.1. That asymmetry is the engine of an early-season climb: when the system is unsure of you, a win is worth an order of magnitude more than a loss costs, so the correct posture is to play rated games aggressively and bank wins while the margin is wide.

The practical plan for this window has three numbers. First, your ± : while it is 130 or higher you are in the high-value band, and every win you bank is disproportionately worth something. Second, your win chance in the lobby, which the interface exposes as the expected value: a win chance under 1% is exactly the condition the +166 figure refers to, which is the "you are the underdog" game and the most valuable one you can win. Third, the season date: the window is front-loaded, so the first week or two of the season is when the uncertainty is widest and the swings are biggest. As you accumulate rated games the ± narrows and the median win change collapses toward single digits (a 2200+ player with a 41% win chance gains a median of only +4.8 on a win), so the same win that was worth +166 early is worth +4.8 later. The decision this drives is about *when* to commit to a rated block: front-load your rated volume into the early, high-uncertainty phase of the session, when a win is worth the most, rather than spreading the same games evenly across eight weeks.

There is a compounding effect that makes the early window even more valuable than the raw numbers suggest, because the +166-class win does not just add rating; it also collapses your uncertainty in one move, which means the next win is measured against a higher, tighter baseline. In other words, an early win buys you two things at once: the large immediate swing and a faster path to the point where the model has enough data to stop over-estimating or under-estimating you. A player who skips the early window and starts rated play mid-season misses both benefits at once, and has to make up the rating gap later when each win is worth less and each loss costs more. The net effect is that the season's climb is front-loaded not just in raw points but in information, and the decision rule is the same as the others: the earlier and the wider the ±, the more a single rated win is worth, so bank them while they are cheap and abundant.

## Which games count at all: the rated-game eligibility gate

The cheapest way to lose rating progress is to play games that the system does not count at all, and the eligibility contract is specific. A game only counts toward your FFA rating if it is a public FFA game (not a private or custom lobby), at least 10 human players actually spawned in the match, exactly one human player is the winner, the total game duration is 40 seconds or more, and you yourself spawned in and were logged in. Each of those is a hard gate, and a single failure means the result produces no rating update no matter how you played. This matters more than most players realize because public FFA lobbies are often small and fast: a lobby with three or six humans that ends early produces nothing, and a game that collapses to a human-free ending or a sub-40-second scrum is the same.

The decision this gate creates is a *queue or drop* call, and it should be made before you commit to the match. The signal to watch is the spawn count: if the lobby has fewer than the 10 humans required by the time you are near spawn, the game is unlikely to qualify even if you win, so the rating cost of the time you spend there is zero return. On the other hand, a full or near-full lobby that stays populated through the endgame is the game that actually moves your number, and it is the one worth committing to. Two habits follow. First, check the human count before you lock in and treat a thin lobby as a no-bet game where the only thing you are buying is experience, not rating. Second, understand that the win condition in these lobbies is a single human survivor, so a game you lead but fail to close is, from the rating's point of view, the same as a loss — you did not win the one thing that banks points. The climb is a function of the rated games that complete, not the number of FFA matches you queue into, so counting the games that qualify is the first metric a climber should track.

The 40-second duration gate and the single-human-winner gate interact in a way that punishes games that look winnable but are not. A game that reaches a decisive end in under 40 seconds, even one you were leading, produces no rating data point, so a climber should avoid the early-game rushes and the instant-collapse lobbies that end before the duration floor is reached. Likewise, a game where the human field thins below the eligibility threshold before a winner is declared is worth nothing, which is why the endgame habit of forcing a single-human survivor — eliminating players one at a time rather than racing to a territory line — is worth more than it looks. The through-line is the same as the other two gates: the rating only updates on a *complete, qualifying* match, so the climb rewards the games that finish cleanly and penalizes the ones that fizzle, and a climber should treat the lobby composition before spawn as the single highest-leverage read of the match.

## Winning is the only points you bank: the climb win routine

Because the rating banks a result only when you are the winner, the highest-leverage work a climber does is converting rated games into wins, and the community data points to a concrete routine for doing that. A dataset of roughly 7,000 public FFA games on the World map found that winners were about 43% more likely to commit ships early, about 33% more likely to use the map edges for their territory, and that "waiting longer is a winning strategy," with winners opening their first aggressive move around the one-minute-six-second mark. Those three are the levers: an early naval commitment so you are not landlocked into a corner, edge land so your expansion is deep and hard to pinch, and patience so you are the one making the first decisive move rather than reacting to it.

The routine that ties those levers into a repeatable win has a shape. Early, place your first city on a defensive edge or a hard-to-take pocket, commit a couple of warships into the water so the map edges work for you, and expand into the big connected land rather than over-fragmenting into small pockets. Midgame, keep your troop levels high and your economy feeding the two or three cities that actually matter, take weak land while other players trade, and bank gold because gold is the tiebreaker the placement adjustment uses when a game is not yours. Endgame, the decision that decides the one win is the 80-land line and the MIRV calculus: once the territory threshold that wins the game is reachable, the question is whether to push it yourself or to let the field nuke each other down to the finish. A climber running this routine is not playing a different game from casual FFA — the map reading, the economy pacing, and the endgame call are the same — but they are playing it toward the single outcome, the win, that the rating banks.

The metric that proves the routine is working is not the win rate but the *rated* win rate, which is the share of your qualifying games you win, because the rating only moves on the games that complete. A player who queues twenty FFA matches but only five of them reach the ten-human and forty-second thresholds is really playing five rated games, and the climb is a function of those five. The routine above is what turns the five into wins: the early ships keep the game from collapsing into a landlocked corner that ends too fast to qualify, the edge land keeps the territory connected enough to force eliminations, and the patience keeps you from rushing into a sub-40-second scrum that produces nothing. ## The median change table: what field strength is worth

The median change table is the single most useful number a climber can keep in their head, because it converts "which field should I face" into a concrete expected swing. The table is keyed by the player's point-estimate band, the win chance the model assigns to them in the lobby, and the outcome. In the low bands the asymmetry is extreme: below 1600 with a win chance under 1%, a win is worth a median of +29.8 while a loss is only -0.3; from 1600 to 1900 at a 3% win chance, a win is +18.2 and a loss is -0.8; from 1900 to 2200 at a 13% win chance, a win is +9.7 and a loss is -1.7. Only at the top, 2200 and above at a 41% win chance, does a win drop to a median of +4.8 and a loss climb to -3.4. The high-uncertainty band is a separate row: at a ± of 130 or more, a win with a win chance under 1% is a median of +166.0 and a loss is -5.1.

| Point-estimate band | Win chance | Win (median) | Loss (median) |
|---|---|---|---|
| under 1600 | under 1% | +29.8 | -0.3 |
| 1600 to 1900 | 3% | +18.2 | -0.8 |
| 1900 to 2200 | 13% | +9.7 | -1.7 |
| 2200 and above | 41% | +4.8 | -3.4 |
| high uncertainty (± 130+), any rating | under 1% | +166.0 | -5.1 |

The decision the table drives is about *where to aim*. Early and low, your wins are worth the most and your losses cost almost nothing, so you want to be in games you have a realistic shot at, because the reward for winning a slightly-harder field is the largest of your life on this ladder. As you rise, the same logic inverts: at 2200 and above a win is worth almost nothing and a loss costs real rating, so the optimal posture is to play only the games you can reliably win and to sit out the lobbies that are too strong, because the downside now outweighs the upside. The crossover is the shape of the whole climb: aggressive and risk-on at the bottom where wins are cheap wins and expensive wins are worth a fortune, and selective and risk-off near the top where the rating you have is worth protecting.

The single most important row to internalize is the high-uncertainty row, because it is the only row where the numbers are two orders of magnitude apart: +166.0 for a win against -5.1 for a loss means the expected value of a rated game in that band is so strongly positive that the optimal play is to play as many rated games as possible, regardless of the point-estimate band you are in. The second most important row is the 2200-and-above row, because it is the only row where the win value drops below the loss cost in absolute terms relative to the rating you are defending, and that is the row that tells a climber to switch from volume to selection. Everything in between — the 1600-to-1900 and 1900-to-2200 rows — is a gradual slope, and the practical read is that the middle of the ladder is where the climb is steady and where the daily habit of "play the games you can beat" does most of the work. The table is not a target to hit; it is a map of where the value is, and the climb is about walking the path that keeps you in the high-value rows for as long as the season allows.

## The placement adjustment: how a loss costs you, and how to soften it

The second half of the climb model is what happens when you are not the winner, because most rated games are losses and the cost of each one is a lever you can pull. Non-winners get a placement adjustment rather than a simple loss, and the order that determines the size of the adjustment is the number of structures you captured, with gold as the tiebreaker when structure counts are equal. Survival time alone does not rank you: a player who survives to the end but controls no structures and holds no gold is placed by those two metrics, not by how long they lasted. This is the mechanism that turns a "loss" into a spectrum from a near-free loss to a costly one, and the decision is how high you can place yourself in the games you do not win.

The concrete way to soften a loss is to bank the two metrics the adjustment reads. Structures captured means the cities and strongholds you actually hold at the moment the game is decided, so a climber should hold the structures that are still on the board rather than trading them away for a last stand that loses; a player who has banked three structures is placed above one who has banked one, and that gap is the difference between a -1.7 loss and a steeper one. Gold is the second metric and the tiebreaker, which is why the midgame habit of accumulating gold while trading is not just economy — it is the thing that softens every future loss. Two scenarios make this concrete. In a game you cannot win, the move that banks the most rating is to keep your land connected, hold your remaining structures, and farm gold until the endgame, because that places you high in the non-winner order and shrinks the loss. In a game you can win, the move is to take the win rather than convert it into a strong second, because the winner gets no placement adjustment at all and the win is strictly worth more than the best possible loss.

A third scenario is the one that most players get wrong, the near-win that converts into a strong second because the climber is afraid of the endgame nuke and backs off the last territory push. In that case the player held the best structures and the most gold of the field, so the placement adjustment prices them as the near-winner and the loss costs almost nothing — but they also gave up the win, which would have been worth far more than any placement. The lesson is that the placement adjustment is a floor, not a ceiling: it softens the worst case, but it does not recover the win you let go, so the decision rule is the same as the others, take the win when the 80-land line is reachable and let the placement adjustment handle the games you genuinely cannot win. ## Two climbs with numbers: early-season underdog and late-season push

The two decisions that matter most on the climb are how to play the early high-uncertainty phase and how to push a near-top rating when wins are worth little and losses cost a lot. Both are about allocating a fixed amount of rated games across a season, and both have a concrete plan with numbers.

**Early-season underdog, around 1550 with a ± of 140.** This is the highest-value state on the ladder. Your win chance in a mid-sized lobby is low, so the model prices you as the underdog, and the high-uncertainty band means a win with a win chance under 1% is worth a median of +166.0 while a loss is only -5.1. The plan: play a short, focused block of five rated games where you spawn into a full 10+ human lobby, commit the win routine (edge land, early ships, patience), and aim for one or two wins. If you win one, the +166-class swing against a hard field has moved your rating by more than a month of mid-season grinding would; if you lose all five, the cost is a median of -5.1 each, roughly 25 total, which is recoverable. The numbers say the expected value of an aggressive early block is strongly positive, because the win is worth twenty-five times the loss. The only game you should avoid in this state is the 3- or 4-player lobby, which produces zero points and wastes the window.

**Late-season push, around 2280 with a ± of 40.** This is the opposite posture. You are in the 2200+ band where a win at a 41% win chance is worth a median of only +4.8 and a loss is -3.4, so the margin between a win and a loss has collapsed and the optimal play is selection, not aggression. The plan: queue only lobbies you can realistically win — the ones where your field reading says you are the favorite or a near-even match — and sit out the lobbies full of 2300+ players, because a loss there costs real rating for a win that is worth almost nothing. Aim for a block of four to six selective games, keep your land connected so your losses (the ones that happen) place high and cost little, and use gold to soften them. The number that decides this block is the win chance in each lobby: you want it at or above the 41% the top band implies, and you want to skip any lobby where the model prices you at the 13% the 1900-to-2200 band implies, because that is a game you were not the favorite in and the loss will cost you disproportionately.

The two scenarios are not just opposites; they are the two ends of a single curve, and the climb is about knowing which end you are on at any given moment. The number that tells you is the ± : wide (130 and up) means you are on the underdog end and should be playing volume, narrow (under 50, say) means you are on the push end and should be playing selection. The point estimate is the second read: low (under 1900) means the wins are still worth a lot, high (over 2200) means the wins are worth little and the losses cost real rating. The through-line is the same decision the whole guide is about, which rated games do you play and how do you play them, and the answer in both scenarios is the same sentence: play the games that move your point estimate the most, and skip the ones that move it the least. ## Failure modes and the counterplay for each

Climbers make the same five mistakes, and each has a concrete counterplay. The failures are not "I lost a game" — they are reading errors about which games count, what a win is worth, and how a loss is priced.

**Failure 1: queueing into lobbies that do not count.** The most common and the most expensive, because it costs time for zero return. A 3- or 4-player public FFA, a game that ends under 40 seconds, or a lobby that never reaches 10 humans produces no rating update. The counterplay is the "check the spawn count" rule: before you lock in, confirm the lobby has at least the 10 humans the eligibility contract requires, and treat a thin lobby as a no-bet game. The cheap version of the fix is to leave a thin lobby early and re-queue into a full one, because a full lobby is the game that actually moves your number.

**Failure 2: playing out of your uncertainty band.** A player at 2200+ plays the same aggressive, risk-on games they played at 1600, where a win was worth +29.8 and a loss was -0.3. At 2200+ a win is worth +4.8 and a loss is -3.4, so the identical aggressive play now has a negative expected value. The counterplay is the "posture tracks the band" rule: aggressive and risk-on in the low and high-uncertainty bands where wins are worth a fortune, selective and risk-off in the 2200+ band where the rating you have is worth protecting. The number that tells you which posture to take is the win chance the interface shows for the lobby.

**Failure 3: converting a winnable game into a strong second.** A player who is ahead converts a certain win into a "safe" second because they are afraid of the endgame, and the rating banks nothing from a second — only the winner's result is positive. The counterplay is the "the winner gets no adjustment" rule: a win is strictly worth more than the best possible loss, so when the 80-land line is reachable, take the win. The endgame call is the MIRV calculus — decide whether you can push the winning territory before the field nukes each other, and if you are the strongest on the board, close it.

**Failure 4: treating survival time as a placement.** A player who survives to the endgame but controls no structures and holds no gold expects to be placed high because they "lasted the longest," but the placement adjustment orders non-winners by structures captured, with gold as the tiebreaker. The counterplay is the "bank structures and gold" rule: hold the structures still on the board and accumulate gold through the midgame, because those two are the only metrics the adjustment reads.

**Failure 5: planning around the wrong season boundary.** Because the season re-baselines and widens the ± at a UTC midnight boundary, a player who plans around their local midnight or around "a month" ends up out of phase with the window. The counterplay is the "watch the season countdown" rule: the interface shows the season dates and the remaining time, so use that as the authoritative measure of how much of the high-uncertainty window is left, and front-load rated volume into it. The through-line of all five is the same reading error in different clothes: mistaking a game that does not move your rating for one that does, or mistaking a win's value for the value of a different state, and the fix is always the same number, the rating change and the ± after the match. ## Mode, map, and season-phase adjustments

The final layer is how the climb flexes by map and by the phase of the season you are in, because the same win routine produces different data points on different maps and different value at different times of the season.

**Map adjustments.** Public FFA spans the full map pool, and the map you land on sets the tempo of the match and the quality of the rating data point it produces. On a high-land, compact map where most of the territory is solid and connected, the game is a spread-and-accumulate contest: place the first city on the widest bridge, spread into the biggest pockets, and hold the most connected land. On a water-heavy map with internal seas and broken archipelagos, the game is a pinch-and-eliminate contest: own the land bridges early, target the smaller players in sequence, and force eliminations so the field shrinks toward the single human winner the rating requires. The adjustment that matters for the climb specifically is that a rating data point is most informative when your play is representative of your real skill, so on a map you know well you produce a cleaner signal and the win you bank is a truer measure of your strength than a win you coasted to on an unfamiliar map. The practical habit is to note where your win rate and your rating change diverge across map families — if you are consistently worse on water-heavy maps, that is a real gap the rating is measuring, and closing it is the fastest way to move the point estimate up across the maps you actually play.

**Season-phase adjustments.** The eight-week season has a shape, and the value of a rated game is not flat across it. In the first week or two after a reset the uncertainty is widest for everyone, so the high-uncertainty band is open to the whole field and a win is worth the most it will be all season; the goal in this phase is quantity of rated wins, because every win banks the maximum-value result. In the middle of the season the board is stable and the top of each rank is set, so the goal is to climb within your band: play the games you are the favorite in, keep your land connected so your losses place high, and push toward the next division. In the final stretch the goal is to consolidate: if you are near the top of your band's top 250, protect that position with selective, low-risk games; if you are below it, use the last rated games to close the gap, because the next season's board is a fresh field and a strong finish is the best heading into the reset.

The habit that ties the phases and the maps together is the same as the other ranked ladders and the same as the rest of this guide: treat each rated game as a data point, read the rating change and the ± after every match, and track the point estimate and the uncertainty across the whole season rather than over a single game. The map tells you the shape of the match, the phase tells you the value of the win, and the ± tells you which posture to take, and the climb is the product of reading all three at once and playing the game that, given those three, moves your point estimate the most. That is the entire decision model, and it is the same model whether you are at 1500 in week one or 2300 in week seven, only the numbers in the rows change.
