## Direct answer: only the leader can send, only to members, and everyone sees it

A clan Plutonium payout is a one-way transfer of Plutonium out of the shared clan fund, addressed to one current member, and initiated by the clan leader alone. It shipped in v0.36.3 (commit `ced0c4e0a`, PR #6393) and is unchanged in the current version, v0.36.9. The leader opens the clan fund screen, picks a member, types an integer amount that does not exceed the per-member limit shown live on screen, and confirms a dialog that reads, verbatim from the game: "Send {"{amount}"} Plutonium from the fund [{"{tag}"}] to {"{name}"}? This cannot be undone." That single sentence carries the whole decision, because four facts gate the transfer, and each one removes a different kind of regret that the design is explicitly trying to eliminate.

First, only the leader can send, and only to another current member of the clan, never to the leader's own account, so a payout is a leadership decision, not a member right. Second, the transfer is capped by a regime of limits set by the operator, including a per-clan daily cap and a per-member daily cap, a minimum time in the clan before a member can receive, a cooldown before a new leader can send, and account properties such as not being banned. Third, every transfer is visible to all members: who sent, who received, how much, when, and whether it was reversed. Fourth, the transfer is final unless the operator reverses it, which is recorded, not deleted.

Hold those four facts in mind before you type a number, because each one changes the decision. The "leader only" rule tells you this is a budgeting decision, not a self-service withdrawal; the limits tell you how much you can move and who is eligible; the visibility rule tells you there is no discreet spend; and the finality rule tells you the only way back from a payout is an operator reversal, not a refund. If any one of those four is missing from your mental model, the payout will feel like a mistake after you confirm, and the design has already told you which of the four you failed to read. That is the direct answer: this is a leader-only, member-only, visible, and mostly final transfer of premium currency, and the decision is whether the spend is worth a permanent, on-record entry in a ledger every member can read. Before you reach for the amount field, ask which of the four gates you are actually negotiating with, because the rest of this guide is a map of those gates and the decisions they make possible.

## What a payout is, is not, and the version boundary

The most common error is treating a payout as the inverse of the donation, because both live in the same fund and both move Plutonium. A donation is a member pushing their own Plutonium into the fund; a payout is the leader returning fund Plutonium to a member. The fund is the middle, and the two directions are different screens, different locks, and different decisions. The [guide to clan treasury donations](/guides/clan-treasury-donations/) covers the inbound side and, before v0.36.3, honestly noted that the open repo did not expose what the clan does with the pooled currency; v0.36.3 closed that gap with the payout. So a payout is not a reversal of a donation: a member who donated Plutonium does not get it back through a payout, and a payout is not a withdrawal right. It is a deliberate, leader-initiated allocation to a member, for whatever reason the leader chooses, and the only structural constraints are the limit regime and the visibility ledger. Because the payout spends the same pool that donations fill, the two actions form a closed loop the leader must manage end to end: a fund that is drained by payouts before it is refilled by donations cannot keep paying, and a fund that is never spent stops giving members a reason to keep donating. That loop is why the payout decision is inseparable from the [caps versus plutonium trade-off](/guides/caps-vs-plutonium/) — the currency you are paying out in is the one a member most often cannot earn back from a match, which is exactly what makes a payout feel like a real reward rather than a token.

A second distinction is between the payout and the in-match transfer, which moves gold or troops between teammates inside a single lobby and resets when the lobby ends. A payout is out of match, persistent, and moves specifically premium Plutonium, not caps and not in-match resources. That matters because the community uses "send" and "donate" loosely, and a payout is neither a lobby mechanic nor a donation reversal. A third, subtler distinction is between the payout and a clan-subscription allocation: paid clan subscriptions award a monthly Plutonium pool to the leadership, and the subscription is a separate purchase with its own tiers; a payout draws from the member-funded pool, not from a subscription grant. Keeping those three things separate — donation, in-match transfer, subscription pool — makes the payout stop being a confusing piece, because once you know it is a leader-only, member-only, visible, out-of-match premium-currency allocation, the decision is a budgeting decision, not a feature puzzle.

The version boundary is part of the decision, because the payout is new and a large share of clan content predates it. The components `ClanSendFundsView` and `ClanFundShared`, the `send_funds` and `payouts` UI strings, and the member-visible ledger all shipped together in v0.36.3, and the current version v0.36.9 changes none of them. The practical consequence is a hard line: if you are on v0.36.3 or newer, the leader has the payout and the whole guide applies to your client; if you are on v0.36.2 or older, there is no send screen, and the "honest gap" the fund guide flagged is real on your version. High-view clan tutorials recorded before v0.36.3 simply cannot document a feature that did not exist at recording time, so a "guide" to payouts that predates v0.36.3 is a red flag, not a source. Within v0.36.3 and above, the verified mechanics are the open-source client's: the leader-only lock, the member-only recipient rule, the self-pay prohibition, the integer input with a live per-member cap, the finality dialog, and the member-visible payout ledger with a `reversedAt` marker.

## The limit regime: caps, membership days, and cooldowns

The payout is gated by a limit regime the Terms of Service describe as set and changeable, and that single phrase is the load-bearing fact: the numbers you read on screen are the current numbers, not permanent constants. There are four limit families, and each one blocks a different kind of payout. The first is a daily cap for the clan, the aggregate amount the fund can send in a day, surfaced to the leader as "The clan has reached its send limit for today." when they try to exceed it. The second is a per-member daily cap, the maximum a single member can receive, surfaced as a live hint "Up to {"{amount}"} for {"{name}"} right now" in the send field. The third is a minimum time in the clan before a member can receive, an eligibility lock on the recipient side, not the sender side. The fourth is a cooldown before a new leader can send, plus a cooldown before a former leader can receive from the clan they led, both anti-fraud delays around leadership transitions. On top of those, account properties such as not being banned apply, which can make a member temporarily ineligible to receive even when the other limits are satisfied.

The practical consequence of the regime is that you cannot plan a payout like a fixed budget. The correct reflex is to read the live per-member hint at the moment you are about to send, because that number is the server's current answer to "how much can this member take right now," and it folds the member's daily cap, their eligibility, and any active cooldown into a single figure. A payout that was fine yesterday can be blocked today by a membership-days lock or a new-leader cooldown, and the refusal is not a bug, it is the regime doing its job. The second consequence is that the per-clan daily cap makes the fund a shared, depleting pool: a leader who pays out to several members on one day draws down the same day budget, so the order and size of multiple same-day sends is a real decision, not a formality. The honest summary is: the limits exist, the client shows you the live number, the operator can change the numbers, and the only safe planning assumption is "read the hint, honor the refusals, and never assume a cap the screen is not currently showing you."

| Limit | What it controls | Where you see it | Who it blocks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Per-clan daily cap | Total the fund can send in a day | "The clan has reached its send limit for today." | The leader, once the day budget is spent |
| Per-member daily cap | Max one member can receive | "Up to {"{amount}"} for {"{name}"} right now" hint | Any member past today's receive ceiling |
| Minimum membership days | Eligibility to receive | A per-member eligibility refusal | Recent members, until the day threshold |
| New-leader cooldown | When a new leader can first send | "Clan fund sends are not yet available." | A newly promoted leader, for a set period |
| Former-leader receive cooldown | When an ex-leader can receive | An eligibility refusal on the recipient | A former leader of the same clan |

## Who can send, who can receive, and the self-pay prohibition

The sender side is narrow and stable: the current clan leader can send, and officers can spend the fund on other clan features where a feature allows, but the Plutonium payout specifically is a leader-only action. When leadership transfers to another member, the fund control transfers with it, so the payout follows the role, not the person who previously held it. The recipient side is broader but locked: a payout goes only to another current member of the clan, never to a non-member and never to the leader themselves. The self-pay prohibition is the single most important constraint on the feature, because it is the structural answer to the obvious abuse — a leader paying themselves out of the fund they were supposed to steward for the clan. The Terms of Service are explicit: the leader can send "only to other current members of the clan, not to themselves," and the client enforces it, so a payout to the leader's own account is not a thing that can happen through the normal path.

Two implications follow from the self-pay prohibition and are worth stating plainly, because the community will try to route around them. First, a leader who wants to enrich themselves with the fund cannot do it through the payout, and the only ways to route around it are the ones the ToS flatly prohibit: selling, trading, or gambling away clan fund Plutonium or clan positions outside the service. Second, the self-pay prohibition is a feature for members, not a limitation to be annoyed by, because it is the guarantee that the leader is not the recipient of the fund they control. For a member deciding whether to keep donating Plutonium into a fund that can now pay out, this is the load-bearing fact: the fund cannot be paid back to the leader, it can only go to current members, and every such transfer is on a ledger the member can read. The donation decision is therefore a decision about whether you trust a leader who spends a fund whose payouts are capped, visible, and structurally unable to flow back to the leader, and those three properties are the entire safety case for the feature.

The operational detail that makes the self-pay prohibition enforceable is that the recipient picker is populated from the current member list with the leader's own account excluded from the payout target list. That means the restriction is not a warning you can dismiss; it is a menu that simply does not offer your own account as a payout destination, which is a stronger guarantee than a confirmation prompt. It also means that a leader who changes their own account details cannot re-enter the recipient list under a different identity, because the list is the clan's current roster, not a free text field. This is the part of the feature that makes the payout safe enough to be trusted with real currency, and it is the part a member should specifically verify has shipped on their version, because the whole "trust the leader" case collapses if the recipient picker ever allowed the leader's own account.

## Visibility and the payout ledger

Every payout is visible to every member of the clan, and the Terms of Service list exactly what that means: who sent, who received, how much, when, and whether it was reversed. The client presents this as a dedicated payout screen with the explainer "Plutonium the leader has sent from the clan fund. Every send is listed here." and an empty state that reads "No payouts yet." Each entry carries a `reversedAt` field, so a reversal is not a deletion but a marked, logged event, and the entry stays in the ledger. This visibility is the second load-bearing safety property of the feature, and it changes the social dynamics of a fund in a way the numeric limits do not. A capped but invisible spend is one thing; a capped but publicly logged spend in a clan that runs a Discord is something a leader thinks twice about, because every member can see the pattern of who is being paid, how much, and how often.

The practical consequence for a member is that the ledger is a trust instrument. When you are deciding whether to keep donating into the fund, the payout screen is the evidence you read: a leader who pays a small, even, broad set of members is operating the fund the way the feature is designed for, and a ledger that is empty for weeks or that shows lopsided, irregular, large sends to a narrow set of members is a signal about the leadership, not a feature malfunction. The practical consequence for a leader is the mirror: you have no private channel, so a payout you are uncomfortable making is a payout you should not make, because it will be seen, and it will be the one members remember. Reversals reinforce this, because when the operator reverses a send, it is logged with a reason and the recipient can ask why, which means even an operator action is shown to the people it affects rather than handled quietly. The single habit this produces is simple: read the ledger before you donate, and as a leader, treat the ledger as an audience and spend accordingly.

There is also a timing consequence of the ledger that is easy to miss: because every send is timestamped and visible, a payout made in a burst right after a leadership change is read differently from the same payout made gradually over weeks. A new leader who sends one large lump sum on day one announces, to every member, exactly how much of the fund they intend to move and how quickly, and members form a durable impression of the leadership's style from that first pattern. A gradual, small, and regular payout history is read as stewardship; a single large payout is read as a test or a transfer. So the ledger does not only record what happened, it frames how the next decision will be judged, which means a leader should spend the way they want the whole clan to perceive the fund being run, not just the way a single send is maximally useful in the moment.

## Failure modes and the counter for each

The payout has only a few ways to fail, and the design already answers most of them; the rest are judgment calls the player owns. The first is the daily-cap refusal, "The clan has reached its send limit for today," which appears when the fund's day budget is exhausted by earlier sends. The counter is to size and order sends deliberately and to read the live per-member hint rather than assume the pool is open, because the cap resets on the operator's schedule, not on yours. The second is the availability refusal, "Clan fund sends are not yet available," which appears when the leader is in the new-leader cooldown or another delay. The counter is patience, not a retry loop, because the cooldown is a deliberate anti-fraud delay and the send will become available once it has elapsed.

The third is the prior-send protection, "A prior send from this page was completed," which guards against double-sending when the connection drops after a submission. The counter is to trust the protection and not re-submit a send you already made, because the design is idempotent in the same spirit as the donation dialog. The fourth failure is the membership-days lock, where a member has not been in the clan long enough to receive, and the counter is to understand that it is an eligibility rule on the recipient side, so you cannot size around it, you can only wait for the member's tenure to qualify. The fifth is the banned-account lock, where the recipient's account eligibility has lapsed and they simply cannot receive until it is restored, which the leader should treat as a temporary condition and not as a reason to route the send through a different account. The sixth is the self-pay attempt, which the client blocks flatly and the ToS prohibit, and the counter is to recognize that there is no legitimate version of a leader paying themselves. The seventh is the routing failure, where a leader and members try to move fund Plutonium to themselves through a non-member account or a third-party arrangement, which the trading prohibition prohibits and which is the most consequential failure, because it is the point where a payout stops being a budgeting tool and becomes a scheme.

Every one of these either resolves to a refusal you should read and honor, or it is a judgment call where the safe answer is to spend less, wait for the lock to clear, and keep the ledger clean. The unifying counter across all of them is the same as the unifying fact: the payout is a capped, visible, leader-only transfer, so the failure that actually hurts you is not the refusal, it is the one you force through by ignoring a refusal, and that is the only failure the design cannot protect you from, because at that point you are no longer using the feature the way it was built to be used.

## Two scenarios: a ranked clan stipend and a new leader's first payout

The first scenario is the durable end of the clan spectrum: a clan with eight named members, a Discord, and a win-loss ratio on the leaderboard you have held across a few hundred games. The members are real, the leader answers when you ask, and the fund has a healthy balance from a few deliberate caps-and-Plutonium donations. The recurring problem the clan wants to solve is not money, it is attendance: the roster thins in the quiet weeks, and the player who shows up most is the one the whole team leans on in ranked lobbies. The leader's decision is whether to use a small recurring payout as an attendance stipend, and the answer the feature supports is yes, small and broad rather than large and narrow. Concretely, assume the fund's daily cap is such that the screen shows enough for a small round of sends. The leader pays each of several active members a modest, roughly equal amount on the day the weekly play block ends, reads the live per-member hint before each send, and honors the "Up to {"{amount}"}" number. The sizing rule that keeps this defensible is proportionality to the proof: a clan that has held a stable ratio for two months can carry a slightly larger stipend than a clan that has held it for two weeks, and the breadth rule is that a stipend paid to five members is a team-retention signal, while a stipend paid to a single member is a transfer the trading prohibition was designed to make awkward. The failure to avoid is the inverse of underpaying: it is using the stipend as a disguised reward to a favored member, because that pattern is exactly what the member-visible ledger exposes and exactly what a donation decision watches for.

The second scenario is the other end: you just became the clan leader, the fund has a balance the previous leader built, and you want to make your first payout to reward the members who carried the roster. The payout is available, but three locks work against your instinct to do something big and immediate. First, a new leader is in the cooldown before they can send, so the very first impulse — a celebratory payout the moment you take the role — is structurally blocked for a delay period. Second, some members may not yet be in the clan long enough to receive, so the membership-days lock narrows who is eligible on your first try. Third, the daily cap is a real ceiling on how much the fund can move today, and it is shared across every member you pay. The correct first payout is the small, eligible, patient one: wait for the new-leader cooldown to clear, send to the members who are eligible right now, and keep the total well under the daily cap so the fund still has room for the coming weeks. The decision to make the first payout small and clean is the feature protecting the transition, and it is the signal that tells every member whether the new leader will steward the fund or spend it.

## Modes and maps: when a payout is the right spend

The payout is mode-independent in the narrow sense that the fund screen is the same in every game mode, but the value of a payout is not, because what the fund can afford and what it should reward both depend on what the clan actually does in match. In a competitive or Masters framework, where the clan is a stable roster chasing a ratio, the payout earns its place as a coordination-and-retention budget: a small recurring stipend keeps the roster whole across quiet weeks, and the members who hold the ranked lobby are the ones holding the ratio, so paying them is paying for the thing the clan actually optimizes. In a casual or FFA-leaning clan, where the same frustration the community keeps raising is that same-flag players out-equip each other and the "point of a clan" is thin, a payout is the wrong spend, because the problem there is not a lack of premium currency, it is a lack of a shared reason to keep playing, and Plutonium does not create that.

Map conditions matter less to the payout itself than to the surplus you can afford to give back, because the payout is an out-of-match transfer and the map never changes the fund screen. What the map changes is the clan's income, and therefore the fund balance and the size of a sustainable recurring payout. On maps and in modes where the clan clears quickly and stacks caps and Plutonium, the fund refills faster and a larger recurring payout is sustainable, so the stipend can be a little bigger without draining the pool. On maps and in modes where the roster dies early and generates little, the surplus is small and the right payout is small too, a modest amount the fund actually has, not a number that drains the pool and forces a pause before the next send. The discipline is to let the clan's real surplus and real goal set the payout, not the mode's noise: pay retention in a competitive clan, do not buy engagement in a casual one, and size the number to the map's income so the fund never dries up between sends. The one habit that separates a sustainable payout program from a draining one is reading the fund balance before each send and treating the balance, not the ambition, as the number that sets the ceiling.

The same mode logic runs the other direction and is worth naming: a payout is most defensible exactly when the clan's in-match goal and the payout's purpose are aligned, and least defensible when they are not. If the clan plays ranked and the payout funds the members who hold the ranked lobby, the spend is buying the thing the clan optimizes, and the ledger will look like a rational budget to anyone who reads it. If the clan plays relaxed FFA and the payout is a way to keep a few favored players engaged while the rest drift, the spend is buying a feeling, and the ledger will look like a transfer to anyone who reads it. So the mode is not a rule that blocks or allows the payout; it is the evidence you use to decide whether the payout is aligned with what the clan is actually trying to do, and the honest test is one question: if I read this ledger as a member, does this spend look like a budget or like a transfer? The answer to that question is the mode and the map deciding, together, whether the payout is the right spend.
