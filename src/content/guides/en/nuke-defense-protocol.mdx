## Direct answer: count the window, then fire at the landing side

When a warhead is already inbound, the decision is not "do I have enough SAMs" but "does my ring actually cover the last 150 tiles of this warhead's flight, and is a slot ready on that side right now." A nuke in v0.34.18 is `targetable` only while it is strictly inside 150 tiles of either its launch point or its destination, and a SAM Launcher can only shoot a `targetable` projectile that also lies inside its own firing range. That means your interceptable window is the final 150 tiles before impact, not the whole arc across the map. At the v0.34.18 flight speeds the window is short: a hydrogen moves at 0.5 tile per tick, so the last 150 tiles take 300 ticks, about five seconds; an atom moves at 1 tile per tick, so the same 150 tiles take only 150 ticks, about two and a half seconds. A MIRV warhead flies at 0.5 tile per tick like a hydrogen, but the warheads arrive spread across the ladder, so you are not defending one window, you are defending a stream of them. The protocol is therefore four steps, in order. First, the moment you see the first warhead, locate the landing zone and ask which of your launchers sits within firing range of a point inside the last 150 tiles of that arc. Second, check that a slot on that launcher is actually ready, because a slot that just fired is reloading for 90 ticks, nine seconds, and is useless for this intercept. Third, if a launcher on the landing side has a ready slot, fire it now, do not wait for a better angle. Fourth, if no launcher covers the landing zone, stop spending launchers on a losing intercept, pull your backline troops away from the impact ring, and preserve your completed Cities, because the hit is going to land and your only question is how much you keep. This guide owns that real-time moment. The [SAM launcher guide](/guides/sam-launchers/) answers the planning question of how many launchers to build; the [MIRV mechanics guide](/guides/mirv/) answers what the weapon is and how the ladder works. Here the warhead is already flying and you are the defender, and the question is what to do in the next few seconds. The flight and range numbers below come from the official v0.34.18 tagged source, not from a forum estimate.

## The flight window you actually have, per warhead

The single most useful number in a nuke-defense game is the length of the interceptable window, and it is different for every warhead type because the nuke flight speed differs. In v0.34.18 the nuke speed function returns 1 tile per tick for an atom and 0.5 tile per tick for both a hydrogen and a MIRV warhead. The targetable range is 150 tiles, defined in the `defaultNukeTargetableRange()` function, and a nuke is `targetable` while it is strictly within 150 tiles of its launch point or of its destination. For a defender the launch-point half of that window is irrelevant, because the warhead left the enemy silo long ago and is already out of range of your launchers. The window that matters is the destination half: the final 150 tiles before the warhead hits your map. Divide that distance by the flight speed and you have your reaction time in ticks. A hydrogen at 0.5 tile per tick needs 300 ticks to cross the last 150 tiles, which is five seconds at sixty ticks per second. An atom at 1 tile per tick needs 150 ticks, about two and a half seconds. A MIRV warhead at 0.5 tile per tick needs the same 300 ticks, five seconds, but only for the warhead it is describing. The numbers in v0.34.18, derived from the speed and targetable-range source, are laid out here so you can read the whole defense at a glance:

| Warhead | Speed (tile/tick) | Last-150-tile window (ticks) | At 60 ticks/s | Intercept note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Atom | 1.0 | 150 | ~2.5 s | Fastest arrival; shortest reaction time, the most dangerous first strike |
| Hydrogen | 0.5 | 300 | ~5.0 s | Single warhead, one ready slot is enough if the ring covers the landing |
| MIRV warhead | 0.5 | 300 | ~5.0 s | Same window per warhead, but warheads arrive staggered, so the window repeats |

The practical consequence is that a hydrogen gives you roughly twice as much time as an atom, which is exactly why an atom is the more dangerous first strike: it arrives fast enough that by the time you read the alert and check your launcher coverage, the window may already be closing. This is the number the community underestimates, because the visible arc of the warhead across the map looks long and slow, but the part of that arc your SAMs can actually touch is only the last 150 tiles. The rest of the flight the warhead renders opaque and is invulnerable, so a launcher sitting far from the landing point is watching a projectile it will never be able to shoot. The rule to internalize is that the warhead becomes a target only when it is inside the final 150 tiles, and your total useful decision time is the 150 divided by the speed, in ticks. When you see the alert you should already know, for each warhead type, how many seconds that division gives you, so you are not doing the arithmetic in real time against a timer.

## Does your ring cover the landing, the geometry check

The second question is whether a launcher on the landing side of your ring is close enough to a targetable point to actually fire, and this is where most "my SAMs did nothing" reports come from. A level-N SAM Launcher has N missile slots and a real firing range given by the formula 150 minus 480 over level plus 5. A level 1 launcher reaches 70 tiles, a level 5 reaches 102, a level 10 reaches 118, and the range approaches 150 as the level climbs but never reaches it. The launcher also has a broad 600-tile search radius, but that number is only for early detection of fast projectiles; it is not the firing range, so a launcher 400 tiles from the landing zone sees the warhead early and still cannot shoot it. The geometry you must check is the triangle between the landing zone, your launcher, and a point inside the last 150 tiles of the flight. The warhead is targetable at any of those last-150-tiles points, but your launcher must be within its own firing range of the specific point where its missile will meet the warhead. If your ring is built tight around the core, every launcher is close to the landing zone and most will be in range of a targetable point, which is the correct defensive placement. If your launchers are spread out to a wide perimeter, some of them are inside the 600-tile search radius, see the warhead, and are still more than 150 tiles from any targetable point on the landing side, so they idle while the warhead lands. The check is a per-launcher test: for the launcher you want to fire, is the landing side of the impact within its firing range? If the ring is built around the core, the answer is yes for most of it. If the ring is built on a wide arc, the answer depends on which direction the strike came from. The single most useful pre-game habit is to place at least one mid-level launcher directly between the core and each side of the map, so that no matter which direction an inbound warhead is aimed from, one launcher is on the landing side and inside its own firing range. That placement converts the geometry from a per-direction gamble into a guaranteed covered landing, and it is the difference between a ring that looks dense on the map and a ring that actually intercepts.

## The real-time decision tree, warhead to fire

Now combine the window and the geometry into the four-step decision you run every time a warhead appears, and do it before the warhead reaches the landing zone. Step one is detection and landing-zone identification. The moment the alert fires, note where the warhead is aimed, because the landing zone is the only point that matters for your geometry check; everything you build toward the launch side is wasted for this intercept. Step two is the coverage check against that landing zone. Ask which of your launchers sits within its own firing range of a point inside the last 150 tiles of the flight toward that landing zone. If the ring is built around the core this is usually two or three launchers, the ones nearest to the impact side. Step three is the ready-slot check on exactly those launchers. A slot that fired on a previous warhead is reloading for 90 ticks, nine seconds, and a launcher whose only slot is reloading cannot intercept this warhead no matter how well placed it is, so the count that matters is not the number of launchers you own but the number of ready slots on the landing side. Step four is the fire or hold decision. If at least one launcher on the landing side has a ready slot inside range, fire it immediately; waiting for a better angle costs ticks you do not have, because the window is only a few seconds long. If no launcher on the landing side has a ready slot inside range, do not fire a launcher from the far side of the map in a panic, because a missile flying from the wrong side at 12 tiles per tick will not reach a targetable point in time, and instead accept the hit, pull your backline troops out of the impact ring, and focus on preserving your completed Cities. The decision tree is fast on purpose. You are not trying to maximize intercepts in the abstract; you are answering one question for this specific warhead, which is whether a ready launcher on the landing side can touch a targetable point before impact, and acting on the yes or no without hesitation. The hesitation is the real cost, because every second you spend checking a second launcher is a second of the window that closes, and on an atom the window is only two and a half seconds long, so the check has to be a reflex, not a calculation.

## MIRV, a stream of windows instead of one

A MIRV changes the problem from a single intercept into a staggered stream of intercepts, and that is the reason it is the hardest defense to run. The MIRV launch cost follows the 10000 divided by 6 plus 2000 times level squared price ladder, and each additional warhead on the same Silo launches with a delay, so the warheads do not arrive at the same moment; they arrive one after another, spread by the ladder delay, and each one has its own 150-tile interceptable window. That means your SAM ring is not being asked to shoot one target at one instant; it is being asked to shoot a sequence of targets, each with its own short window, and the windows overlap in time. A level-10 MIRV is the 350-warhead envelope, and the warheads arrive spread across the flight, so you are defending window one, then window one plus two, then window one plus three, and so on, with your launchers reloading between them. The reload is the critical constraint here. A slot reloads for 90 ticks, nine seconds, and if the MIRV warheads arrive faster than your ready slots reload, you will have a window with a ready launcher and the next window with none, and the second warhead lands. The defense question for a MIRV is therefore not "can I intercept the first warhead" but "can I sustain intercepts across the whole staggered arrival." The practical answer depends on the level of your ring. A ring of high-level launchers, where each launcher has many slots, can keep a pool of ready slots cycling through the staggered windows, because while some slots fire and start reloading, other slots on the same launcher are still ready. A ring of level-1 launchers, one slot each, cannot, because after the first volley every slot is reloading and the later warheads have no ready launcher to intercept them. The rule is that against a MIRV you need slot capacity, not launcher count; a few high-level launchers on the landing side beat many level-1 launchers, because the staggered arrival punishes any launcher whose slots all fire at once. The community answer that matches this is to keep at least two launchers on the landing side at a level high enough that their combined ready slots exceed the number of warheads arriving in any overlapping pair of windows, so that the ring can absorb the stagger instead of being overwhelmed by it.

## Scenario 1, a hydrogen at your core in a one-versus-one

Walk the full protocol against a concrete situation, a one-versus-one where the enemy has a level-1 Hydrogen Silo and fires at your core with no prior warning. Your setup is a ring of three launchers built around the core, two at level 5 and one at level 10, and your backline is holding near the coast. The enemy Silo is on the far side of the map, so the hydrogen has a long arc and arrives at 0.5 tile per tick. The alert fires. First, the landing zone is your core, so the last 150 tiles of the flight are the region right around your core, and your ring built around the core means all three launchers are on the landing side. Second, the coverage check: a level 5 launcher reaches 102 tiles and a level 10 reaches 118, and the launchers are built within roughly 30 to 50 tiles of the core, so each is well inside its firing range of the targetable points in the last 150 tiles around the core. Third, the ready-slot check: the two level 5 launchers have five ready slots each and the level 10 has ten, and none of them has fired yet, so there are twenty ready slots on the landing side. Fourth, the fire decision: a hydrogen is a single warhead, so one ready slot is enough to intercept it, and you fire the level 10 launcher, which has the longest range and the most margin. The missile travels at 12 tiles per tick, the warhead is inside the targetable window for 300 ticks, and the intercept happens well inside that window, so the hydrogen is destroyed before it detonates. The backline stays put, no troops are lost, and the core is unharmed. Now the variant that matters: the same hydrogen, but your ring was built on a wide perimeter arc and the two launchers nearest the impact side are both at level 1 with a single slot each, and one of them fired on an earlier skirmish and is reloading. The coverage check now fails for the reloading launcher, and the other level 1 reaches only 70 tiles, which may not cover the specific targetable point on the landing side if the arc brings the warhead in from the flank. With no ready launcher inside range on the landing side, the correct move is not to panic-fire the far-side launcher, it is to accept the hit, pull the backline out of the 80-tile hydrogen impact ring, and let the blast take the coast while the core and the Cities survive, because the Cities are worth far more than the bare tiles. The difference between the two outcomes is not the number of launchers, it is whether the ring was built around the core with ready slots on the landing side, which is the placement this guide is built to protect.

## Scenario 2, a MIRV volley against a mid-level ring

Now the harder situation, a one-versus-one where the enemy has a level-8 MIRV Silo and fires a staggered volley at your core while you hold a mid-level ring. Your ring is four launchers, two at level 8 and two at level 12, built around the core, and your backline is holding near the coast. The level-8 MIRV launches its warheads with the ladder delay, so they arrive staggered, not together. The first wave arrives and your ring intercepts it, using ready slots on the landing side, and the warheads that hit your 80-tile and 100-tile rings are destroyed before impact. The critical moment is the second wave. While the slots that fired on the first wave are reloading for 90 ticks, the second wave arrives inside the same landing zone, and the question is whether enough ready slots are still cycling. Your level 12 launchers have twelve slots each and your level 8 launchers have eight each, so even after the first volley, several slots on the level 12 launchers are still ready, and those are the ones that take the second wave. The stagger is the design of the problem: the enemy is trying to time the second wave to land in the reload gap of your first volley, and a ring with deep slot capacity on the landing side closes that gap because the higher-level launchers still have ready slots while the lower-level ones are reloading. The variant that fails: the same MIRV volley against a ring of level 1 launchers, twelve of them, one slot each. The first volley uses a dozen ready slots and destroys the first warheads, but now every launcher on the ring is reloading, and the second wave arrives with zero ready slots, so the later warheads land and take the core and the Cities. The lesson is that against a staggered MIRV, slot capacity beats launcher count, and the placement that matters is a couple of high-level launchers on the landing side that keep a ready-slot pool cycling through the staggered windows. If you are defending against a known MIRV and you have reserve Gold, the move before the volley is to upgrade one landing-side launcher to a higher level so that its slot pool can absorb the stagger, because the cost of one upgrade is far less than the cost of a lost core. The backline, in this scenario, should already be pulled out of the 100-tile hydrogen-equivalent ring around the core, because even a well-defended MIRV leaves a few warheads that may punch through, and the troops in the ring are the first thing lost when one does.

## When the defense will fail, the five cases

Most nuke-defense losses come from one of five specific, avoidable cases, and recognizing which one you are in is more useful than trying to remember a formula. Case one is the late build. If you build your launchers after the warhead is already in its opaque, invulnerable flight phase, no launcher in the world can intercept it, because the warhead is not `targetable` while opaque, and the fix is to keep a permanent ring around the core rather than building launchers reactively when the alert fires. Case two is the placement that is far from the landing zone. A launcher built on a wide perimeter sees the warhead inside its 600-tile search radius but is more than its own firing range from the targetable points on the landing side, so it idles; the fix is to place at least one mid-level launcher between the core and each side of the map so the landing side is always covered. Case three is the reloading slot. A launcher whose slots all fired on the previous warhead is reloading for 90 ticks and is useless for this one; the fix is to keep a ready-slot pool by using high-level launchers with many slots, so that some slots are always ready even after a volley. Case four is the staggered MIRV against a thin ring. A volley of level-1 launchers, one slot each, fires its whole pool on the first wave and has nothing left for the staggered later warheads; the fix is slot capacity on the landing side, not more single-slot launchers. Case five is the economic mismatch. If the enemy's nuke program is funded from a much larger economy than yours, you cannot match their launch frequency with a ring you keep rebuilding, and the correct response is not a bigger ring, it is to deny the launch itself by destroying or capturing the enemy Silo, because every Silo you remove removes an entire source of inbound warheads and no amount of SAM coverage fully replaces that. The pattern behind all five is the same, the defense fails when a launcher on the landing side is either absent, out of range, or out of ready slots at the moment the warhead needs intercepting, and the pattern is fixable in every case by the specific placement and capacity the rest of the guide describes. The community threads that say "my SAMs do nothing" almost always resolve to one of these five, and the diagnostic is to run one hydrogen test against your own ring and watch which case is the one losing, because the test takes thirty seconds and tells you exactly which of the five to fix before a real match.

## What to do in the seconds you lose, the fallback

When the geometry check says no launcher covers the landing zone, you are going to take the hit, and the question becomes how to lose it as well as possible, which is a real decision and not just a resignation. The first move is to pull your backline troops out of the impact ring before the warhead lands. A hydrogen's outer damage ring is 100 tiles, so troops standing within 100 tiles of the core are inside the blast and will be destroyed along with the tiles; moving them to more than 100 tiles, or across water, keeps the force alive even though the land is gone. The second move is to preserve your completed Cities, because each completed City level adds 250,000 to your maximum troops, and a level-3 City is worth 750,000 of your cap, so the Cities are the highest-value target to keep alive and the bare tiles are the lowest-value target to sacrifice. The third move is to not spend your last ready launcher on a losing intercept, because a missile fired from the wrong side at 12 tiles per tick will not reach a targetable point in time and will just be wasted, and that launcher's slot is now reloading for 90 ticks when you may need it for the next warhead. The fourth move is to check whether the incoming warhead is the last one the enemy can afford. If the enemy just spent their reserve on a hydrogen, their Silo may be empty and their Gold low, which means the threat is a one-shot event and your recovery, not your intercept, is now the priority; the [post-MIRV recovery guide](/guides/post-mirv-recovery/) covers exactly how to preserve the cities and time a bounded counterattack after a nuclear landing. The fifth move is to plan the counter-strike before the dust settles, because a nuke that took your coast has also removed your front line, and the enemy is now extended, which is the moment to hit their flank with the force you pulled back. The fallback is not failure, it is the correct branch of the decision tree for the case where the intercept is not possible, and treating it as a resignation instead of a plan is how a defended nuke loss becomes a game loss. The rule is that the moment the geometry check says no, switch from "how do I stop this" to "how do I keep the Cities and the force alive and hit back," because the warhead is already going to land and the only things left to decide are which tiles you surrender and when you punish the overextension.

## Mode and map adjustments, where the numbers shift

The same protocol changes in a few specific ways depending on the mode and the map, and the shifts are large enough that copying a one-versus-one answer into a different setup is a real mistake. In one-versus-one the map is small and the enemy Silo is far, so the flight arc is long and the last-150-tiles window is the whole window you have to defend, which is why the ring-built-around-the-core placement works so cleanly. In a Nations or team mode the map is larger and there are more players with nukes, so you can be hit from multiple directions at once, and the single-landing-side check becomes a multi-direction check, which is why the placement of a mid-level launcher between the core and each side of the map, not just one side, becomes mandatory. In a team mode the SAM ring is a shared budget, and the "how many launchers" question is answered per team rather than per player, which means the combined launcher count and the combined ready-slot pool are what matter, so the team should concentrate its launchers on the shared core rather than spreading one launcher per player around the edge. Map size matters for the flight window in a specific way: a larger map means a longer arc from the enemy Silo to your core, which means the warhead spends more of its flight outside the targetable 150-tile zone, so the useful decision window is the same 150 divided by speed, but the time you have before the alert fires is longer, which is actually helpful, because you have more time to reposition the backline and pre-place a landing-side launcher before the warhead enters the final 150 tiles. The difficulty setting shifts the troop growth and the nuke damage, so on a higher difficulty the impact ring is more punishing and the backline should be pulled further out than the 100-tile hydrogen ring to stay safe. The one rule that does not change across any mode or map is that the warhead is only `targetable` inside the last 150 tiles, and that your total useful decision time is that 150 divided by the flight speed in ticks, so whatever the setup, the moment the alert fires you already know how many seconds you have, and the rest of the protocol, the coverage check, the ready-slot check, and the fire-or-hold decision, is built on that single number. The mode and map change the geometry and the budget; they do not change the window, and the window is the number the whole defense is built on.
