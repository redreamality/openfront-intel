## Direct answer: membership is one team, not a league

Joining a clan in OpenFront is not entering a league, a ranked pool, or a subscription. It is
one concrete, version-stable change plus one newly shipped surface, and everything else in this
guide is attached to those two facts. The version-stable change is that in every team mode the
engine groups players by clan tag and assigns every member of your clan to the same team, so
your clan-mates become your automatic teammates the moment you are inside a clan. The newly
shipped surface is the v0.35.0-beta2 clan browser (PR #5945), which lets you search, sort and
filter every public clan before you join, and it is the first time in the game's life that the
"how do I even find a clan" question has an in-game answer instead of the old default, which
was that a friend had to tell you a tag to type in. Keep those two facts in front of every
other detail, because together they explain nearly every question a new member asks. You are
not locking yourself into a competition format; you are opting into a shared team-identity that
changes who your teammates are and gives you a handle that other players can find in the
browser. That is why the rest of the guide exists: to make the decision deliberate rather than
reflexive. You should join a clan only when you can name the other members, when you understand
exactly what power an officer holds over your account, and when the browser columns you are
looking at are actually measuring the thing you care about, because a clan's win-rate number is
a ratio, not a total, and a "full" clan is capped at ten members and cannot take you at all. If
you can hold those three guards in your head before you press anything, you have already avoided
the four most common mistakes new members make, and the remainder of the guide only adds the
numbers and the boundaries that let you make the call with confidence instead of on the vibe of
a tag you do not understand. The framing also matters for one more reason: the browser surface
is a beta2 addition, so the "find a clan" half of this guide is the part that is new in
v0.35.0-beta2 and the "what does being inside change" half is the part that has been true the
whole time, and keeping that split in mind stops you from attributing the auto-team behavior to
the browser release when it is actually the older, stable rule that the browser only makes
easier to act on.

## What a clan actually is: a tag, a name, and a hard cap

The cleanest way to stop guessing is to separate the three things that are all true of a clan
at once, because the community blends them together and every blend is a small misunderstanding
that compounds. The first is the tag, which is the identity the game stores, shows on your
profile, and uses to run the auto-team rule. It is validated by a schema that allows two to
five ASCII letters or digits, minimum two characters, maximum five, so `MOB`, `x9`, `TEAM1`
and `openfront` are all legal tags while a tag with a space, an underscore, or a sixth letter
is rejected outright. This is the single most misunderstood fact in the community: the letters
in a tag are free text that the founder typed, not a rank, not a tier, and not a country code.
The recurring thread asking "what do all the [MOL]s mean" gets the same answer every single
time, which is that there is no official meaning, so do not read status into a tag you are
about to join, because the tag is an identity, not a badge. The second is the display name,
which is separate from the tag and human-readable; it is the string you see in the browser,
while the tag is the hidden key the engine actually uses to group teams, so two clans can look
alike in the list but be keyed by completely different tags. The third is the member limit, a
hard number: a clan holds at most ten members, and once it is at ten the join is refused until
a seat opens. That cap is the number that turns "I can join this clan" into a decision with a
real deadline, because the clan you want can fill up in the gap between the moment you look at
it and the moment you press join, and nothing in the UI warns you that the seat you were eyeing
is the last one. If you can hold the tag, the name, and the ten-seat cap in your head, you can
read the whole clan screen correctly. One more practical note on the tag, because it bites
later: the tag is editable by an officer, which means the handle other players use to find you
in the browser can change out from under you mid-clan, so a tag you like is not a permanent
identity the game guarantees, it is whatever the current leader decides to type in the two to
five characters the schema allows.

## How the v0.35.0-beta2 browser works: sorts and filters

The browser is the new decision surface, and it is the only part of this guide that is
beta2-specific, so treat every sort and filter described here as a v0.35.0-beta2 fact, not
something that exists on the stable v34 line the site's data snapshot currently reflects. It
lists the public clans and lets you reorder them by one of several sort orders and narrow them
with one of three filters. The sort orders are: random, by member count, by win score, by
oldest, by richest, and by lowest win score. The filters are: all clans, open clans only, and
invite-only clans only. Two of the columns most change your decision, the win-score column and
the treasury column, and both are easy to misread if you assume they measure what you think
they measure. The win-score column is a ratio, computed as wins over the sum of wins and
losses, and it is zero when the clan has played no games at all. That means a brand-new clan
with a single win outranks a veteran clan that has split ninety wins and ninety losses, which
is exactly why the "lowest win score" order exists as its own sort: it is the surface for
finding a clan with a real, visible record of losing, and that is precisely where a new player
adds the most value and is most likely to be approved. The "richest" sort ranks by treasury
balance, the pooled caps and plutonium the clan has accumulated, so it is the surface for
finding a clan invested enough to actually fund its members rather than a clan that is all talk
and no balance. The table below keeps the two columns that matter, the win-rate column and the
treasury column, side by side with what each one is actually measuring and the misread to
avoid, because reading the column wrong is the whole reason a join that looked good on the
surface turns into a regret. It is also worth pausing on the "random" order, because its
presence is the tell that the browser is a listing, not a recommendation engine: the game does
not decide for you which clan is best, it hands you six re-sorting keys and three filters and
leaves the judgment to you, which is why the columns in the table are the only part of the
screen you can actually trust to rank anything at all, and the random order is the part you
should never use when making a real decision.

| Column | What it measures | How it is computed | Easy misread |
|---|---|---|---|
| Win score | Win ratio | wins / (wins + losses), 0 if none | Reads a ratio as a total; a 1–0 clan beats 90–90 |
| Treasury | Pooled value | soft caps + hard plutonium balance | Reads "rich" as "active"; the balance can sit idle |
| Members | Headcount | 0–10, hard-capped at 10 | Ignores that 10/10 means you cannot join at all |

## Open versus invite-only: the real difference in how you get in

This is the filter that changes the mechanics of joining, so it deserves its own decision rule
rather than being folded into the browser description. An open clan shows a join button that
adds you directly the moment you press it, with no review and no waiting; the clan is telling
you out loud that it wants players and it will take you the instant you ask, so the cost of a
bad fit is low and reversible, you can simply leave. An invite-only clan shows a request button
instead, and pressing it does not make you a member: it puts you into a pending state where the
button flips to a "request pending" label, and the request now has to be approved by a clan
officer or the leader before you are actually inside. Re-requesting while you are pending is
blocked, so you cannot spam the button to get attention, which is a small but real constraint
on how you ask. Two consequences follow that are not obvious from the surface. First, the
invite-only filter is the honest surface for finding competitive clans, because a clan that
gates itself behind an approval step is usually doing so because it genuinely cares about the
people it lets in, which means a pending request from a brand-new player is a screening, not a
formality, and the officers are deciding whether your record and your presence are worth a
seat. Second, the open filter is the honest surface for a player who just wants to stop playing
alone and does not care about a ladder at all, because an open clan takes you without a review
and the downside of a wrong match is a leave button. The practical rule that falls out of both:
if you are still testing whether clan play suits you at all, filter to open, join one, and
leave after a few matches if it does not sit right; and if you have already proven the concept
and want a real squad, filter to invite-only and send the request to the clan whose member
list you can actually name and evaluate before you commit. One detail that keeps new members
from fumbling the flow: the difference is a button, not a toggle, so you cannot switch an open
clan into an invite-only request or vice versa from your side, the clan's setting decides
which button you see, and if you see the request button the only lever you have is choosing a
different clan, which is why the filter row at the top of the browser is the fastest way to
compare the two joining experiences side by side before you spend a request on the wrong one.

## Auto-team: what membership changes inside your matches

The auto-team rule is the version-stable core of what membership is, and it is the source of
the single most common "did I break something" report a new member files. In team modes the
engine assigns teams by clan tag: every member of your clan is placed on the same team, so your
clan-mates show up as your teammates whether you wanted them to or not. What players misread as
a bug is the visible symptom, which is that you open a match and the whole clan is stacked on
one side of the map, and the instinct is to assume the matchmaking broke. It is not a bug and it
is not a report you can file; it is the feature working exactly as designed. The important
boundary, and the one the community keeps asking in every new-member thread, is where the rule
applies and where it does not. It applies in public team modes and in the ranked Clan Wars mode,
where the whole point is for the clan to compete as a single unit, and that is the reason the
ranked mode exists at all, which is covered in the dedicated [Clan Wars guide](/guides/clan-wars/).
It does not ban you from FFA: being in a clan does not stop you from playing free-for-all, so
the "does joining a clan force me to stop playing FFA" question has a flat, version-stable no.
And it does not apply to private custom lobbies, where you can pick your own side, which is
exactly why the auto-team rule is felt most in the public modes and least anywhere you control
the setup. There is one edge that does matter and that you should keep in mind: if your team is
already at capacity when the engine runs the assignment, the overflow member is dropped from
the team rather than splitting the clan across both sides, so a large clan in a small-lobby
mode can lose one of its own to the opposing team. That is the one case where being in a clan
can put you on the opposite side of a clan-mate, and it is the one to remember specifically in
the ranked mode, where the team size is fixed and the clan may not all fit. The rule also has a
quiet consequence that the community rarely asks about but that shapes the feel of a match:
because the engine keys the assignment on the tag rather than on any individual profile, two
players in the same clan who have never spoken to each other end up on the same team as
strangers, which is why a clan is a social contract before it is a competitive one, you are
agreeing to be paired with people you cannot individually vet, and that is the real cost of
the automatic teammate that the benefit, never being alone on a team, does not cancel out.

## Roles: what an officer can do to you, and what you can do back

A clan is not a flat group of ten; it has a permission structure with three roles, leader,
officer, and member, and the power over your account is concentrated in the top two. The leader
holds every officer power plus the two things no officer can do, which are transferring
leadership to another member and dissolving the clan entirely. An officer can kick any member,
ban a member from the clan outright, promote a member to officer, and edit the clan tag and the
display name. A plain member can do none of those things to anyone else; a member's only
self-service is to leave, and leaving is always available and does not require anyone's
permission, so the exit door is never locked from the inside. The ban state is the one that
matters most for your account, because a banned player cannot rejoin the clan and the only path
back is for an officer to unban you from the member list, which means a falling-out with an
officer carries a real, officer-controlled cost that you cannot undo yourself. This is the fact
that should make you cautious before you join any invite-only clan: the approval you are
requesting travels through the same channel that can later kick or ban you, so the clan you are
asking to take you is also the one holding the only undo on a bad relationship, and that
asymmetry is the whole reason to look at the people before the columns. A small but important
clarification for the power table: the officer permissions are not a flat list you can cherry
pick, a single officer holds the kick, ban, promote and edit powers together, so the person
holding the ban on your account is the same person who can kick you the moment before, which
means the risk is not a feature you opt into piece by piece, it is one officer standing at
three levers at once, and reading the member list as a stack of anonymous handles is the
fastest way to underestimate exactly how concentrated that control is. The practical read of
the role structure is a simple filter. If a clan's member list shows a clear leader and a
handful of officers and you can map those handles to people you have actually spoken to, the
power is legible and you can judge the officer risk before you commit. If the member list is
anonymous handles you cannot connect to anyone you know, the power is opaque, the officer risk
is higher than the surface suggests, and the sensible move is to pick the open, low-commitment
clan first and earn a legible position before you trust an officer with a ban on your account.

## Reading the numbers: two concrete situations with the math

The browser hands you numbers, so the guide's job is to make two of them concrete instead of
decorative, because the ratio rule is abstract until you run it against a real comparison.
Situation one: you are comparing two clans in the "lowest win score" sort and you want to join
the one that actually needs you. Clan A shows a win score of 0.25 and Clan B shows 0.40. Both
are ranked by ratio, so Clan A has lost three out of every four matches and Clan B has lost
six out of ten. If your own record is above 0.40, you lift Clan A's average more than you lift
Clan B's, so the join that adds the most value, and therefore carries the most reason for the
officers to approve a pending request, is Clan A. If your own record is below both, joining
either clan is a drag on the ratio, and you are better off improving your own wins first,
because a pending request that reads as a liability is the one that sits unapproved. Situation
two: you are comparing a new clan and a veteran clan and the win-score column is doing exactly
the thing the ratio warning predicts. The new clan is 1–0, which computes to a win score of
1.00, and the veteran clan is 90–90, which computes to a win score of 0.50. The column ranks
the new clan on top because its ratio is higher, not because it is the better clan, so the top
of the "win score" sort is not a leaderboard of the best clans, it is a leaderboard that
rewards a small sample. Reading the ratio the way the column actually computes it, you now know
to cross-check the "richest" sort and the member list before you treat a 1.00 as a badge of
quality rather than what it is, a one-game fluke. Keep the ratio rule in front of both
situations and you will never misread the column again: the number is always wins over
wins-plus-losses, it is zero when there is nothing to divide, and every read of the column has
to pass that test before you act on it. And the same discipline applies to the headcount
column, which is the only one of the three that is not a ratio at all: ten is ten, a full clan
is a hard stop, and no amount of a flattering win score changes the fact that you cannot join
a clan that is already at its cap, so the headcount check is the one column you should read
first, before the ratio, because it is the only column that can say no to you regardless of
how good the rest of the row looks.

## Decision framework and the failures to avoid

The whole membership decision resolves to one question you can answer straight from the browser
screen: do I know who I am joining, and is the column I am looking at measuring the thing I
care about? If you can name the members, you can judge the officer risk, which is the
version-stable reason a clan is worth it at all, because the value of a clan is the automatic
teammates it hands you, not any format it locks you into. If you cannot name the members and
the only column you are reading is the win score, you are making the decision on a ratio that
rewards a small sample, and you should slow down and look at the member list and the treasury
balance instead, because those two are the columns that actually predict whether the clan will
outlive your patience. The four failures to avoid map one-to-one onto the four facts above.
First, do not read status into the tag, it is two to five free characters with no official
meaning, so the letters tell you nothing about quality. Second, do not treat a full clan as
joinable, ten members is the hard cap and a full clan will refuse you until a seat opens, so
check the headcount column before you assume there is a slot. Third, do not assume invite-only
is just a formality, the pending request is a screening by the same officer who can later kick
or ban you, so the approval channel is also the punishment channel. Fourth, do not mistake the
auto-team stacking for a bug, it is the feature in public team modes and it does not apply to
FFA or to private lobbies, so the "whole clan on one side" view is correct behavior. Join when
the clan is open or invite-only by your own choice, the members are legible, the treasury is
invested enough to fund its members if funding matters to you, and the win-score ratio you are
reading genuinely means what you think it means. The treasury is where your money can go once
you are inside, and it is covered separately at the [Clan Treasury guide](/guides/clan-treasury-donations/)
so you can make the donation decision with the same deliberateness you used to make the join
decision. That is the whole loop: find it, read the columns honestly, name the people, join,
and donate only after you know the clan will outlive your patience. The one last habit worth
building is to keep the version label in view: the browser sorts and the open versus
invite-only filter you are using right now are beta2 surfaces, so if you hand this page to a
new player who is still on the stable line, the browser half of the advice does not apply to
their screen yet, and only the tag, the ten seat cap, the auto team rule and the role table
are the parts they can trust on any version, which is why the split between the stable core
and the beta2 surface is the single most useful thing a returning player can pass along to the
next one joining the clan.
