**Short answer.** Dropping a nuke that slices enemy territory into a piece no longer connected to
their main body can hand that cut-off piece to a surrounding enemy, instead of the owner keeping it.
It only fires when the piece has no coast of its own, borders only other players and fallout, and the
cut-off player has other land left. It is a new v0.35.0-beta rule (PR #5751) and is **not** in the
current stable v0.34.25, so on live stable servers it still behaves the old way.

## The rule in one picture

Think of an enemy's territory as a single blob of land. Most of it is the main body: the largest
connected cluster that also touches their border. Now you drop a nuke. The crater erases a ring of
their tiles, and the fallout that follows is treated as your land for connectivity purposes for a
short window after the strike. If that crater plus fallout happens to slice the blob in two, one side
stays attached to the main body and the other becomes a free-floating island of their land with no
path back to it. That floating piece is what the engine calls the severed cluster, and the new rule
decides what happens to it. Before v0.35, the engine's old enclosure logic rarely treated a nuke-cut
arm of a coastal empire as annexable, which is exactly why players complained for a long time that
nuking to cut land off "looked random" and "stopped working for coastal empires." Pull request #5751
reworked the capture path so that a genuinely cut-off, landlocked piece is now claimed by the enemy
that surrounds it, instead of being left to the original owner.

The decision this unlocks is a real return-on-investment call, not a passive bonus. A single nuke
that would only destroy a few buildings can now also transfer an entire district, sometimes dozens of
production tiles, to a third faction or to you. That changes which strikes are worth the gold. A
strike aimed at the thickest part of an enemy's territory may cut nothing at all, while a strike that
lands on the narrow isthmus connecting their coast to their inland heart can both crater that
connection and detach a chunk that a bordering enemy then swallows. You are no longer just buying
damage with the nuke; you are buying a territorial transfer on top of the damage. That is why the
feature matters even though it only triggers in a narrow set of board geometries: the geometry is
precisely the geometry a thoughtful endgame nuke already tries to hit. This is also why the feature
changed how experienced players evaluate a nuclear strike in the first place. The nuke is no longer
a pure damage purchase where you subtract the crater cost from the buildings destroyed. It is now a
compound investment, where the same gold can simultaneously buy the crater and the transfer, and the
transfer is usually the larger of the two. Because the payoff is conditional and geometry-dependent,
the difference between a winning strike and a dud is no longer your aim alone but your read of the
board geometry. The rest of this guide walks through the exact conditions, the capture logic, the
version boundary, and how to read the map so you can set up the cut deliberately instead of
discovering it by accident, and it ends with a quick reference table you can run before every strike.

## The three conditions that must all hold

The engine does not annex a cut-off piece just because it looks disconnected on the map. Three
independent checks must all pass at the same time, and if any one of them fails the piece is left
entirely alone and the owner keeps it. The first condition is disconnection from the main body. The
cluster must not be connected, through the owner's own border tiles, to their largest landmass. A
piece that still touches the main body by even a single tile is not severed and is ignored by the
annex path. This is why a nuke that merely damages the edge of a blob, without fully clearing the
connecting isthmus, does nothing at all: the connection survives, so nothing is actually cut off. The
second condition is that the piece has no coast and no map edge on its own. If the cut-off piece
touches open water or the border of the map by itself, the owner can simply boat out of it or treat
it as a self-sustaining enclave, so the engine refuses to annex it. This is the single most common
reason a cut appears to "not work" in real games: the detached arm still has its own coastline. The
third condition is that the piece borders only other players and fallout, and that at least one enemy
and at least one fallout tile surround it. The piece must be fully ringed by fallout, which counts as
the nuker's land, and by enemy territory, with no open unclaimed tiles touching it at all. If any
tile of the piece opens onto empty no-man's-land, the annex is skipped, because there is no
surrounding enemy that could claim it.

The practical takeaway is that you need a landlocked pocket of enemy land, fully surrounded by your
fallout and a rival's border, that has completely lost its road home. A nuke that carves a ring of
fallout around a district while the district's only land bridge back to the owner sits inside that
ring is the textbook setup that satisfies all three conditions at once. Note the order in which
players usually fail these checks. First they get the disconnection right but the piece still has a
coast. Then they handle the coast but the piece still touches the main body by a single tile. Then
they clear both of those but a gap of open land breaks the "borders only players and fallout" rule.
Each failure mode is visible on the map before you spend a single unit of gold, which is what makes
the whole rule plan-able rather than lucky. The failures section later breaks down each of these with
concrete counterplay and the situations where you should avoid the strike entirely, but the core idea
is that the rule is precise and deterministic, and a single failed condition means the strike is
crater damage and nothing more.

## Which enemy actually captures the piece

When all three conditions hold, the cut-off piece does not go to a random player and it does not
necessarily go to you, the player who dropped the nuke. It goes to the capturing player, which the
engine computes by scanning every bordering tile of the severed cluster and counting, for each enemy,
how many of the piece's tiles that enemy actually borders. Any faction that is friendly to the owner
of the cut-off land is skipped, as is the nuker's own friendly circle, so the pool of candidates is
strictly the hostile factions that share a border with the piece. The enemy that borders the most of
the piece's tiles wins the capture outright. If two enemies are exactly tied, the engine keeps the
first one it encounters in its internal iteration order, so a tie is effectively decided by map
layout rather than by any player action you can influence in the moment. This has a subtle but very
important consequence: nuking to cut an arm off can be a third-party gift. If the detached district
is ringed more by the player to your north than by you, the north player swallows the district and
you get the crater damage without the land. You paid the full gold cost of the nuke for the damage
only, and a rival collected the transfer.

There is also a special case the code handles on its own, and it is the highest-stakes version of
the entire rule. If the severed cluster is the only land the cut-off player still has, the capture is
not a tile transfer at all but a full conquest: the whole player is eliminated and their remaining
assets go to the capturing enemy. This is the variant most likely to end a game early in the endgame,
because once a faction has been reduced to a single capital district, a nuke that isolates that
capital can delete them entirely rather than just shrink them. Because the capturing player is
whichever enemy borders the piece most, you must not assume that being the nuker makes you the
beneficiary of the annex. Before you commit the gold, work out who actually surrounds the target
pocket. In a three-faction endgame that is often the player who has been grinding the target's flank
for the whole match, not the player who is about to spend the nuke. If the intended beneficiary turns
out to be a rival, you have just bought their win. That reordering of incentives, where the person
who pays for the strike is not the person who collects the land, is exactly the thing the two
scenario sections put concrete numbers around, and it is the single most expensive mistake the rule
can produce if you skip the border-count check.

## Version boundary: stable v34 versus the v35 playtest

The single most important operational fact about this feature is that it is not live on the current
stable servers. The rule was introduced by pull request #5751, which landed in the v0.35.0-beta1
engine tag and is also present in the v0.35.0-beta2 tag, but the stable build that the public servers
run is still v0.34.25, and that build does not contain the annex-severed-clusters code path at all.
On stable v0.34.25 the engine still uses the old enclosure logic, where a nuke that cuts an arm off a
coastal main body almost never triggers an annex, which is precisely the long-standing player
complaint that nuking to cut territory off "stopped working for coastal empires." So if you are
playing on the live public servers today, you will see the old behavior: the crater appears, the arm
is cut visually on the map, but the owner keeps every tile and nothing transfers. The cut looks
dramatic and the transfer simply never happens, because the code path that would produce the transfer
does not exist in the stable build.

The v0.35.0-beta1 and v0.35.0-beta2 builds are prerelease, opt-in playtest builds that began
circulating in mid-October 2026. The v0.35.0-beta2 release, published on 2026-10-14, calls out
"Nukes annex again" among its headline changes. To actually experience the new annex rule you need to
be running a v35 beta build, or a custom server on that engine, and not the stable default. This
matters for every other piece of advice in this guide, because all of the deliberate "cut the
isthmus" strategies only pay off on v35 beta and later. If you plan around the annex payoff and then
end up playing on stable, you will spend the nuke gold for the crater damage and get no territorial
transfer, because the stable build has no annex path to fire at all. Treat the version boundary as a
hard precondition for the whole strategy, not a footnote. If you can choose your build, target a v35
beta to test the annex behavior; if you are locked to stable, this section tells you what will not
happen so you do not misread a failed transfer as a bug or a random miss. Once v0.35 ships as the
stable release, the boundary collapses and every strategy in this guide goes live everywhere at once,
which is the moment the endgame meta is most likely to shift hard.

## Reading the map before you commit

Turning the three conditions into a pre-strike checklist makes the difference between an accidental
bonus and a reliable one, and each check is a simple yes or no read you can do before spending gold.
First, find the isthmus: the narrowest band of the owner's land that connects the target district
back to their main body. You want a nuke whose crater, plus the fallout ring it leaves behind, can
fully clear that band. Because fallout counts as your land for connectivity, the effective cut is
wider than the crater alone. A nuke that would not look big enough to bridge the gap by its raw blast
can still sever the connection once the fallout ring closes around it. If the isthmus is wider than
your crater plus fallout can clear, disconnection fails and nothing happens, so skip that target and
look elsewhere. Second, check the coast on the cut-off side. Trace the outline of the district you
are trying to detach and confirm it has no water and no map border on its own. If the district you
want to gift to a rival has its own shoreline, it is unannexable no matter how cleanly you cut it, and
you are far better off targeting the district's landlocked inner half instead of the coastal shell.
Third, verify the surround. The detached piece must be ringed entirely by fallout and enemy border
with zero open tiles touching it. Any gap of open no-man's-land breaks the "borders only players and
fallout" rule and kills the annex, so the cut needs to sit deep inside enemy territory, not on the
fringe of unclaimed land where a stray gap is likely.

Do all three checks before you spend a single unit of gold, because a single no means the strike is
crater damage only and the transfer is impossible. Then, and only then, do the fourth check, which is
the border count that decides who benefits. Work out which hostile faction borders the prospective
detached piece most, because that is the capturing player, and confirm that it is you rather than a
rival. If a third faction would collect the land, the strike is a gift to them and you should either
reposition to gain border first or pass entirely. The two scenario sections that follow apply these
exact reads to two concrete board states, so you can see the thresholds, the tile counts, and the
gold-per-tick numbers that separate a real transfer from a dud. The skill in miniature is: read the
isthmus, read the coast, read the surround, count the border, and only then decide whether the crater
damage is worth the territorial transfer, with the version boundary as the zeroth check that gates
everything else in this entire guide.

## Scenario A: cutting a coastal arm off a mid-game empire

Set the board for a concrete mid-game case. Three factions on a standard island map. You are at
about sixty percent population. The target enemy owns a long coastal strip on the east side that
connects to their inland capital by a two-tile-wide isthmus. Their east strip has its own coastline,
which is the trap that catches most players. If you nuke the isthmus and detach the whole east
strip, the strip still touches the sea, so the "no coast" condition fails and the owner keeps every
tile of it. That is the mistake to avoid. Instead, look at the inland pocket behind the isthmus. It
is a cluster of roughly eighteen farmland tiles that the east strip protects, and it is fully
landlocked. It is bordered on its north and south by your own territory and on its west by the
isthmus itself. A MIRV that bursts directly on that isthmus produces a crater and a fallout ring that
is wide enough to clear the two-tile connection. After the detonation the inland pocket is
disconnected from the main body, has no coast of its own, and is ringed by your fallout and the
territory of the factions around it, so the three conditions all hold at once.

Now count the actual value. The pocket is about eighteen production tiles, which at a mid-game tile
income of roughly half a gold per tile per tick is worth about nine gold per tick transferred. That
swing dwarfs the value of the few buildings the crater actually destroyed, so the crater damage is a
net loss you are accepting in order to buy the transfer. If you are the faction that borders the
pocket most, you annex it and collect those nine gold per tick. If the faction to the north borders
it more than you do, they annex it and you paid the gold for nothing. The decision is therefore
conditional. If you can confirm on the map that the north faction does not border the pocket more
than you do, the strike is a clear net win and you commit the nuke. If you cannot confirm that, the
strike buys the north player an eighteen-tile district, and your only sane options are to reposition
so that you border the pocket first, or to pass on the strike this round and wait for a better
geometry. This is the whole skill compressed into one example: read the isthmus, read the coast, read
the surround, count the tiles, and only then decide whether the crater damage is worth the transfer.
The numbers, not the drama of the crater, are what make the decision.

## Scenario B: the endgame capital that is the last land

Now the high-stakes version of the same mechanic, where the special case of a full conquest becomes
the center of the board. It is the endgame. Two factions are still alive plus a third that is nearly
dead. The surviving target has been reduced to a single twenty-five-tile capital district, and that
district is literally their only remaining land. You have a nuke ready. The third faction borders the
capital on two of its four sides, and you border it on the other two. The target holds one small
outlying farm on a single tile of open road that is the only thing keeping the capital from being
fully surrounded. If you nuke that one road tile, and the crater plus the fallout ring clears the
road, the capital is now disconnected from any other land of the owner's, has no coast of its own,
and is ringed by fallout plus both the third faction and you. All three conditions hold at once.

But because this cluster is the target's only remaining land, the engine does not just transfer
twenty-five tiles. It is a full conquest, and the target is eliminated outright by the capturing
player. Here the capturing-player rule decides the entire game. You and the third faction both border
the capital, so you must work out who borders more of its twenty-five tiles before you fire. If you
border fourteen of those tiles and the third faction borders eleven, you conquer the target and win
the game. If the third faction borders fourteen and you border eleven, you have just dropped an
expensive nuke to win the game for the rival you were about to face. The numbers that matter are the
border counts, not the crater size and not the damage. The decision framework is strict: only commit
if you can confirm that you strictly border the capital more than the third faction does, and that
the cut fully isolates the capital with no coast and no open tile left touching it. If you are unsure
of the exact border count, the safer play is often to not nuke the road at all, but to keep the
target alive as a shared third front that bleeds the other faction, because eliminating them with the
conquest going to the wrong player is the worst available outcome on the board. This scenario is the
one most likely to end a game instantly, which is why the pre-strike arithmetic here matters more than
it does anywhere else in this guide.

## Failures and counterplay

Most failed annexes trace back to one of four failure modes, and each one is visible on the map
before you spend a single unit of gold, so the goal is to catch the failure in the planning phase
rather than after the strike. The first failure mode is that the connection survives. Your crater plus
fallout did not fully clear the isthmus, so a single tile still links the piece back to the main body
and the disconnection check fails. The counter is to aim the burst at the narrowest point of the
isthmus and confirm, before you fire, that the fallout ring will be wider than the remaining gap. If
it will not be wider, do not fire and look for a different target. The second failure mode is that
the piece keeps a coast. You cut the district but it retains its own shoreline, so the no-coast
condition fails and the owner keeps the land, still able to boat out of it. The counter is to target
the landlocked inner half of the district rather than the coastal shell, or to accept that this
particular district is unannexable and pick a different pocket that has no coast of its own.

The third failure mode is that open land breaks the surround. A gap of no-man's-land touches the
piece, so it does not border only players and fallout, and the annex is skipped entirely. The counter
is to only cut districts that sit deep inside enemy territory, where the surround is complete and
there is no fringe of unclaimed land that could leave a gap. A piece that sits on the edge of open
land is a guaranteed dud. The fourth, and most expensive, failure mode is that you gift the land to
the wrong player. All three conditions hold, the transfer fires, but the capturing player is the
enemy who borders the piece most, which is not you, so the land goes to your rival and you only paid
for the crater. The counter is to run the border count before you commit. If you do not strictly
border the piece more than any other enemy, either reposition to gain border first or pass on the
strike this round.

There is also a counterplay angle for the owner of the territory being cut, and it is worth knowing
because it tells you when a player can safely refuse to set up the annex against them. An owner
defends by never leaving a landlocked pocket fully surrounded. Keep at least one coastal exit on
every district, which makes the no-coast condition fail for any attacker. Keep the isthmus to your
main body wider than a single nuke's crater plus fallout can clear. And avoid being reduced to a
single capital that an isolated cut could fully conquer. Conversely, if you are the owner and you are
about to lose a district, the best response is usually to open a coast or a road back to your main
body rather than to try to fight the crater, because a surviving connection is the only thing that
guarantees you keep the land. And if you are a third faction watching someone else drop a nuke that
could cut a pocket, you can move units to border the prospective detached piece before the strike,
raising your own border count so the capturing-player rule hands the district to you. The annex is
not just a nuker's tool. It is a three-way race over who borders the cut-off land most, and every
player's movement in the minutes before the strike changes who collects it.

## Mode and map adjustments

The annex behaves differently across the board states players actually meet, so the same
three-condition read has to be adjusted to each one before you trust it. On standard island maps,
which are the natural home of this feature, coasts are everywhere, which means most cut-off pieces
have their own shoreline and fail the no-coast condition. That is why the annexes that do fire on
standard maps are the rarer, higher-value landlocked pockets, and exactly why they reward a deliberate
setup rather than an accidental bonus. On standard maps, prioritize isthmus targets deep inland where
the surround is complete, and expect the capturing-player race to usually involve a third faction you
have to account for before you fire. On maps or modes that include a large central lake or an inland
sea, the number of landlocked pockets multiplies, because the water divides territory into many more
disconnected islands. Here the annex fires far more often, and the no-coast check on the piece itself
becomes the dominant filter. A piece with its own lake shore is unannexable even though it looks
completely isolated, so on those maps you should target the pocket that the lake does not touch.

In high-nuke or MIRV-heavy modes, the math of the isthmus changes. A MIRV burst is substantially
wider than a single nuke, so it clears a broader band of the connection, which means isthmuses that a
single nuke could not sever at all become severable. If your mode allows MIRV, re-run the "can the
crater plus fallout clear the isthmus" check with the wider MIRV footprint, because targets that were
duds with a single nuke become live annexes with a MIRV. The gold cost of a MIRV only justifies
itself when the annex payoff actually lands, so the width of the burst is what makes the difference
between a broken investment and a working one. In endgame modes, where factions are reduced to just
a few districts each, the full-conquest special case becomes the center of the board. When a rival
is down to a single capital, the annex stops being a tile transfer and becomes a potential game-ender,
so the border-count arithmetic is the entire decision, and the safest play is often to keep the rival
alive as a shared front rather than risk the conquest going to the third faction. Across every mode
the underlying read is identical: isthmus, coast, surround, border count. But the frequency and the
stakes of each condition shift with the map, so the numbers in the two scenario sections above should
always be recomputed for the specific board you are actually on, not copied from a different map shape.

## Quick reference table

Run this table top to bottom before every strike, and do not skip rows. If the version row is wrong
you stop immediately, because nothing below it can fire on a stable build and the rest of the
checklist is wasted effort. If the disconnection row is wrong the owner keeps every tile, so the
whole annex is impossible no matter how clean the crater looks. If the no-coast row is wrong the
piece is unannexable by definition, and the only remedy is a different target. If the surround row
is wrong the engine skips the annex outright, and there is no in-game way to close the gap after the
strike. If the capturing-player row is wrong you have chosen the right geometry but the wrong
beneficiary, which is the most expensive kind of mistake this rule can produce, because you spend
the full gold and a rival collects the land. The only-land row is the one that decides whether the
strike is a tile transfer or a full conquest, and it is the single highest-stakes row on the table
because a wrong answer can end the entire game in the wrong direction.

The table is a filter, not a scoring system. Every row is a hard gate, and one failed row collapses
the annex to crater damage only. The order matters: version first, then disconnection, then coast,
then surround, then capturing player, then only-land, because each earlier row is cheaper to check
and eliminates more candidates than the ones after it. Checking the version before you do any tile
math saves the entire rest of the exercise when you are on stable. Checking disconnection before
coast saves the coast check when the piece is not actually severed. The full derivation of each row
is in the sections above, and the two scenario walks show the tile and gold numbers that turn a
passing row into a real decision on your specific board. The table is deliberately short, because the
goal is to run it in under a minute at the moment of decision, not to re-derive the rule under time
pressure.

| Check | What to confirm on the map | If it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Version | You are running v0.35.0-beta1 or beta2, not stable v0.34.25 | Rule not present; old enclosure behavior holds |
| Disconnection | Crater plus fallout fully clears the isthmus to the main body | Owner keeps the piece; re-aim at the narrowest isthmus |
| No own coast | Detached piece touches no water and no map edge | Unannexable; target the landlocked inner half |
| Complete surround | Piece borders only fallout and enemies, no open tile | Annex skipped; only cut deep inside enemy land |
| Capturing player | You strictly border the piece more than any other enemy | Land goes to the rival; reposition or pass |
| Only-land special case | Detached piece is the owner's last remaining land | Full conquest by capturing player; verify border count first |
