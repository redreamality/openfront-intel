**Short answer.** Dropping a nuke that slices enemy territory into a piece no longer connected to
their main body can hand that cut-off piece to a surrounding enemy, instead of the owner keeping it.
It requires an eligible severed inland pocket surrounded by other players and fallout,
with an eligible enemy recipient. This behavior is in **stable v0.35.0 through v0.35.2**;
you no longer need a beta build. The older v0.34.25 does not contain this capture path.

## The rule in one picture

Think of an enemy's territory as a single blob of land. The main body is the largest
connected cluster by land area, not by border length. Now you drop a nuke. The crater erases a ring of
their tiles, and the fallout that follows is an unowned boundary accepted by the annexation check.
It is not your land and gives you no border advantage. If the remaining land is split in two, one side
stays attached to the main body and the other becomes a free-floating island of their land with no
path back to it. That floating piece is what the engine calls the severed cluster, and the new rule
decides what happens to it. Before v0.35, the engine's old enclosure logic rarely treated a nuke-cut
arm of a coastal empire as annexable, which is exactly why players complained for a long time that
nuking to cut land off "looked random" and "stopped working for coastal empires." Pull request #5751
reworked the capture path so that a genuinely cut-off, landlocked piece is now claimed by the enemy
that surrounds it, instead of being left to the original owner.

The decision this unlocks is a real return-on-investment call, not a passive bonus. A single nuke
that would only destroy a few buildings can now also transfer an entire district, sometimes dozens of
production tiles, to a third faction or to you. That changes which strikes are worth the gold. A
strike aimed at the thickest part of an enemy's territory may cut nothing at all, while a strike that
lands on the narrow isthmus connecting their coast to their inland heart can both crater that
connection and detach a chunk that a bordering enemy then swallows. You are no longer just buying
damage with the nuke; you are buying a territorial transfer on top of the damage. That is why the
feature matters even though it only triggers in a narrow set of board geometries: the geometry is
precisely the geometry a thoughtful endgame nuke already tries to hit. This is also why the feature
changed how experienced players evaluate a nuclear strike in the first place. The nuke is no longer
a pure damage purchase where you subtract the crater cost from the buildings destroyed. It is now a
compound investment, where the same gold can simultaneously buy the crater and the transfer, and the
transfer is usually the larger of the two. Because the payoff is conditional and geometry-dependent,
the difference between a winning strike and a dud is no longer your aim alone but your read of the
board geometry. The rest of this guide walks through the exact conditions, the capture logic, the
version boundary, and how to read the map so you can set up the cut deliberately instead of
discovering it by accident, and it ends with a quick reference table you can run before every strike.

## The three conditions that must all hold

The engine does not annex a cut-off piece just because it looks disconnected on the map. Three
independent checks must all pass at the same time, and if any one of them fails the piece is left
entirely alone and the owner keeps it. The first condition is disconnection from the main body. The
cluster must not be connected, through the owner's own border tiles, to their largest landmass. A
piece that still touches the main body by even a single tile is not severed and is ignored by the
annex path. This is why a nuke that merely damages the edge of a blob, without fully clearing the
connecting isthmus, does nothing at all: the connection survives, so nothing is actually cut off. The
second condition is that the piece has no coast and no map edge on its own. If the cut-off piece
touches open water or the border of the map by itself, the owner can simply boat out of it or treat
it as a self-sustaining enclave, so the engine refuses to annex it. This is the single most common
reason a cut appears to "not work" in real games: the detached arm still has its own coastline. The
third condition is that the piece borders only other players and fallout, and that at least one enemy
and at least one fallout tile surround it. The piece must be fully ringed by fallout, which is
an unowned boundary, and by other players' territory, with no ordinary unclaimed tiles touching it. If any
tile of the piece opens onto empty no-man's-land, the annex is skipped, because there is no
surrounding enemy that could claim it.

The practical takeaway is that you need a landlocked pocket of enemy land, fully surrounded by
fallout and other players' borders, that has completely lost its road home. A nuke that carves a ring of
fallout around a district while the district's only land bridge back to the owner sits inside that
ring is the textbook setup that satisfies all three conditions at once. Note the order in which
players usually fail these checks. First they get the disconnection right but the piece still has a
coast. Then they handle the coast but the piece still touches the main body by a single tile. Then
they clear both of those but a gap of open land breaks the "borders only players and fallout" rule.
Each failure mode is visible on the map before you spend a single unit of gold, which is what makes
the whole rule plan-able rather than lucky. The failures section later breaks down each of these with
concrete counterplay and the situations where you should avoid the strike entirely, but the core idea
is that the rule is precise and deterministic, and a single failed condition means the strike is
crater damage and nothing more.

## Which enemy actually captures the piece

When the pocket qualifies, start with the players whose actual territory borders it. Candidates
friendly to its owner are excluded. The relationship to the player firing the nuke does not add
another exclusion: your ally can still qualify if that player is hostile to the owner. Fallout
does not count as your territory, so creating a large radioactive boundary gives you no extra
votes. A candidate needs real land beside the pocket. Moving a ship nearby or merely placing
troops somewhere on the screen does not change the ownership of those neighboring tiles.

The first priority is the largest single ongoing attack against the owner among these bordering
candidates. Compare individual attacks, not the sum of all attacks by each player. A neighboring
enemy with one larger attack can receive the pocket even when another candidate has more border.
An attacker elsewhere on the map does not qualify just by sending a huge force: that player
still needs to be one of the pocket's bordering candidates. This is why checking only the outline
before firing can be costly. The fighting already in progress is part of the recipient decision,
and its state may change before the transfer is evaluated.

Only when none of those candidates has a qualifying ongoing attack does the boundary comparison
decide. The count is shared four-direction contact edges, not a count of distinct pocket tiles.
One pocket tile can contribute more than one edge to the same neighbor. In a hypothetical case
with no ongoing attacks, fourteen contact edges beat eleven. Do not use that example when an
eligible attack exists. Equal highest values retain the candidate encountered first internally;
neither a tied attack nor a tied border is a dependable way to promise yourself the land.

For the remaining examples, any conclusion based solely on border counts assumes that no
candidate has an ongoing attack against the owner. If that assumption stops being true, repeat
the recipient check using attack priority before trusting the example. A strike that meets every
geographical condition can still hand the district to a third player. The person paying for the
missile and the person collecting the land are separate roles, so include both in your decision.
If you cannot confidently identify the recipient, value the shot for its destruction alone.

There is also a settlement distinction. If the pocket is all the owner's land at the instant
it transfers, the recipient conquers that player. This can happen when other territory was taken
earlier in the same annexation pass. It does not mean that any lone capital can be cut away
from itself, or that conquering one player wins the entire match. First establish an eligible
severed pocket, then its recipient, then whether any of the owner's land remains. Keep these
questions separate before counting a possible elimination as your own finishing move.

## Version boundary: stable v35 and older v34 games

Nuke-cut annexation is now part of a stable release. v0.35.0 was published on
2026-10-16, and the behavior remains in v0.35.2, published on 2026-10-17. You do not need to
choose a beta server to use it in a game running these versions. The practical change is that
a deliberate cut can now be evaluated for both crater damage and the land a surrounding enemy
may receive. Treat those as separate returns: the damage can still be useful when the transfer
fails, while a transfer to the wrong rival can make an otherwise accurate strike a bad investment.

The older v0.34.25 does not contain this particular capture path. That historical boundary
still matters when comparing old recordings, older replays, or a custom server deliberately
running an earlier build. Do not expect an old game to reproduce the stable v35 result simply
because the visible shape looks the same. Equally, do not explain a failed cut in a current
v35 game by saying that annexation is beta-only. First establish which version the game uses,
then examine the geometry and the beneficiary. A release date identifies when a build became
available; it does not establish the build of every custom server or every recorded match.

For a v35 game, start with the connection you intend to remove. A thin-looking strip can still
leave a surviving route around the crater, and an isolated-looking district can retain its own
coastline. The release does not turn every nuclear strike into automatic annexation. The piece
must meet the conditions described above, and you still need to identify the surrounding enemy
that receives it. Check that player before counting the expected land toward your own position.
If a rival surrounds the district more effectively, the strike can advance their finish rather
than yours. Waiting for a better border can be more valuable than firing as soon as you can.

When reviewing a failed attempt, separate version, geometry, and reward. An older build can
explain why this capture path is unavailable. In a supported v35 build, inspect the surviving
connection, water or map edge, and any unclaimed gap before changing your aim or buying another
missile. If the transfer happened but helped someone else, changing versions will not solve the
problem: the next decision is about who surrounds the target. This order also makes old videos
useful without treating their outcome as a promise for your present match.

Use the [official v0.35.2 release notes](https://github.com/openfrontio/OpenFrontIO/releases/tag/v0.35.2)
for the published v35 boundary. They include the v0.35.0 change restoring annexation of territory
cut away by a nuke. For actual play, keep the decision simple: supported build, complete cut,
eligible pocket, intended recipient, then cost. Stable availability removes the need to seek a
playtest; it leaves every geographical and strategic check in place.

## Reading the map before you commit

Turning the three conditions into a pre-strike checklist makes the difference between an accidental
bonus and a reliable one, and each check is a simple yes or no read you can do before spending gold.
First, find the isthmus: the narrowest band of the owner's land that connects the target district
back to their main body. Check the owner's actual surviving land after the expected blast;
fallout is an unowned boundary accepted by this check, not your land or an extra friendly radius.
It gives you no contact edges. Do not infer a severed connection from a radioactive color alone.
If a land route survives the strike, disconnection fails and this path does not transfer the pocket.
You need the actual connection removed, rather than a larger-looking visual effect, so skip that target and
look elsewhere. Second, check the coast on the cut-off side. Trace the outline of the district you
are trying to detach and confirm it has no water and no map border on its own. If the district you
want to gift to a rival has its own shoreline, it is unannexable no matter how cleanly you cut it, and
you are far better off targeting the district's landlocked inner half instead of the coastal shell.
Third, verify the surround. The detached piece must be ringed by fallout and other players' land,
with an eligible enemy candidate; not every player neighbor has to be hostile. There must be
zero ordinary unclaimed tiles touching it. Any gap of open no-man's-land breaks the "borders only players and
fallout" rule and kills the annex, so the cut needs to sit deep inside enemy territory, not on the
fringe of unclaimed land where a stray gap is likely.

Do all three checks before you spend a single unit of gold, because a single no means the strike is
crater damage only and the transfer is impossible. Then, and only then, do the fourth check, which is
the recipient check that decides who benefits. Check ongoing attacks first, then contact edges
if no candidate is attacking, and confirm that the recipient is you rather than a
rival. If a third faction would collect the land, the strike is a gift to them and you should either
reposition to gain border first or pass entirely. The two scenario sections that follow apply these
exact reads to two concrete board states, so you can see the thresholds, the tile counts, and the
positions and costs that separate a useful transfer from a poor investment. The skill is: read the
isthmus, read the coast, read the surround, count the border, and only then decide whether the crater
damage is worth the territorial transfer, with the version boundary as the zeroth check that gates
everything else in this entire guide.

## Scenario A: cutting a coastal arm off a mid-game empire

Set the board for a concrete mid-game case. Three factions on a standard island map. You are at
about sixty percent population. The target enemy owns a long coastal strip on the east side that
connects to their inland capital by a two-tile-wide isthmus. Their east strip has its own coastline,
which is the trap that catches most players. If you nuke the isthmus and detach the whole east
strip, the strip still touches the sea, so the "no coast" condition fails and the owner keeps every
tile of it. That is the mistake to avoid. Instead, look at the inland pocket behind the isthmus. It
is a cluster of roughly eighteen farmland tiles that the east strip protects, and it is fully
landlocked. It is bordered on its north and south by your own territory and on its west by the
isthmus itself. A MIRV that bursts directly on that isthmus produces a crater and a fallout ring that
is wide enough to clear the two-tile connection. After the detonation the inland pocket is
disconnected from the main body, has no coast of its own, and is ringed by unowned fallout and the
territory of the factions around it, so the three conditions all hold at once.

Now count the actual value. Eighteen tiles do not imply nine gold per tick: there is no fixed
half-gold-per-tile income rule. These are hypothetical map dimensions, not an income formula or
a guarantee that a MIRV's impacts will clear the connection. Value the position, access routes,
and any buildings that actually survive separately from the crater damage. Assume for this
example that no bordering candidate has an ongoing attack. If you have the most contact edges,
you receive the pocket. If the northern candidate has more, that player receives it. If a
qualifying attack starts, attack priority replaces this border-only comparison. The decision is therefore
conditional. Even after confirming the recipient, compare the position and cost before firing;
receiving land alone does not prove a net win. If you cannot confirm the recipient, the
strike buys the north player an eighteen-tile district, and your only sane options are to reposition
so that you border the pocket first, or to pass on the strike this round and wait for a better
geometry. This is the whole skill compressed into one example: read the isthmus, read the coast, read
the surround, count the tiles, and only then decide whether the crater damage is worth the transfer.
The numbers, not the drama of the crater, are what make the decision.

## Scenario B: a severed pocket becomes the last land

Consider a hypothetical endgame with you, a rival, and a weakened target. Before the annexation
pass, the target still has a main body and a separate eligible inland pocket of twenty-five
tiles. The pocket has lost its connection, touches neither water nor map edge, and is surrounded
by other players and fallout without an ordinary unclaimed gap. During that same pass, the main
body is taken through enclosure. When the severed pocket is evaluated for transfer, it has
therefore become the target's last remaining territory. The order is essential: it was identified
relative to another main body, rather than being a lone capital somehow disconnected from itself.

Now assume neither bordering candidate has an ongoing attack against the target. You share
fourteen four-direction contact edges with the pocket and the rival shares eleven. These are
hypothetical contact counts, not fourteen different pocket tiles versus eleven different tiles.
Under this explicit no-attack assumption, your larger count makes you the recipient. If the
numbers are reversed, the rival receives it. If the rival instead has the largest qualifying
ongoing attack at evaluation time, the rival takes priority despite your fourteen edges.
Recheck the battle state before treating a promising boundary as a guaranteed reward.

Because the transfer now covers all of the target's remaining land, the recipient conquers
the target rather than merely taking one district from a still-living player. That is a
player elimination, not a promise of victory in the whole match. The rival in this example
still exists after the target is gone, and the rest of the board can remain dangerous. If the
main body had not already been lost, the same pocket transfer would leave the target alive
elsewhere. Likewise, another surviving outpost would prevent this transfer from being the
last-land case. Do not decide that a visible capital is the entire player without checking.

For your strike decision, compare the board after both possible recipients. If you receive the
pocket, does the new border expose your core to the surviving rival? If the rival receives it,
does removing the target release pressure on that rival's other front? Destruction can still
be useful, but do not pay for it under the assumption that the conquest belongs to you. When
the recipient is uncertain, postponing the strike can preserve a shared front and give you time
to change the actual territory boundary. Extra nearby troops without a land transfer do not
increase your contact count.

The useful lesson is the sequence, not the twenty-five-tile figure: establish a qualifying pocket,
account for other land lost in the same pass, identify the recipient by attack priority or its
border fallback, and only then assess elimination. A more dramatic crater cannot replace any
of those checks. If even one premise differs in your match, recompute the outcome rather than
copying the hypothetical conquest.

## Failures and counterplay

Most failed annexes trace back to one of four failure modes, and each one is visible on the map
before you spend a single unit of gold, so the goal is to catch the failure in the planning phase
rather than after the strike. The first failure mode is that the connection survives. Your crater plus
fallout did not fully clear the isthmus, so a single tile still links the piece back to the main body
and the disconnection check fails. The counter is to aim the burst at the narrowest point of the
isthmus and confirm that the actual land connection will be removed, without treating fallout as
your own land or an extra friendly radius. Otherwise, look for a different target. The second failure mode is that
the piece keeps a coast. You cut the district but it retains its own shoreline, so the no-coast
condition fails and the owner keeps the land, still able to boat out of it. The counter is to target
the landlocked inner half of the district rather than the coastal shell, or to accept that this
particular district is unannexable and pick a different pocket that has no coast of its own.

The third failure mode is that open land breaks the surround. A gap of no-man's-land touches the
piece, so it does not border only players and fallout, and the annex is skipped entirely. The counter
is to only cut districts that sit deep inside enemy territory, where the surround is complete and
there is no fringe of unclaimed land that could leave a gap. A piece that sits on the edge of open
land is a guaranteed dud. The fourth, and most expensive, failure mode is that you gift the land to
the wrong player. All three conditions hold, the transfer fires, but the capturing player is the
enemy selected by attack priority or the no-attack border fallback, so the land can go to your rival.
The counter is to inspect both ongoing attacks and real contact edges before you commit. If you
cannot establish that you are the recipient, either change the actual territory boundary or pass on the
strike this round.

There is also a counterplay angle for the owner of the territory being cut, and it is worth knowing
because it tells you when a player can safely refuse to set up the annex against them. An owner
defends by never leaving a landlocked pocket fully surrounded. Keep at least one coastal exit on
every district, which makes the no-coast condition fail for any attacker. Keep the isthmus to your
main body wider than the expected strike can actually clear. Avoid an eligible pocket becoming
your last land after other regions are lost. Conversely, if you are the owner and you are
about to lose a district, the best response is usually to open a coast or a road back to your main
body rather than to try to fight the crater, because a surviving connection blocks this severed-pocket
path. Other attacks and enclosure can still threaten the land. A third faction can change its real
territory boundary before the strike; merely moving units does not increase contact counts.
A qualifying ongoing attack also takes priority over the border fallback. The annex is
therefore a contest involving actual land and attacks, not an automatic reward for the person
who fires. Recheck both when the board changes instead of relying on an earlier outline.

## Mode and map adjustments

The annex behaves differently across the board states players actually meet, so the same
three-condition read has to be adjusted to each one before you trust it. On standard island maps,
which are the natural home of this feature, coasts are everywhere, which means most cut-off pieces
have their own shoreline and fail the no-coast condition. That is why the annexes that do fire on
standard maps are the rarer, higher-value landlocked pockets, and exactly why they reward a deliberate
setup rather than an accidental bonus. On standard maps, prioritize isthmus targets deep inland where
the surround is complete, and expect the capturing-player race to usually involve a third faction you
have to account for before you fire. On maps or modes that include a large central lake or an inland
sea, water can separate territory without creating an eligible inland pocket. There is no
guaranteed increase in annexations simply because a map has more water. The no-coast check remains
essential: a piece with its own lake shore is unannexable even though it looks
completely isolated, so on those maps you should target the pocket that the lake does not touch.

In high-nuke or MIRV-heavy modes, evaluate the actual expected impact locations rather than treating
a MIRV as a guaranteed wider single crater. Multiple explosions can remove different connections;
they can also leave a route intact or destroy a pocket you intended to receive. If your mode allows
MIRV, repeat the complete connection check for the expected pattern and retain uncertainty where
you cannot predict the surviving land. Cost can be justified by destruction, territorial position,
or both; annexation is not the only possible return. In endgames, identify whether the eligible
pocket really becomes the owner's last land during settlement, instead of assuming a lone capital
always triggers conquest. A transfer can eliminate the target while leaving other rivals alive.
Check attack priority before the border fallback, and decide whether removing that shared front
helps you more than the rival. Across every mode the underlying read is identical: surviving
connection, coast, surround, recipient, and remaining land. The frequency and the
stakes of each condition shift with the map. Check whether receiving the pocket exposes a new
front, and whether keeping the target alive still ties down a rival's troops. Those questions
remain relevant even when the annexation itself is certain. The numbers in the two scenario sections above should
always be recomputed for the specific board you are actually on, not copied from a different map shape.

## Quick reference table

Run this table top to bottom before every strike, and do not skip rows. If the version row is wrong
you stop immediately, because this path is absent in older v0.34.25 builds and the rest of the
checklist is wasted effort. If the disconnection row is wrong the owner keeps every tile, so the
whole annex is impossible no matter how clean the crater looks. If the no-coast row is wrong the
piece is unannexable by definition, and the only remedy is a different target. If the surround row
is wrong the engine skips the annex outright, and there is no in-game way to close the gap after the
strike. If the capturing-player row is wrong you have chosen the right geometry but the wrong
beneficiary, which is the most expensive kind of mistake this rule can produce, because you spend
the full gold and a rival collects the land. The only-land row is the one that decides whether the
strike is a tile transfer or a full conquest, and it is the single highest-stakes row on the table
because a wrong answer can end the entire game in the wrong direction.

The table separates eligibility, recipient, and settlement. Only the eligibility checks determine
whether this annex can happen; the last-land row changes settlement. Check version, disconnection, coast,
then surround, then capturing player, then only-land, because each earlier row is cheaper to check
and eliminates more candidates than the ones after it. Checking the version before you do any tile
math saves the entire rest of the exercise when you are on an unsupported older build. Checking disconnection before
coast saves the coast check when the piece is not actually severed. The full derivation of each row
is in the sections above, and the two scenario walks show the tile and gold numbers that turn a
passing row into a real decision on your specific board. The table is deliberately short, because the
goal is to run it in under a minute at the moment of decision, not to re-derive the rule under time
pressure.

| Check | What to confirm on the map | If it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Version | Verified stable v0.35.0–v0.35.2; v0.34.25 lacks this path | Assess the actual build; do not assume beta-only |
| Disconnection | Crater plus fallout fully clears the isthmus to the main body | Owner keeps the piece; re-aim at the narrowest isthmus |
| No own coast | Detached piece touches no water and no map edge | Unannexable; target the landlocked inner half |
| Complete surround | Other players and fallout surround it; no ordinary unclaimed gap; an eligible enemy exists | No eligible annex through this path |
| Capturing player | Largest single ongoing attack among bordering enemies; otherwise most contact edges | Recipient may be a rival; reassess |
| Last-land settlement | Is this all the owner's remaining land at transfer time? | No: ordinary land transfer; yes: recipient conquers the target |
