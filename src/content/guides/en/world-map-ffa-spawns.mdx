In a public FFA on the standard **World** map, choose a spawn for two usable exits, not for a famous country or an old heatmap's favorite color. Check the actual lobby and nearby human and Nation claims, identify a first land route and an independent fallback, then commit only while the first route leaves a defensible border and enough troops to answer the closest rival. A continent is a starting position, not a plan.

## Confirm World and read the live spawn contract

Start with the exact map name. The official [v0.34.11 World manifest](https://github.com/openfrontio/OpenFrontIO/blob/v0.34.11/resources/maps/world/manifest.json) identifies `World` as a 2,000 by 1,000 source map with 651,569 listed land tiles. It is not Giant World Map, World Inverted, the standalone Europe map, or Europe Classic. A route that works across the standalone Europe's river does not automatically exist at the World map's scale. Nor does a thumbnail prove that two blue areas connect, that a thin land strip is passable, or that an island has a safe landing. Read the map selected in the current lobby before applying anyone's regional advice.

The manifest supplies Nation anchors such as the United States, Canada, Mexico, Brazil, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, India, and Indonesia. Those coordinates are entries for AI Nations, not a list of guaranteed human starting squares. A human may appear near a familiar label, but ownership and nearby pressure depend on the match. The manifest also includes a forced Crowded modifier entry; do not convert that into a promise that every public lobby has a fixed number of active human opponents. Use the actual mode, player count, spawn setting, and visible markers. A Nation is not a passive empty-country bonus: it may already own the coast or land corridor you expected to take.

The tagged [SpawnExecution](https://github.com/openfrontio/OpenFrontIO/blob/v0.34.11/src/core/execution/SpawnExecution.ts) validates the selected tile; random-spawn games do not honor a manual favorite. Random candidates must be usable land rather than water, owned land, or a map border, and placement initially respects separation from other spawns. On a crowded board, the separation check can relax after repeated attempts. That is why a screenshot of an apparently open coast is not a reservation. In manual spawn, compare candidates before confirming; in random spawn, run the same comparison immediately after placement. Neither mode allows you to assume an exact region from the map's Nation coordinates.

Use a three-part observation during the spawn phase: where is your current land component, which other marker can reach the same first pocket, and which second direction stays open if that player gets there first? You do not need a perfect route across the planet. You do need a route that survives one adversary's ordinary expansion. A broad inland area can be worse than a narrow coast if two humans contest its only useful exit. Conversely, an island that looks safe can be a slow trap when its next shore depends on a Port, Transport, landing, and a defender who will not wait. Name the next capture after the first one, not just the country you want to occupy.

This is a World-specific placement answer. [FFA opening](/strategies/ffa-opening/) covers the general spawn clock, first buildings, and reserves; [map strategy](/guides/map-strategy/) explains topology on any asset. The separate [Europe FFA guide](/guides/europe-ffa-openings/) uses Europe's own river and Nation layout. Here the decision is whether a World position still has an independent way forward after a neighboring claim changes the map. Treat the official manifest as the identity and layout anchor, and the live lobby as the final authority for this particular match.

## Compare exits instead of ranking continents

Before confirming a manual spawn, compare two or three positions with the same questions. For a random spawn, compare possible directions from the given position. An exit is not a compass point: it is a reachable sequence of land or a water route whose landing can be held. The first land pocket should lead to a second useful claim without making your core border impossible to defend. The fallback must not require the same contested neck, Port, or Nation to remain yours. If both routes fail when one neighboring player captures one tile, you have one route with two names.

| World position to inspect | First route worth testing | Independent fallback | Reject or revise when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Continental interior with several neighbors | Take a short, defensible land pocket away from the nearest human | Preserve a different land edge and a safe City site | Both apparent exits meet the same rival or produce three active fronts |
| Coastal mainland near a narrow passage | Secure inland depth before paying for a water crossing | Keep another land direction, or prove a distinct reachable shore | The only landing is defended and the home coast has no reserve |
| Island or distant edge | Claim enough local space to fund a genuine crossing | Identify a second shore or another island chain that does not use the same Port | A single blocked Port or Transport would strand the entire plan |

This table is a checklist, not a claim that one named continent wins more. Community posts about World spawn heatmaps often compare millions of historical spawns or thousands of games, but their maps describe where players appeared or what happened in those samples. They do not hold lobby settings, player skill, Nation behavior, map version, and the first two human choices constant. A hot spot might be popular, frequently offered, or favorable in that sample; a cold spot might simply be unavailable under the sampled modes. Do not read a color as a current causal win-rate estimate. The usable lesson is that people disagree about what makes a spawn good and often fail to separate the land available now from an exit they must still win.

Score each candidate on four visible axes. First, count reachable land exits, not coastline length. Second, mark the nearest human and Nation whose expansion can close the first pocket. Third, ask whether taking that pocket shortens or lengthens the border you must defend. Fourth, find interior room for growth and a reserve that can reach both routes. You can use a small ledger: “route A reaches this neutral land; rival R can deny it from here; route B reaches that other component; I stop A if R arrives first.” The labels are observations from your match, not fixed coordinates from a guide.

Do not force a Port because World has oceans. A coastal Port is valuable only when it opens a trade or naval route you can use and protect; it is a poor first purchase if a close human is taking your inland exits. Nor should you choose a landlocked interior just because it contains many pixels. A cluster of Nations and humans can turn apparent space into expensive borders. If you cannot identify an independent second route for any manual candidate, choose the one with the shorter defensible frontier and keep more liquid Gold and Troops. On random spawn, that same constraint means delaying speculative infrastructure until you know which exit actually exists.

## Separate a Nation border from a human race

A World Nation marker and a human spawn marker should not be written in the same column of your opening plan. The manifest's Nation positions identify possible AI actors, not vacant territory or the fixed human population of a public room. A human can redirect instantly to deny the first neutral pocket; a Nation expands according to its behavior and current borders, and in v34 Hard and Impossible Nations can favor valuable bordering rivals they can safely beat. Neither actor is harmless. The useful distinction is what you can observe before deciding where to send a limited first wave: a human marker moving toward the same pocket, a Nation border already touching it, or a Nation engaged elsewhere.

Suppose your manual World candidate has broad-looking land between a Nation and a human rival. Do not count the whole visual gap as your future territory. Mark the first tile sequence that either opponent could claim and the border you would inherit after taking it. If the human reaches the gap sooner, attacking that player to preserve your original map sketch may create the very second front you were trying to avoid. If the Nation reaches it, ask whether a short border with it is easier to hold than a long human border; the answer depends on troop levels and the other exposed side, not on the label “bot.” A small neutral claim that preserves an inland alternate can be worth more than the larger, contested wedge.

The same distinction matters after random placement. You might be assigned near two Nation anchors while a human starts behind a long coast. The coast is not automatically safe, and the Nations do not automatically become your early targets. Look at actual ownership, troop posture, and expansion direction. Choose a first pocket whose capture does not give both Nations a new border into your core. If the human crosses the apparent open land before you, switch the planned first attack or pull back to a shorter line. If one Nation is busy elsewhere, treat that as a temporary opportunity to claim a useful route, not a permanent promise that it will ignore your new border.

Diplomacy is an option after contact, not a substitute for geometry. An alliance request can reduce one active border, but a request can fail and an accepted pact has a duration. The [Nation alliance decisions](/guides/nation-alliance-decisions/) guide handles requests and exit timing; [Nations pressure](/guides/nations-pressure/) handles whether an AI border is a useful pressure valve or an exposure. For the World spawn, write a simpler condition: “if this actor arrives first, route A closes; route B still reaches land without crossing that actor.” If that sentence is false, move the starting candidate or keep the first investment small. Classifying the neighbor correctly prevents you from paying for a route that never existed.

## Use regional shapes as scenarios, not a tier list

World's recognizable geography invites shortcuts: “North America has room,” “Europe is crowded,” “South America is safe,” or “an island is protected.” Each is incomplete. The map asset has a particular land-water shape, but the outcome of a starting position is made by lobby settings and neighbors. Compare the same four questions in each region: where is the first accessible land pocket, who can reach it, what frontier does it create, and what independent second path remains? A regional name is useful for orientation; it is not a stand-in for these answers. A heatmap of past spawns can suggest which shapes deserve inspection, but it cannot decide the current opponent's first claim.

Imagine two candidates in the Americas. One is a broad inland position with two apparent land directions and two nearby human markers. The other has less local land, a short defensible edge, and one coast whose crossing remains unproven. The inland candidate wins on raw visible area but may lose on border cost; the coastal one wins on defense but may fail if its second route requires an unaffordable crossing. Do not declare either continent or coast superior. Identify which of the two markers can close a route before your first expansion and how much force must stay home. A better candidate is the one that preserves a useful option after that plausible closure, not the one that looks largest at zero seconds.

An Africa-Europe or Asia-island comparison raises a different question. A mainland start can have several neighboring Nations and a human arrival across a nearby corridor. An island start may have few immediate land enemies but a dependent naval exit. Ask whether the mainland's second land route avoids the same contested neck, and whether the island's first landing reaches more than a tiny shore. If both mainland routes meet one bottleneck, the island with a proven crossing may be more flexible. If the island has only one Port and the enemy already holds the arrival coast, the crowded mainland with a short line and a second inland path may be better. The conditional statements are the decision, not a universal ranking of Africa, Europe, Asia, or islands.

At a far edge such as an Antarctic area, local isolation can delay contact but also delay conversion of land into a consequential attack or useful income route. First check whether that land is actually usable in the selected mode and how it connects to another region. Then compare the time and resources required to leave against the security it buys. A quiet first minute is not a victory condition. If the route out needs a single vulnerable Port and a long crossing, keep another plan for the home land and do not overbuild the edge merely because nobody is attacking yet. In all regions, the live marker positions can reverse the recommendation. The table above stays valid because it describes dependencies and stop signals rather than promising a best country.

## Turn the first pocket into a route without losing the core

Once the match begins, treat the opening as a sequence of tests, not a sprint toward a named continent. The standard [FFA opening guide](/strategies/ffa-opening/) describes the spawn phase and default five-second protection, but protection does not reserve neutral land and it expires for everyone. Watch which neighbor sends a first wave, which Nation grows toward your planned pocket, and whether your first capture creates a narrow front or exposes two more. A rival who is not attacking you can still close the route by arriving first. That is a reason to re-evaluate before adding a second costly structure or a full troop commitment.

Use the first small claim to verify geometry. Can your force reach the intended land without passing through a hostile tile or impassable terrain? Does the newly acquired shape let you defend the return path? Is the next pocket still neutral, or has an opponent already made it part of their core? If the answer changes, revise while the bulk of your Troops are available. If route A buys only a long border around a dense defender, stop feeding it. Turn to route B, hold a short line, or wait for a better target; do not confuse repeated attacks with progress. The [land-combat guide](/guides/land-combat/) handles troop ratio, density, terrain, Defense Posts, and counterattacks once contact becomes the main problem.

Keep two budgets separate: the force committed to the first route and the force that can answer a breach at home or make the fallback real. The same applies to Gold. A City solves a troop-cap bottleneck when it can be protected; a Defense Post solves a particular threatened line; a Port solves a reachable water problem. None fixes a plan with no second exit. Do not buy a Port on an exposed coast merely because the nearest overseas region looks empty on the thumbnail. Trace the embarkation, actual water path, landing ownership, follow-up target, and the troops left behind. If a Transport has already sailed, [transport landings](/guides/transport-landings/) takes over the wave and escort decision.

Consider an illustrative North American mainland spawn with one neutral pocket west of your marker, a human approaching it from the south, and another land edge leading toward an interior Nation. Assume the first pocket is reachable and your back line has room for a City. A bounded first capture is reasonable while the southern rival is still distant; reserve enough force to prevent their counterattack. If they claim the pocket first, the original plan is not “almost complete.” Reclassify the rival as an active border, shorten that line, and inspect the Nation-side land exit before another attack. This is not a prediction about all North American positions. Move the human marker or Nation border and the decision changes.

Now consider a coastal African or South American start in which one promising overseas shore is separated by water, while a second land pocket remains on the home continent. Assume Ports and Transports are available, but the landing owner and route are not yet proved. Secure enough home depth to keep a reserve; test the land pocket while tracing the water path. Only invest in the crossing when the arrival has a second claim and you can survive an opponent contesting the Port. If a Nation takes the intended landing or a human closes the home pocket, the next move is to revise the route and spending order, not to ship more Troops into an unverified shore. The two cases illustrate conditional exits, not recommended geographic spawn coordinates.

## Prove a water exit before buying the crossing

World's ocean makes a distant coast look like an easy second route, but a water exit is a chain of permissions and risks. You need a legal departure shore, the right Port and transport access, a navigable path in the actual game map, a landing not already denied by another owner, and a second useful land move after arrival. Failing any one link leaves you with a Port and ships that do not solve the land race at home. Trace that chain on the live board rather than drawing a straight line across the thumbnail. If the route uses a narrow strait, ask who can threaten both departure and arrival. If it uses open water, ask what Warship can contest the crossing and how much of your army remains to defend the core.

The first question is not “can a Transport technically arrive?” but “what decision becomes possible after it arrives?” A landing on a small empty island may shorten a route or provide a protected staging point. It may also strand Troops with no next claim while an opponent takes your mainland pocket. A landing on a larger coast can be valuable, but only if the arriving force can hold enough depth to survive the defender's counterattack. Write down the target after the beachhead and the stop condition if it is captured before you sail. That separates an independent fallback from a maritime version of the same contested land lane.

Consider a conditional South American coastal position. Assume your home land route is blocked by a human and one overseas shore appears neutral, with a second defended shore beyond it. A cheap-looking crossing is not enough. If the neutral shore has no practical follow-up, use the remaining home reserve to shorten the human border and postpone the Port. If it does lead to a second claim, and the Port site can survive a raid, a limited first wave may establish a real alternate. The result flips if a Nation takes the first shore, a Warship enters the path, or the home defender needs those Troops. Those changes are observable before sending another wave; do not keep sailing because the first Transport has already been paid for.

Water can also be a way to escape a crowded continental interior, but do not equate any blue pixel with a connected sea. Impassable terrain and separated components matter; newer Water Nuke changes can create different routes, but an old map screenshot cannot certify the current path. A Port's trade income may help finance a crossing in a later phase, yet it is not guaranteed by building near the coast. Read ownership, reachable trading partners, and the home defense before treating future income as available Gold. [Port versus Factory](/guides/port-vs-factory/) covers the investment comparison, and [transport landings](/guides/transport-landings/) covers the actual wave. At spawn time, the water route stays a candidate until its departure, arrival, follow-up, and return costs are all visible.

## Allocate a reserve to both exits, not two full attacks

Two routes do not mean two simultaneous all-in pushes. The point of a fallback is to preserve the ability to change direction when the first lane becomes expensive. Keep a mobile troop reserve behind the first front and avoid placing all new buildings where the first route alone can protect them. On World a long-looking empty space can lure you into sending a large wave across it, but distance does not remove the rival who can cut the return line. Before each large commitment, ask whether the troops left at home can answer the nearest human, whether the second route still has a reachable first step, and whether one counterattack would trap the main force away from your City or Port.

Spending should follow the current bottleneck, not a universal build order. If your cap is limiting growth and your interior is secure, a City can support both routes. If a specific neck is under threat, a Defense Post may protect the short line while a smaller force probes elsewhere. If a tested water route is the only real second exit and the departure coast is holdable, a Port may unlock it. If none of those claims is true, liquid Gold is an option: it can answer the next observed threat after the map changes. An early investment is expensive not only in its displayed price but in the alternative response it removes.

Suppose you have just claimed the first pocket and both your next land target and a Port site are visible. Assume a human rival can reach the land target soon, but cannot yet hit the Port. If the land target creates a short defensible border and leaves a second inland path, hold troops for that contest and defer the Port. If the rival already owns the land target, do not spend an entire reserve to recreate the old plan. Re-evaluate whether the water site reaches a useful landing and whether enough force remains to protect the mainland. The same snapshot can justify opposite purchases after one change in ownership. That is why the live map, not a memorized minute mark, determines the order.

The reserve is also attention. Assign a check after every meaningful capture: inspect the nearest rival's direction, the second exit's ownership, and the shortest line back to your economic core. In a hectic FFA, a fallback you never revisit is just a sentence written at spawn. If the first route succeeds cheaply, you can invest more there while keeping the alternate open with small claims. If it fails, stop the spending that depends on it before sunk costs persuade you to double down. The [adaptive build order](/guides/adaptive-build-order/) guide covers the next Gold purchase after this placement choice; this page's narrower rule is to keep both a physical route and a responsive reserve alive until one has proved itself.

## Change plans when a World exit closes

The most expensive mistake is treating an initial continent choice as an obligation. The World map contains long coasts, disconnected islands, busy continental interiors, and multiple Nation anchors, so a route can lose value without your army losing a battle. Set a stop signal before spending: an opponent reaches the second pocket first, both routes collapse into the same choke, the home border grows faster than your reserve, or the water landing cannot be followed by useful land. Each signal is visible. If one occurs, compare the remaining two options: defend a compact core while growing capacity, or redirect a bounded force into an independent exit. Do not pay for a third speculative front to avoid admitting that the first one closed.

Opponents can counter this plan by taking the neutral pocket before you, attacking the thin home line while your main force is away, or denying a coastal landing with a Warship or a defended shore. The response is different in each case. Against a race, make a smaller defensible claim or switch direction. Against a home breach, cancel further expansion and protect the return path. Against naval denial, keep the home army and postpone a Transport rather than sending additional waves. If the opposing player has a stronger position, an alliance request may temporarily reduce a front, but it has a clock and is not a permanent map exit. The [threat assessment guide](/guides/threat-assessment/) helps compare that rival with other opportunities after the initial World placement decision.

Mode and map identity can invalidate an example. In random spawn you cannot preselect the illustrated coast; begin the exit check from your assigned tile. In a manual-spawn room you may compare candidates, but another marker or last-second lobby change can take the best pocket. Compact scaling changes travel and available space. A team lobby changes the ownership and teammate-spacing problem, so use [team spawn formation](/guides/team-spawn-formation/) instead of this public-FFA priority order. Giant World Map and World Inverted are separate resources, and the standalone Europe map has a different layout. Do not transfer a favorite coordinate or a video timestamp between them.

The current boundary is the formal [v0.34.11 Release](https://github.com/openfrontio/OpenFrontIO/releases/tag/v0.34.11) and its tagged [World manifest](https://github.com/openfrontio/OpenFrontIO/blob/v0.34.11/resources/maps/world/manifest.json) and [spawn validation](https://github.com/openfrontio/OpenFrontIO/blob/v0.34.11/src/core/execution/SpawnExecution.ts). They establish map identity and rule shape, not a guaranteed spawn ranking. Before committing, say aloud which first pocket you can reach, who can deny it, which second route survives that denial, and what event makes you stop. If you cannot answer the last two, preserve Troops and Gold until the live board supplies an answer. Your next useful choice is a route with a return path, not a continent with a reputation.
