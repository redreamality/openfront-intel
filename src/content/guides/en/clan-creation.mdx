## Direct answer: there is no "create" button; you either keep a fresh tag or get blocked

There is no "create a clan" button anywhere in OpenFront, and that absence is the whole answer to "how do I make one." A clan is formed the moment the server recognizes a clan tag you have claimed as yours and no one else does. The client checks a tag before you enter a match through a function named `checkClanTagOwnership` in `ClanApi.ts`; it first asks a public endpoint whether that tag already belongs to a live clan. If the tag is unknown, the check fails open and you keep it, which makes you the de-facto founder of a clan that did not exist a second earlier. If the tag already belongs to a clan and you are not a member of it, the same check sets a `tag_not_member` error that disables play until you join that clan, a rule introduced in the v32 milestone as issue #3911 and closed on May 23, 2026. The tag itself is constrained to two to five alphanumeric characters, and the game tells you the exact reason when a tag is rejected with three short strings, one for too short, one for too long, and one for illegal characters. So "making a clan" is not a form you fill out; it is a collision test between the tag you type and the tags the server already knows, and your outcome is decided entirely by which side of that test you land on. Hold that frame, because the rest of this guide separates the three genuinely different paths that all pass under the loose phrase "make a clan," the rules that gate each of them, and the match behavior that decides whether a clan is worth making at all. The single most important distinction to internalize before you type anything is that the path where you succeed quietly and the path where you are blocked both run the exact same code; the only difference is whether the tag you chose already existed. That is why the tag is the decision, not the button, and why the next few sections read the error state the client sets rather than hunting for a menu item that is not there. The same ownership check also runs when you re-enter a match after your tag has been claimed by someone else, so a tag that worked on day one can stop working later without you changing anything, and the correct response in that case is the same membership logic, not a retype of a tag you already own. Keep that in mind as you read on: every path in this guide ends at one of two states, a tag you own and can play under, or a tag that belongs to someone else and will not, and the entire rest of the guide is a map of how to land on the first side and recognize the second.

## The three paths: self-serve founder, the blocked reuser, and the listed clan

The first and most common path is the self-serve founder. You type a brand-new, valid tag into your username or clan field, the ownership check finds no live clan behind it, and the check fails open, so the tag becomes yours and, with it, a brand-new clan of one whose leader is you. Nothing is sent to a staff queue, no Discord link is required at founding time, and the clan is live the moment you play. This is the path that answers the literal search "how to make a clan," and it is the one the in-game experience is built around. The second path is the blocked reuser, which is what most confused players actually hit. You type a tag you think is free, or one you saw somewhere, and it turns out to already belong to a clan you are not in; the ownership check then sets the `tag_not_member` error, play is disabled, and you cannot proceed until you are a member of that existing clan. This is the direct effect of the #3911 tag-ownership lockdown: before it, a fresh client could more easily slip a tag that was already taken; after it, reusing a taken tag is an explicit, gated state instead of a silent collision. The third path is the listed or staff-created clan, which is a different object entirely. These are the clans that appear in the in-game browser and the territory listing, each with a name, a description, and usually a Discord link, and they are created on request with a Discord presence rather than by a player simply typing a tag. A listed clan is what you browse and join through the clan section; a self-serve clan is what you found by picking an unused tag. Confusing these two is the single biggest source of "why can't I make my clan" questions, because the browser shows you the listed path while the founder path happens invisibly in your own tag field. The guide on [joining and belonging to a clan](/guides/clan-membership/) covers the membership side of all three, and the table below is the fastest way to tell which of the three you are actually standing in.

| Path | How it happens | What you get | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self-serve founder | Type a fresh, unused, valid tag; the ownership check fails open and you keep it | A new one-member clan where you are the leader with leader-only settings | You want your own clan with no approval step |
| Blocked reuser | Type a tag that already belongs to a clan you are not in; `tag_not_member` disables play | Nothing until you join that clan or pick a different tag | You want that specific clan, or you misread a tag as free |
| Listed / staff-created | A clan is created on request with a Discord presence and appears in the browser and territory listing | A discoverable, joinable clan with a name, description, and Discord link | You want other players to find and join you, not just a private identity |

## Tag rules: two to five alphanumeric characters, and the three rejection strings

Every clan path is gated by the same tag schema, so the tag is the first thing to get right. The rules are enforced by a validation function and a schema in the client, backed by minimum and maximum length constants, and they reduce to one sentence: a valid tag is two to five characters long and contains only letters and numbers. No spaces, no dashes, no underscores, no punctuation, no length below two and none above five. When you get it wrong the game does not show a generic error; it shows the specific reason, and those reasons are three short strings defined in the language files. A tag shorter than two characters produces the "too short" message telling you it must be two to five alphanumeric characters; a tag longer than five produces the "too long" message saying it cannot exceed five characters; and a tag containing a character that is neither a letter nor a number produces the "illegal characters" message saying it can only contain letters and numbers. These three strings are the only format feedback you will get, so they are worth memorizing because there is no second chance for a tag the client rejects before it ever reaches the server. The practical implication is that your tag is also your clan's public identifier in every listing, every member roster, and every in-match team display, which means a two-letter tag is technically legal but hard for a teammate to search for and a five-letter tag is the most the game will ever accept. Because the ownership check runs on this exact tag, a one-character difference between the tag you type and the tag a clan already holds is the difference between becoming a founder and hitting the `tag_not_member` block. Treat the tag as a permanent, case-insensitive public name you are claiming, not a throwaway placeholder, because the client treats it that way and so does the server. If you want a memorable identity, pick it now within the two-to-five window; if you want to find and join someone's existing clan, the same two-to-five rule applies to the tag you are searching for, and the [clan membership guide](/guides/clan-membership/) walks through the browse-and-join flow that uses this same identifier. The table below lists the three rejection strings and the exact input that triggers each one.

| Input | Rule violated | Rejection string the game shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One character, or nothing typed | Shorter than two characters | Too short: must be two to five alphanumeric characters |
| Six or more characters | Longer than five characters | Too long: cannot exceed five characters |
| Any character that is not a letter or a number | Illegal character set | Illegal characters: can only contain letters and numbers |

## The tag-ownership lockdown: what #3911 changed and why you hit it

The most important rule change in the lifetime of clan tags is the ownership lockdown that shipped in the v32 milestone, tracked as issue #3911 and closed on May 23, 2026. Before this change, the boundary between "this tag is free" and "this tag is taken" was less cleanly enforced from the client, and a player could more easily end up typing a tag that was already in use by a clan they had no relationship to, which let a stranger's game show up under a clan's name. The lockdown makes that state explicit and gated instead of a silent collision. The mechanics, as implemented in the client's ownership check, are now a two-step sequence. First, the client calls the public endpoint that reports whether a tag already belongs to a live clan: a response meaning the tag exists routes the check into the ownership branch, a response meaning the tag is unknown routes it into the founder branch, and a null or inconclusive response is treated conservatively rather than failing open. Second, in the ownership branch, the client checks whether you are a member of the clan that already holds the tag. If you are a member, the check passes and you play under that clan exactly as expected. If you are not a member, the check sets the `tag_not_member` error and play is disabled, so you cannot enter a match under a tag you do not belong to. The reason for the lockdown is the same reason every shared-identity system has a reason: a clan tag is a shared name, and letting anyone silently borrow a taken tag corrupts the roster, the listing, and the in-match team display for everyone in that clan at once. The practical effect on you is that the "free tag" you found is no longer a free tag if anyone is already playing under it, and the game now tells you, in effect, "join that clan or pick a different tag" instead of letting you collide. This is why a tag that looked valid and unused can suddenly refuse to work: it is not a format error, it is a membership error, and the only two exits are joining the existing clan or choosing a genuinely fresh tag. The [clan plutonium payouts guide](/guides/clan-plutonium-payouts/) and the broader clan economy are built on the assumption that a clan is a stable, gated identity, and #3911 is the rule that makes that stability real, which is exactly why the payouts and fund features only make sense once the tag is properly owned.

## Team, FFA, and what a clan actually does in a match

Once you hold a valid tag, the next question is what that tag changes in the game, and the answer is different for team games than for free-for-all. In team modes, players who share a clan tag are grouped together as a unit, and the result is presented as a single outcome for the clan when it plays as one side, which is why a clan's game history can show a clean victory or defeat even when several of its members were in the same lobby. The client's history view is explicit about this: for team or human-versus-nation games the clan surfaces one result when everyone is on the winning side, and it deliberately distinguishes the case where only some of the clan's players won from the case where the clan played as a single unit. In free-for-all and in ranked one-versus-one, the logic flips to per-player outcomes, because in a free-for-all of twenty players the fact that one clan member won is not the same as the clan winning, and the server stamps each clan player's individual result so the history counts exactly who won rather than declaring the whole lobby a victory. This is the "FFA exception" that matters to a founder: a clan tag in free-for-all does not make your teammates, it identifies them, and it does not force you to be on the same side as the other members of your clan. The consequence is that a clan is worth a lot more in the modes where being grouped matters, and it is worth a lot less in the modes where you are alone against everyone anyway. If you founded a clan to play coordinated team matches, the tag is doing real mechanical work for you; if you founded it only to play free-for-all, the tag is mostly an identity badge and a shared history, not an ally. The v36.7 release also added conveniences on the clan screen that do not change these grouping rules but change how you manage the identity: the clan Map tab now opens full screen with a back button and a side panel visible to every member, and a dice button by the name field can pick a random name with a tribe-style or anonymous preference. These are navigation and naming conveniences, not new combat bonuses, which is worth keeping straight when you read the release notes and do not want to over-read a convenience as a power change.

## Failure modes and the counter for each

Most "I can't make my clan" threads are one of a small number of failures, and each has a specific counter rather than a universal fix. The first is the format failure: the tag is one character, or six characters, or contains a dash or a space, and the game returns one of the three rejection strings. The counter is trivial, shorten it or lengthen it into the two-to-five window and strip every character that is not a letter or a number, because the schema accepts nothing else and there is no workaround for a tag it rejects before it reaches the server. The second is the membership failure, which is the one the lockdown introduced: the tag is a valid format but it already belongs to a clan you are not in, and play is disabled by the `tag_not_member` error. The counter is either to join that existing clan through the browser, which is the right move if that is in fact the clan you wanted, or to pick a genuinely fresh tag that the public endpoint reports as unknown, which is the right move if you wanted your own clan. The third failure is the wrong-path confusion: you are looking in the browser for a "create" button and cannot find one, because the browser only lists clans that already exist and the founder path does not use the browser at all. The counter is to stop looking for the button and to realize that typing an unused tag into your own field is the creation act, and that the browser is for the join path, not the founding path. The fourth failure is the inconclusive check: the endpoint that reports whether a tag is taken returns a null or uncertain response, and the client treats that conservatively rather than failing open, which can feel like a tag that should have worked does not. The counter is to wait a moment and retry, or to try a different tag, because an inconclusive response is a transient state, not a permanent block, and it is the one failure that does not correspond to anything you did wrong. The fifth failure is the identity mismatch after founding: you keep changing the tag or the display name and your earlier games, your roster, and your listing no longer line up, which is a management problem rather than a creation problem. The counter is to treat the tag as permanent and to change the display name deliberately, because the tag is the stable key that every listing, roster, and in-match display hangs off of. Each of these has a cause that is visible in the error state the client sets, so the skill is learning to read which of the three tag strings or which membership state you are in, and then applying the matching counter rather than guessing at a fix that does not address the actual gate.

## Two scenarios: a solo player founding a five-letter tag and a team that hit a taken one

The first scenario is a solo player who wants their own clan and picks a five-letter tag, "ZETA9", which has never been used. They type it into their clan field, the client runs the ownership check, the public endpoint reports that no live clan holds "ZETA9", the check fails open, and the player keeps the tag. The clan now exists with exactly one member and that player is the leader, with leader-only access to the settings that a later member would not have, including the name, the description, the Discord link, and the open flag that controls who can join. Nothing was queued, nothing was approved, and the next time the player enters a match the team display shows "ZETA9". The decisions this player actually made were three and they are worth naming: the format decision, keeping the tag within the two-to-five alphanumeric window; the identity decision, accepting that a five-letter tag is the most the game allows and is easier for a future teammate to search for than a two-letter one; and the path decision, choosing the self-serve founder route over browsing for an existing clan. The second scenario is a team of three who agree on the tag "WOLF" and each type it in on day one. The first player's check finds no clan behind "WOLF" and fails open, so they become the founder. The second and third players then type the same tag; by the time their checks run, "WOLF" already exists and they are not yet members, so they hit the `tag_not_member` error and cannot play under it until they are added. The counter is that the founder opens the clan's settings, marks it as open, and the two teammates join through the browser using the same "WOLF" tag, after which all three play under one clan in team mode and the history groups them as a single unit. The decision point the team hit is exactly the one the lockdown exists to surface: the tag is free only until the first person claims it, and everyone else must go through membership. The contrast between the two scenarios is the lesson. A solo founder can pick any unused tag and is done, but a team has a sequencing problem, because the founder must exist before the others can join, and the `tag_not_member` state is not a bug to fight but the intended gate that forces the founder to make the clan joinable. The [guide to setting up a private or custom game](/guides/create-private-custom-game/) covers the related decision of how to get a specific group of players into the same lobby, which is the natural next step once a clan's members can actually find one another by tag.

## Modes, maps, and when founding a clan is worth it

The final decision is not whether you can make a clan, you almost always can, but whether it is worth it for the way you play. In team modes on larger maps, a clan is worth a lot, because the tag groups you with your regulars, the history tracks the clan as a unit, and the in-match display lets teammates find each other without trading exact usernames. On the maps and modes where coordination is the main lever, the identity a clan provides is a real asset, and the full-screen clan Map tab from v36.7, with its side panel visible to every member, makes it easier to keep the whole clan pointed at the same plan. In free-for-all, the calculus is different: the FFA exception means your clan tag identifies you and builds a shared history but does not put you on a team, so a clan in free-for-all is primarily a social and identity structure, and the value you get is the roster, the shared game history, and the ability for regulars to spot each other, not a mechanical advantage. On the largest maps, where a lobby can hold a very large number of players, a clan's shared identity is what lets a small group of regulars act like a single coordinated player, which is exactly the situation a clan is designed for. The honest boundary is that a clan does not change any combat number, any economy rate, or any map rule; it changes who you can group with, what shows in your history, and how easy it is for a team to find and coordinate with itself. So the right time to found one is when you have a repeatable group of players you want to identify and track, and the wrong time is when you are expecting the tag itself to make you stronger. If your goal is a listed, discoverable clan that other players can browse and join rather than a self-serve identity, the listed path with a Discord presence is the one to pursue, and the membership guide covers how to join one when you are on the other side. The founder path, the blocked-reuser path, and the listed path each answer a different version of the question "how do I make a clan," and the only one you cannot do is pretending there is a button. There is no button. There is a tag, a check, and a decision about which of the three paths you want to be on, and that decision is the whole thing.
