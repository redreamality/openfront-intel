Rio de Janeiro is the new v0.35 map that gives every lobby a single all-Brazilian nation set of 30 real neighbourhood names, and it plays at a 75-player base because its 1,491,794 land tiles round the formula to a 75 base. In two-team mode the 1.5× team multiplier is capped at that 75 limit, so teams run 75/75/60 by tier rather than a bigger board, and compact mode thins every figure to a quarter (18/13/10 FFA, 18/18/15 teams). The spawn layer is a hard west/east split at x=744 on the 1488-wide map, with the bay islands sitting between the halves, and a v0.35.5 river fix now drains the west-side rivers into Guanabara Bay instead of dead tiles. The decisions that matter are: which mode you queue (FFA density versus team side-selection), which half you spawn into, and whether a compact board suits your map. Pick your side before the countdown ends and you keep the bay-pivot choices that the other side loses.

## Version boundary: what the v0.35 Rio de Janeiro map actually is

Rio de Janeiro is a new map that ships in the v0.35 series, and the version boundary is the first thing to pin because it decides which facts are real and which are carried over from an older patch. The map is absent at tag v0.34.25, the last release of the previous series, and it is present at v0.35.0, the first release of the new series, where its manifest already carries 1,498,664 land tiles. By v0.35.9, the latest formal release, the count has settled at 1,491,794 land tiles on a 1488-by-1680 canvas, which is a 0.5% adjustment to the coastline between the beta and the final tag. The v0.35.9 release body lists it explicitly under "New maps" with the line "Rio de Janeiro: Guanabara Bay, 30 nations from Rio's neighbourhoods and municipalities," which is the first-hand statement that this is a v0.35 addition and not a rename or a port of an older map. That sentence also carries two other facts that the rest of this guide leans on: the map has a 2-team spawn layout, and the historical v0.35.5 entry records the river fix for rivers that were "cut off from the ocean."

Every number in this guide is taken from the v0.35.9 tag source, not from the beta or from community recollection. The manifest at that tag reports 1,491,794 land tiles out of a 2,500,160-tile canvas, which is a 59.7% land fraction — denser than a pure ocean map and close to a full land map, which is exactly what a city-and-bay map should be. The same manifest reports 30 nations, every one of them carrying the Brazilian flag, so there are no unflagged overflow names the way a sparse map would have; the entire board is a named civic layer. The map also ships three pre-baked resolutions (full 1488-by-1680, a 4x map at 744-by-840 with 368,670 land tiles, and a 16x map at 372-by-420 with 90,245 land tiles), and the 4x resolution is the one that matters for the 2-team layout because it is exactly half the width of the full map, which is how the game splits the board down the middle. The map is tagged with the "new" category and the "south_america" theme, and its in-game translation key is map.riodejaneiro, so the name you see in the lobby list is the one that resolves to this manifest.

The practical consequence of pinning to v0.35.9 is that any guide or video that quotes a Rio lobby from the v0.35 betas may be off by a tile-count or a formula change, and the only safe statement is the one that comes from the final tag. This guide does not carry over any Rio-specific number from an earlier build, and the version boundary is the reason the lobby table below uses the v0.35.9 formula exactly as it is written in the tag source. ## The lobby size the land formula hands you

The single most important number on this map is the lobby size, because it decides how many players you actually face and how dense the nation layer is. The live formula in the v0.35.9 tag source, in the file that handles the multiplayer playlist, derives a base player count from the land-tile count with the expression "max of (the land tiles divided by one million times fifty), rounded to the nearest five, floored at five." For Rio's 1,491,794 land tiles, that expression evaluates to a base of 75, because one million times the ratio is 74.59 and rounding to the nearest five gives 75. That base of 75 is the large-FFA number, and it is the ceiling for every other figure on this map, which is the first thing to understand before you look at the rest of the table.

From that base, the game builds three rolled tiers: the large tier is the base itself at 75, the medium tier is the base times 0.75 rounded to the nearest five, which is 55, and the small tier is the base times 0.5 rounded to the nearest five, which is 40. The roll is 30 percent toward large, 30 percent toward medium, and 40 percent toward small, so a small board is the most common lobby you will actually land in. Free-for-all mode uses the rolled tier directly, which gives you the 75, 55, and 40 figures. Two-team mode is where the cap matters: the formula multiplies the base by 1.5 and rounds up, but then clips that result at the large-FFA tier. So a 75 base gives a team figure of min(ceil(112.5), 75), which is 75, and the 55 and 40 small tiers give min(ceil(82.5), 75) and min(ceil(60), 75), which are 75 and 60. That is the counterintuitive result: on this map, two-team mode does not produce a bigger board than free-for-all, it produces a board capped at the same 75. Compact mode then shrinks each figure to a quarter, floored at a minimum of three players, which gives 18, 13, and 10 in FFA and 18, 18, and 15 in teams.

| Rio de Janeiro | Large (30%) | Medium (30%) | Small (40%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| FFA | 75 | 55 | 40 |
| 2-Team | 75 | 75 | 60 |
| Compact FFA | 18 | 13 | 10 |
| Compact 2-Team | 18 | 18 | 15 |

The reason the cap clips the 1.5× multiplier is that the multiplier is applied to the base and then compared against the large-FFA tier, and on a map whose base is already 75, the multiplier's result (112) is always above the 75 ceiling. On a smaller map the multiplier would not be clipped, but on Rio it is, which means the team-mode board is bounded by the same 75 that bounds the FFA board. This is the single most-misquoted number on the map, and it is the reason a guide that tells you "team mode gives you 150 players on Rio" is wrong for this map. The full derivation, with the formula written out and the rounding rules explained, lives in the [lobby-size guide](/guides/lobby-size-map-driven/), which is the reference for how the land-tile count becomes a player count across every map in the game. The table above is the Rio-specific instance of that derivation, pinned to the v0.35.9 tag, and it is the number set that every scenario in this guide is built on. If you want to understand why a 75-player board is the right size for a 30-nation map, the [map-strategy guide](/guides/map-strategy/) covers the general density logic, and the compact-mode guide covers how the quarter-shrink works and when you should deliberately queue a compact board instead of a full one.

## The nation layer: 30 Brazilian names with no overflow pool

The nation layer on Rio is the reason the map feels different from a generic flag-pool map: every one of the 30 spawn names is a real Rio neighbourhood or municipality, and every one of them carries the Brazilian flag, so the map presents a single national identity that the players themselves subdivide. There is no overflow pool of unflagged names, no generic "Nation 17" slot, and no mixed-flag board. The 30 names are Copacabana, Ipanema, Leblon, Flamengo, Méier, Tijuca, Niterói, São Gonçalo, Magé, Itaboraí, Maricá, and the rest of the municipal list, plus the three bay islands Ilha do Fundão, Ilha do Governador, and Paquetá. The consequence of a 30-name, all-Brazilian board is that the nation layer is a civic mosaic rather than a military one, and the strategic question is not "which nation is stronger" but "which of these 30 names do I take and which do I leave for someone else."

The 30 names split across the two spawn halves in a way that matters for side selection. Using the nation coordinates from the manifest and the 744-pixel split line, 16 names sit on the west half and 11 on the east half, and the three bay islands (Ilha do Fundão and Ilha do Governador in the west, Paquetá in the east) straddle the line close enough to the middle to act as the pivot between the two team zones. The west half is the dense city-and-beach corridor: the downtown names (Glória, Flamengo, Méier, São Cristóvão, Penha) cluster in the centre, the beach strip (Copacabana, Ipanema, Leblon, São Conrado) runs along the bottom edge, the two bay islands (Ilha do Fundão, Ilha do Governador) sit in the water, and the names further north (Campos Elyseos, Belford Roxo, São João de Meriti, Duque de Caxias) fill out the western coast. The east half is the longer north-and-south coast: Niterói and São Gonçalo sit in the north, Magé, Guapimirim, and Itaboraí run down the eastern shore, Maricá anchors the far south-east corner, Paquetá floats in the bay, and the rest (Suruí, Imbariê, Guia de Pacobaíba, Neves, Sete Pontes, Itambi, Cafubá) fill the eastern and southern periphery. The 16-versus-11 split means the west half has a denser nation layer, which is why the west is the more contested side in two-team play: more names in the same half means more neighbours to fight over, and the beach choke points are where those fights concentrate.

The density of the nation layer relative to the player count is the second key number. With 30 names and a player range of 40 to 75, the average FFA board is roughly 1.3 to 2.5 players per name, which means a full 75-player board leaves about ten names unclaimed at the start and a 40-player board leaves almost twenty. Those unclaimed names are the early game of the map: they are the expansion targets, the free captures, and the reason a Rio FFA does not feel like a 75-player brawl the way a smaller map does. The compact board changes the ratio to roughly one player per two names, which thins the layer enough that expansion into a neighbouring municipality becomes a free move rather than a fight, and that is the mode where the 30-name layout is at its most open. The population-growth guide covers how the nation layer fills up over the course of a match and what an unclaimed name is worth in the midgame, and the v0.35 new-maps overview covers how the 30-nation layout fits into the broader v0.35 trend of denser, more named maps. The point of this section is that the nation layer on Rio is a real, countable, 30-name civic board with no overflow, and the 16-versus-11 west/east split is the first fact you need before you choose a side.

## The two-team west/east split and the river fix

The two-team spawn layout on Rio is a hard vertical split at the x=744 line on the 1488-wide map, which divides the board into two 744-by-1680 halves: the west half from x=0 to x=744 and the east half from x=744 to x=1488. This is not a random line; it is the horizontal midpoint of the full canvas, and it is the line the 30 nation coordinates cleanly straddle (the western names end at x=688 and the eastern ones begin at x=851, leaving the midpoint in open water). The practical consequence is that every nation falls on exactly one side of the line, and the 16 west names and 11 east names from the previous section are the two team zones. In two-team play, your side is the half you spawn into, and the split line is the boundary you are fighting over for the whole match. The bay islands — Ilha do Fundão and Ilha do Governador in the west half, Paquetá in the east half — sit close enough to the middle that they are the pivot: whoever controls the bay islands controls the water route between the two halves, and the bay itself is the contested zone that the split line does not cleanly divide.

The river fix in v0.35.5 is the second fact that changes how the map plays. The release body lists "Fix rivers cut off from the ocean on United States and Rio de Janeiro," and on Rio the fix matters because the map's geography revolves around Guanabara Bay: the west-side rivers drain into the bay, and before the fix some of them ended at a dead tile instead of the water, which meant a river-adjacent nation had no clean path to the coast. After the fix, the west-side rivers now drain into the bay, which means a nation on the western river corridor has a water route back to the coast that it did not have before, and that changes the value of the western river names (Méier, São Cristóvão, Penha) as expansion and retreat options. The bay is the centrepiece of the map for the same reason: it is the water body that the 30 names wrap around, and controlling the bay means controlling the water routes between the west and east halves. The east half is longer and more spread out, so it is the side you want if you plan to expand along the coast and use the water as your retreat route rather than your attack route. ## Decision framework: pick your side and your mode before the countdown ends

The decision that decides a Rio match is the one you make before the countdown ends: which mode you are in, which side of the split you are on, and which of the 30 names you are going for. The framework has three layers, and you work them in order. The first layer is mode, and the choice is between the FFA density game and the team side-selection game. Free-for-all on Rio is a 40-to-75-player board with 30 names, which is a density game where the unclaimed names are the early game and the bay is the midgame pivot. Two-team on Rio is a capped 75/75/60 board with a hard 16-versus-11 split, which is a side-selection game where the west/east geometry is the early game and the bay islands are the contested centre. If you want to play a density game where you pick your own name and expand freely, queue FFA. If you want to play a side-selection game where the map hands you a zone and you fight for the pivot, queue two-team. The second layer is side, and it only applies in two-team mode. The west is the side you want if you plan to fight for the bay islands and the beach strip, because those choke points are in the west half and the islands are the pivot. The east is the side you want if you plan to expand along the coast and use the bay as your retreat, because the longer geometry gives you more room to build before you have to commit. The third layer is name, and it applies in both modes. The framework resolves to a simple rule: pick the mode that matches your preferred game (density versus side-selection), pick the side that matches your preferred geometry (beach choke versus coast retreat), and pick the name that gives you the best early expansion without forcing an early fight. ## Scenario one: a full 75-player FFA on the west beach

The first scenario is a full 75-player free-for-all on the west half, which is the densest board the map produces and the one that most clearly shows what the 30-name layer does at maximum density. You spawn into the west half, which has 16 names and the downtown cluster plus the beach strip, and the board is at the large-FFA tier of 75 players. The player-to-name ratio on this board is 75 divided by 30, which is 2.5 players per name on average, but the distribution is not even: the west names are contested and the east names are open, so the west half runs closer to 3 players per name and the east half closer to 2. The practical consequence is that the west names (Copacabana, Ipanema, Leblon, Flamengo, Méier) are the contested names, and the east names (Niterói, São Gonçalo, Magé, Itaboraí, Maricá) are the open names. Your first decision is whether to take a contested west name and fight for it, or to take an open east name and expand without a fight.

If you take a contested west name, you are buying density: you have the beach choke point, you are next to the downtown cluster, and you are close to the bay islands that control the water route. The cost is that two or three other players are in the same name or the adjacent names, and the first five minutes are a fight for the name itself. The beach choke points (Copacabana, Ipanema, Leblon) are the names where the fight is sharpest, because they are the names with the most neighbours and the most water access. If you take an open east name, you are buying expansion: you have the longer geometry, you are far from the downtown fight, and you can build without an early commitment. The cost is that you are far from the bay pivot, and reaching the bay islands means crossing the split line into the contested zone. The second decision is the side: the west half is the denser, beach-choke side with 16 names, and the east half is the longer, coast side with 11 names. Pick the side that matches your preferred geometry, and understand that the west is the contested side and the east is the open side. The third decision is the name: with 30 names and a 40-to-75 player count, the unclaimed names are the expansion targets, and the names you leave unclaimed are the early game. Pick the name that gives you the best early expansion without forcing an early fight.

The fourth decision is the bay: the bay islands (Ilha do Fundão, Ilha do Governador, Paquetá) are the pivot, and the bay is the contested centre that the split line does not cleanly divide. Whoever controls the bay islands controls the water route between the two halves, and the river fix means the west-side rivers now drain into the bay. The fifth decision is the compact option: the compact board thins every figure to a quarter (18/13/10 FFA, 18/18/15 teams), and the 30-name layer is at its most open in compact mode. The version boundary is the final decision: this map is a v0.35 new map, pinned to the v0.35.9 tag, and every number in this guide is traceable to the manifest, the lobby formula, or the release body at that tag. The Rio de Janeiro map is the v0.35 map that gives every lobby a single all-Brazilian nation set of 30 real neighbourhood names, and it plays at a 75-player base because its 1,491,794 land tiles round the formula to a 75 base. The two-team cap, the west/east split, the bay pivot, and the river fix are the four physical facts that make the map a water-control and side-selection game, and the 30-name layer is the civic mosaic that the players themselves subdivide. The decisions that matter are the mode, the side, the name, the bay, and the compact option, and they are the specific decisions that you carry into your next match.
