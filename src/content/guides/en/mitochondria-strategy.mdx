## Direct answer: pivot when your land is stable but your army is not compounding

**Short version:** In an OpenFront midgame, if you are being pushed on one or more fronts and your military line has stopped producing value while your territory is still mostly intact, the rational move is usually to stop expanding and start building a low-profile industrial network — ports, factories, cities, and the railways that connect them — then hide that network behind cheap defense posts and, if the table is aggressive, a SAM umbrella around a hidden missile silo. You out-earn the table through the factory train-trade loop and convert to a win only once the table stops expanding toward the 80 percent win line, or once an alliance focus gives you the moment to strike. The pivot works exactly when two gates are both open: your territory is stable enough to let the network compound, and your hidden base is big enough that the table would pay more to destroy it than to keep playing. If either gate is closed — you are still losing land every turn, or your base is small and visible — the industrial build is not a comeback, it is a slower way to lose, because the 80 percent win line is measured on tiles you own, and a shrinking player has no compounding surface to invest on.

This is the play the community has a name for. Players in a recurring thread call it the "mitochondria" alt: a tablemate who stops fighting, keeps a small fixed footprint, builds a hidden industrial base, and then suddenly out-earns everyone or wins late. The name is pejorative and it is a community label, not an official OpenFront term, but it is the clearest signal that the strategy is real, recognized, and polarizing. Your job as the player is not to decide whether everyone should do it. It is to know exactly when it wins, what it costs in gold and in the table's attention, and how to make the late conversion actually happen instead of dying small and hidden.

The rest of this guide prices every step against the v0.34 constants, separates the survival gate from the conversion gate, gives two full scenarios with numbers, and lists the failure modes and the map conditions that change the answer.

The call is deliberate rather than defensive: you keep building economy precisely because you are weak, and you hold the army to reserve rather than spending it on failed captures. The moment the economy compounds faster than the enemy's army degrades, you have already won the game that is being decided in the tiles, not the border, and the military situation is only what you choose to spend on afterward.

## What the strategy is, and the version boundary it depends on

The "mitochondria" strategy is a midgame identity switch. Up to the switch you play a normal OpenFront game: you take land, you fight, you lose some buildings. At the switch you stop treating your army as the product and start treating your economy as the product. The army becomes a thin shell whose only job is to not get deleted, and the industrial network becomes the thing that actually grows your share toward the win line. The switch is only meaningful because of three v0.34 facts. First, the win condition is a share of tiles, not net worth: you win by owning 80 percent of the map's usable tiles, and that line is fixed in the rule set unless the room owner adds overtime. Second, the only way to add to that share is to build, and building is where the industrial network pays: every port, factory, and city you add is both an income source and, for cities, a +250,000 troop-cap bump. Third, the industrial line compounds without exposing a new frontline the way an army does: a factory behind your existing tiles produces trains on a `(factories + 10) × 15` tick cycle and a port produces ships, and neither of those requires you to send troops into contested ground.

The version boundary matters because the costs below are not decorative. In v0.34 a Port, a City, and a Factory all cost a base 125,000 gold and scale as `min(1,000,000, 2^n × 125,000)` where `n` is the combined count of ports and factories you already have — and cities share that same `n`, so a City and a Port and a Factory all bid up each other's price. A City adds +250,000 to your troop cap per level and acts as a train station, and it can be built even without a port. A Missile Silo is a flat 1,000,000 gold and takes 100 ticks, and its upgrades shorten reload. A SAM Launcher is 1,500,000 gold (capped at 3,000,000 as you add more), takes 300 ticks, has a 90-tick cooldown, scales from 70 to 150 tiles of range by level, and — critically — the AI nukes avoid SAM-covered targets. A Defense Post is only 50,000 gold (capped at 250,000), covers a 30-tile zone, multiplies defense by 5 and slows enemies by 3, and is destroyed rather than transferred when its tile is captured. All of these are the tools the strategy is made of, and the gold figures are what let you tell a viable pivot from an unfunded one. These constants come from the generated structure data for the pinned build (upstream v34, commit `4e837520`), and the win line and default nuke settings come from the same generated rule set; the community term "mitochondria" is confirmed by the Reddit thread that names it and by multiple strategy videos, and it is presented here as a descriptive label, not an official one.

## The two gates: survival first, conversion second

The single most common mistake is treating the pivot as one decision when it is two. The first gate is survival, and it asks a blunt question: can you keep your territory stable long enough for the industrial network to compound? If the answer is no — if you are losing a net tile every turn, if two or three larger players are actively pushing your borders, if your defense is being out-produced every time you rebuild — then the industrial build is not an option at all, because the win line is measured on tiles you own and a shrinking footprint has no surface to compound on. This is the problem a "how do I even survive until midgame" player is facing, and it is upstream of the pivot: you must first stop the bleeding with cheap defense posts and a disciplined, small, defensible core, and only once your land share is flat or rising do you have the ground to build on.

The second gate is conversion, and it asks the harder question: is your hidden base large enough that the table would pay more to destroy it than to keep playing, and do you have a moment to flip it into a win? This is where the "mitochondria" complaint comes from. A small, visible base that never converts is exactly what the table is annoyed by, because it looks like a passive player who will eventually be focused while it is still small. The base only becomes a threat when it is big enough that focusing it is a real sacrifice for the table — when it has enough factories and cities that the gold and time it would take to delete it is more than the table would gain from deleting it — and only then does the deterrent logic hold. Between the two gates there is a long, boring, and correct stretch where you do almost nothing visible: you build quietly, you keep your footprint small, you let the table talk about you in chat, and you let the network compound. The guide's decision framework later turns this into a concrete checklist, but the core idea is that survival and conversion are different problems with different failure modes, and the pivot only works when you clear both in order.

Practically, clear the survival gate first and verify it: if your army is below roughly the 75% eligibility band of the strongest bordering force and your structures are still exposed, you are not in the conversion game yet, no matter how much you have saved. The temptation is to start the industrial ramp while still losing the border fight, which means the first focus you draw deletes the factories before they have earned anything. Once the survival gate is genuinely clear, the conversion question becomes a pure arithmetic problem about remaining minutes and remaining tiles, and you can answer it with numbers instead of mood.

## The build: what an outgunned player actually constructs

A practical midgame pivot for an outgunned player is a compact industrial core plus a deterrent, not a sprawling second empire. The core is a handful of ports and factories that produce the income stream, a handful of cities that raise your troop cap and act as train stations, and the railways that tie them into a loop so the factory train-trade loop runs continuously. Because ports, factories, and cities share the same scaling `n`, the order and timing of these builds directly control your cost curve: early industrial structures are cheap, and the cost doubles with each one, so a small network built early is far more efficient per gold than the same network built late. That is the whole economic point of the pivot — you are buying a compounding income line at its cheapest point and protecting it while it matures.

The deterrent is the part that makes the small footprint survivable against aggression. A single Missile Silo at 1,000,000 gold is the statement that the table would have to absorb a nuclear response to take you, and because the win line is about tiles not net worth, the silo does not help you win — it only helps you not be deleted, which keeps your compounding surface intact. A SAM Launcher at 1,500,000 gold (300-tick build) is what lets that silo actually function, because the AI nukes avoid SAM-covered targets and the launcher's 70 to 150 tile range by level means a small number of SAMs can umbrella a compact industrial cluster. Around the whole cluster you lay Defense Posts, which at 50,000 gold each (capped at 250,000) are cheap enough to line a 30-tile perimeter, multiply defense by 5, slow attackers by 3, and — because they are destroyed rather than transferred when captured — act as a clear cost the table pays to push you back. The combination is a small, expensive-to-attack, quietly compounding node. If you want the single-structure timing for the port-to-factory hand-off in isolation, the guide on when to frontload port and factory investment covers that hand specifically; here the point is the cluster, not one structure.

The ordering matters more than any single building. A factory placed before the port that would feed it earns nothing extra; a city placed where it cannot be defended adds cap but also a target. Build the port and the factory in the same small cluster so the rail link between them is short and cheap, then let the cluster compound on itself. Every additional factory multiplies the whole cluster's output against the shared 2^n cost curve, so the marginal building is always the one that extends a connected cluster, not one that starts a new, disconnected outpost far from your rail backbone.

## Scenario one: a small player at 12 percent on a 50-tile map

Assume a 50-tile usable map. Three players hold 25, 22, and 15 percent; you hold 12 percent and are being pushed by the 25 percent player on one border. It is minute 18. Your army is not compounding — every push you send is being traded down by the larger player's deeper defense — but your land share is roughly flat because your defense posts are holding the one contested border. This is the exact condition where the pivot is available: the land is stable enough to compound, and the military line has stopped paying. The conversion target is the 80 percent line, which on a 50-tile map means 40 tiles; you have 6, so you need to add 34 tiles, and you can only add them by building.

A concrete build: two more factories and two more cities to extend the income loop and the train network, a couple of Defense Posts to seal the contested border (each 50,000, so cheap), one SAM Launcher to umbrella the new silo, and one Missile Silo at 1,000,000. The two factories and two cities ride the shared `2^n` curve, so building them while `n` is still low is the efficient point. Over the next several minutes the factory train-trade loop compounds: each factory adds to the `(factories + 10) × 15` tick production, and each city adds +250,000 of troop cap so your thin shell does not have to be a big army to defend the node. By the time the three larger players have spent their gold and attention on each other or on pushing each other's borders, your 12 percent is quietly rising and your node is now expensive enough to attack that focusing it is a real sacrifice. The conversion moment arrives when the table's expansion toward the line slows — that is the minute you push with the troop cap your cities bought you, or you let the 80 percent line catch you on the back of the compounding. The assumption to state plainly: this only works if the 25 percent player is also spending on the other two players and not single-mindedly focusing you; if they are fully focused on you, the pivot becomes a slower loss unless the alliance among the other players shifts to you first.

The read you want out of this scenario is that the small player's survival gate is the binding constraint, not the conversion gate. Until his land share stops falling, every factory is a future target, and the correct move is to spend the cheap defense posts to hold the perimeter and let the cluster mature at zero visible aggression.

## Scenario two: an equal-size player in a stalemate facing an alliance focus

Now assume you are one of four roughly equal players, each around 20 percent, and the table has settled into a stalemate where nobody can push. The threat is not a bigger player; it is a coordinated focus: two of the other players are about to team up and push you while the fourth watches, which is the classic "focus the crown" setup. Here the mitochondria pivot is less about out-earning and more about making the focus not worth it. You shrink your visible footprint to the compact industrial core, you bury a Missile Silo under a SAM umbrella so the AI and the human nukes both have a reason to hesitate, and you line the core with Defense Posts so the two attackers pay 5x defense and 3x slow and lose the posts when they capture tiles. The economic line is still running — the factory train-trade loop keeps producing gold every tick — so while the table argues about who leads the push, your gold and your tile share both keep moving.

The numbers that matter: a Defense Post at 50,000 is cheap enough that you can afford a full perimeter and still fund the industrial core, and because it is destroyed on capture the attackers literally pay gold-equivalent to chew through your line; a SAM Launcher at 1,500,000 with its 300-tick build and 90-tick cooldown is the expensive anchor, so you build it once, early, and upgrade its range toward 150 tiles rather than buying a second; the silo at 1,000,000 is the deterrent that makes the focus a calculated risk for the two attackers instead of a free win. The conversion here is different from scenario one: you do not need to out-earn the table, you need to make the alliance's focus fail or stall long enough that the fourth player (or you, from a stronger position) converts. This is the same decision the table faces in the endgame focus guide on when to join up and bring down a snowballing leader, applied in reverse: you are the node being focused, and your job is to make the focus cost more than it returns. The failure mode is the same as always — if your base is still small and visible when the focus lands, the two attackers delete it before the network matures, and the stalemate breaks against you.

The key read: in a stalemate the binding constraint is not your economy — it is whether you can make the coordinated focus cost more than it returns, which is a deterrence and timing problem, not a production problem.

## When the pivot fails, and how to counter it

The pivot fails in four distinct ways, and each has a different fix. The first is the survival failure: you pivot while still losing land, and the network never compounds because you keep rebuilding the same tiles. The fix is to not pivot until your land share is flat — build the defense-post perimeter and hold a small core first, and treat the industrial build as the second act, not the first. The second is the visibility failure: your base is small and the table sees it, so they focus you while it is still cheap to delete. The fix is the deterrent — the silo under a SAM umbrella and the Defense Post line — which is exactly the cost the table is meant to see and hesitate over; if you are too poor for the full deterrent, you are not large enough to pivot yet, and the correct move is to keep your military line alive longer. The third is the conversion failure: the base compounds but never flips into a win, because you wait for a perfect moment that the 80 percent line does not wait for. The fix is to define the conversion trigger in advance — a specific minute, a specific tile share, or a specific table event like the alliance focus landing on someone else — and commit to it rather than drifting. The fourth is the over-commitment failure: you build the industrial line and the deterrent so heavily that you run out of gold before the win line, and the table watches you go broke. The fix is the cost discipline of the shared `2^n` curve — buy the compounding structures early when they are cheap, and treat the SAM and the silo as the expensive anchors you build once and upgrade rather than duplicate.

The counter to a player running this strategy is the same set of facts in reverse: a visible base can be focused while small, a silo is a single 1,000,000 gold structure that can be captured, and the 80 percent line does not care about your gold. That is why the strategy's entire value is in staying small, staying hidden, and staying funded long enough to convert. It is a real, recognized, and effective OpenFront midgame line — the "mitochondria" alt — and it is the correct answer to a specific situation: a structurally outgunned player whose land is stable and whose army has stopped compounding. It is not the answer to a player who is still losing land, and it is not the answer to a player who has already won the military race. Knowing which of those you are is the whole decision.

## Mode and map adjustments

The pivot is most effective in FFA and large-room games, where there are enough independent players that the table will spend its attention on each other and leave you room to compound. In a 1v1 or a tight team game the pivot is much harder to use, because there is no "table" to let your network mature against — the single opponent or the coordinated enemy team will focus you while your base is still small, and there is no third party the win line can drift toward. If you are playing a team game, the industrial line is still valuable as the team's compounding base, but the individual hidden-node version of the strategy loses most of its value, and the decision becomes a team decision about who holds the industrial core while the team's frontline holds the map.

Map shape changes the answer in concrete ways. On a map with a natural central cluster or a tight landmass, the industrial node is easy to umbrella with SAMs and defense posts because the perimeter is short, and the pivot is stronger. On a sprawled, wide map where your territory is a long thin ribbon, the perimeter is long, the Defense Post line is more expensive, and the SAM range of 70 to 150 tiles may not umbrella the whole node — in that case the pivot is weaker and the survival gate is harder to clear. A map with few usable tiles raises the effective share each tile represents, so the 80 percent line is closer in tiles but also easier for a single push to cross, which means the conversion window is shorter and you must trigger the conversion earlier. A map with a lot of open water favors the port side of the build, because ports produce the ships and the naval line that a small node can use to project onto a wide ribbon without a land frontier. The tall-versus-wide reading of your footprint — whether to keep a dense deep core or a spread thin one — is exactly the variable that decides which of these map conditions you are in, and the guide on tall versus wide footprints covers that trade in depth. The practical rule is to choose the map-aware variant of the pivot: umbrella a short perimeter on a tight map, and on a wide map either skip the hidden-node version or invest more in the defensive perimeter before you commit to the industrial line.

## Decision framework and the late conversion

Treat the pivot as a checklist you run at the top of the midgame, not a personality you adopt. Gate one, survival: is my land share flat or rising, with my defense holding the contested borders? If no, do not pivot; hold the core and stop the bleeding first. Gate two, funding: is my gold curve on the cheap side of the shared `2^n` cost, and can I afford the factory/city core plus at least one SAM and one silo? If no, keep the military line alive longer and pivot later when `n` has not run away. Gate three, deterrence: do I have the silo under a SAM umbrella and the Defense Post perimeter, so that focusing me costs the table real gold and a real nuke risk? If no, you are not a threat yet and you are exactly the "mitochondria" player the table is annoyed by. Gate four, conversion: have I defined the trigger — a minute, a tile share, or a table event like the alliance focus landing on someone else — and am I committed to it? Only when all four are green is the pivot a comeback rather than a slower loss.

The late conversion is the step most players forget. The win line is 80 percent of tiles, and it does not wait; a hidden base that compounds but never triggers is a base that loses. So you define the conversion trigger before you start, and it is usually one of three things: the table's expansion toward the line has slowed enough that you can push into the open tiles with the troop cap your cities bought you; the alliance focus lands on a player other than you, and you convert while the table is split; or your own tile share crosses the threshold where the 80 percent line is within reach of your compounding rate in the remaining minutes. The industrial guide on how many factories you actually need gives you the production math to check whether your remaining minutes can cover the tiles you are short, and the threat-assessment guide gives you the read on whether the table is about to focus you or each other. The pivot's whole value is in the gap between "you are outgunned now" and "the table stops expanding," and the conversion is how you turn that gap into a win instead of a quiet, hidden, well-funded loss.
