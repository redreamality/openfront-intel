**Direct answer.** Vancouver Island is the v0.35 board with the most land and the fewest
named players per square mile: 32 nations on 2,012,664 land tiles, and under the new
land-driven sizing it rolls a 100-player large lobby (75 medium, 50 small) with 150 in
team mode. Commit to the west coast's deep inlets for defence or the open north shore
for aggression, hold one named nation plus its two neighbours, and treat the three
unflagged First Nations as neutral ground you must win, not claim. Read the lobby tier
first, because a 100-player island is a different game from the compact eight-nation one.

Vancouver Island is the map that lets v0.35's two biggest mechanical changes meet in one
lobby. The first is the nation layer, a board with thirty-two named nations on the main
island, twenty-six of them flagged as British Columbia, two flagged as Washington on the
American side of the strait, one flagged as Vancouver, and three First Nations entities
that carry no flag at all. The second is the land-driven lobby sizing that replaced the
flat one-hundred-and-twenty-five cap, which means this is one of the biggest public
lobbies the game can now produce. The map is wide, the water is full of inlets and
peninsulas, and the board's geography decides your match before the first unit is built.
This guide gives you a repeatable framework for the four decisions the map actually
forces: which tier you are dropped into, which side of the island to commit to, which
named nation to anchor on, and how to play the three flagless nations that sit in between.

## Version boundary: what v0.35's Vancouver Island actually is

The single most important thing to pin down before you plan a spawn is that Vancouver
Island is a v0.35 map and it carries the two v0.35 behaviours that reshape every board it
appears on. It shipped in the v0.35.0 formal release on October 16, 2026, and the official
release notes describe it in one line as "30+ nations from the Pacific Northwest," which
is the upstream's own shorthand for a wide, nation-rich coastal board. It is not in the
v0.34 stable playlist, so if you are on a stable build the map does not exist for you, and
if you are on a hosted lobby a player can still pick the older map set where it never
appears. The version boundary matters for two reasons. First, the nation layer on this
board is the v0.35 nation system, the one where nations behave like the humans who win,
save later, fortify borders, and send small beachhead boats before pushing by land, so
the nations on Vancouver Island are not the passive filler of the older builds. Second,
the lobby size you get on this map is the v0.35 land-driven size, not the old
one-hundred-and-twenty-five clamp, which is the reason a full Vancouver Island lobby can
reach a hundred players where the same board would have been pinned to a flat ceiling on
stable.

The second boundary to understand is the data. The numbers in this guide come from the
upstream manifest at the v0.35.0 tag, which is the authoritative source for the board's
shape: a two-thousand-six-hundred by one-thousand-nine-hundred-eighty grid, two million,
twelve thousand, six hundred and sixty-four land tiles on the full
resolution map, and the `map4x` and `map16x` entries that are zoom renderings of the same
board, not separate maps. The full-resolution count is the one the lobby formula
consumes. The v0.35.0 release is the floor, and the current release cursor as of the date
of this guide is v0.35.6, so the map has been live for several patch releases. Nothing in
the v0.35.x patches has changed the manifest's nation count or land figure, which is why
the numbers hold across the patch range, but if a future v0.35 release reworks the map
geometry or the nation list, re-check the manifest before you reuse the spawn logic in
this guide. The boundary is simple to state: on v0.35, this board is wide, nation-rich,
and big; on anything older, it is not there at all.

## The lobby size the land formula hands you

Under the v0.35 land-driven sizing the lobby you land in is not a guess and it is not a
flat number; it is the output of a small formula applied to the map's land-tile count, and
Vancouver Island's two million land tiles put it squarely in the largest tier the formula
produces. The formula takes the land-tile count, multiplies it by fifty per million
tiles, rounds to the nearest multiple of five, and never lets the base fall below five.
That base is the map's largest size tier, and the medium and small tiers are derived from
it by multiplying by zero-seventy-five and zero-fifty and rounding each back to the
nearest multiple of five. Apply it to two million, twelve thousand, six hundred and sixty-four land tiles and the
large tier comes out to one hundred players, the
medium tier to seventy-five, and the small tier to fifty. In team mode the game multiplies
the FFA tier by one-and-a-half, so the same board rolls one-fifty large, one-thirteen
medium, and seventy-five small.

| Team | 150 | 113 | 75 | FFA tier x 1.5 |
| Compact map mode | — | — | — | uses 25% of nations (8), not land |
| Hosted lobby | host-set | host-set | host-set | governed by hosting rules, not this formula |

The reason the tier matters more than the player count is that the tier is the shape of
the board you are actually dropped into. A one-hundred-player Vancouver Island is a
board where every named nation has to absorb a cluster of human players, where the coast
is thick with overlapping claims, and where the inlets that defend a spawn are contested
from the first minute. A fifty-player version is the same board with half the pressure,
where a single named nation can be held by one or two players and the interior stays
quiet long enough to build a real economy. A one-fifty team lobby is a different species
entirely, because two teams of up to seventy-five have to split the island between them
and the fight is for the middle, not for a corner. The practical rule is to read the
lobby tier from the lobby list before you commit to a spawn, because the same physical
board plays as three different games depending on which tier you are in, and the spawn
that is correct in a fifty-player island is often the wrong one in a one-hundred-player
one. The compact map mode is the exception to the land rule: it ignores land and uses
twenty-five percent of the manifest's nations instead, which on this board is eight
nations, and that is a board so small and so dense that the wide-island logic in the rest
of this guide stops applying.

## The nation layer: 32 nations, 30 overflow names, three unflagged

The nation layer on Vancouver Island is the part of the board that is easiest to
misread, so it is worth stating exactly what is on it and what is not. The manifest
carries thirty-two named nations on the main island. Twenty-six of them carry the British
Columbia flag, which is the visual and mechanical signature of the board: the majority of
the named nations are BC place names, which is why the release notes call it a
Pacific-Northwest board. Two carry the Washington flag, Clallam County and San Juan, and
they sit on the American side of the Strait of Juan de Fuca, which means the board does
not stop at the Canadian border and two of your named neighbours are stateside. One
carries the Vancouver flag, and that is the single metro-name nation that anchors the
southern tip of the island. Then there are three nations that carry no flag at all: the
Kwakwaka'wakw Nation, the Coast Salish Nation, and the Nuu-chah-nulth Nation. These
three are visually distinct on the map and in the player list because they render without
a flag, and they are the board's unique feature, the three entities that do not belong to
any of the three flag groups and that sit in the interior and along the coast as
flagless, neutral-looking powers.

The second thing to state is what the thirty overflow names are and are not. The manifest
also lists thirty `additionalNations`, all flagged British Columbia, with names such as
Whistler, Tsawwassen, and Ladysmith. These are not active nations in a default public
lobby. They are the overflow pool the engine draws from only when a lobby's requested
nation count exceeds the thirty-two the manifest defines, and in a standard public
lobby the nation count is matched to the players or capped at the manifest size, so the
overflow names usually do not appear. The release notes' "30+ nations" line is the
upstream's own summary of the main-island nations plus this overflow pool, and the
number thirty-two is the count of nations that are actually on the board in a normal
game. The practical consequence is that you should plan your spawn around the thirty-two
named nations, treat the three flagless ones as the ones to watch, and not assume that a
BC overflow name like Whistler is a nation you will see unless the lobby is forced to a
nation count above thirty-two. If you do see one, it is a signal that the lobby is a
custom-nation-count one, and the board is denser than the default, which changes the
spread of your claim and the number of named neighbours you have to account for.

## Decision framework: pick your side before the countdown ends

The decision framework on Vancouver Island is a four-step read you run in the lobby
before the countdown ends, and it is the same four steps whether you are in a fifty or a
hundred-player lobby. The first step is to read the tier. Look at the lobby list and
note whether the island is rolling at fifty, seventy-five, one-hundred, or the team
variants, because the tier sets the density and the density sets everything that follows.
The second step is to read the sides. The island has a deep, inlet-heavy west coast, a
more open and exposed north and east shore that faces the strait and the mainland, and an
interior of named BC nations that sit between the two. Name the three or four named
nations nearest your side and note which of them is flagless, because a flagless nation
near your spawn is neutral ground that can be won by anyone and is usually the first
target of the player who reads the board. The third step is to read the inlets. The west
coast inlets are the board's defensive geography, the long narrow arms of water that
cover a spawn and make a landing expensive, so if you want a defence-first game you anchor
on the inlet that protects your named nation and use the water as your first wall. The
fourth step is to read the named anchor. Pick one named nation as your anchor, the one
you will actually hold, and name the two neighbours on either side of it, because on a
wide board the player who knows their two neighbours is the player who is never surprised
by a flank, and the player who does not is the one who loses the interior.

The framework has a default and the default is the west coast inlets in a small-to-medium
lobby and the open north shore in a large lobby. In a fifty or seventy-five-player island
the board is quiet enough that the defensive inlets hold, so you anchor a named BC nation
on the west coast, take the inlet in front of it, and expand along the coast into your two
named neighbours while the interior stays unclaimed long enough to build. In a
one-hundred-player island the west coast is too crowded for a pure defence, because the
inlets are contested from the first minute and the defensive bonus of the water is spent
keeping your own spawn instead of holding it, so the correct default shifts to the open
north shore, where you anchor a named nation on the exposed coast and play an
aggression-first game, expanding inland and using the open shore to screen the strait
crossings. The flagless nations change the read in both cases, because whichever side you
pick, the flagless nation nearest you is the neutral target, and the player who takes it
first gets a second economy and a second spawn that the rest of the board then has to
contest. The four steps are the whole of the pre-game decision on this board, and the
player who runs them in the lobby is the player who knows, before the countdown ends,
which side, which inlet, which anchor, and which flagless target they are committing to.

## Scenario one: a full 100-player FFA on the west coast

The first scenario is the one that defines the map: a one-hundred-player free-for-all
where you landed on the west coast, the default in a smaller lobby but the wrong call
here. The assumption is that you are at full density, one hundred players on two million
land tiles, and you anchored a named BC nation on the inlet-heavy west coast because it
looked defensible. The problem with that call at this density is that the inlet that
defends your spawn is now contested by six to eight other players who made the same
defensive read, and the water that was your first wall is now a lane that everyone is
fighting for. The concrete play is to abandon the pure-defence read in the first three
minutes. You hold your named anchor, you take the one inlet that protects it, and you do
not try to hold the whole coast, because holding the whole coast at one hundred players
means a unit on every inlet and a unit on every inlet is a unit not funding your
economy. The numbers that matter here are the two: your anchor nation and the two named
neighbours on either side of it. You expand into those two, you claim the port or factory
on your side before you commit a unit to the inlet lane, and you keep one screen on the
nearest crossing where the American-side Washington nations push. The flagless nation
nearest you is the target you take in the midgame, because at one hundred players the
flagless nation is the one everyone is eyeing and the player who takes it first gets a
second economy that the rest of the board then spends the game contesting. The failure
here is the spread: the player who wins the inlet and then spreads along the whole coast
gives the other players a free crossing through the thin lane, and the counter is the
concentration, which is to keep your units on your two named neighbours and the flagless
target and not to send a unit to the far coast unless the far-coast player is the one
funding the crossing. The scenario is the whole of the large-lobby west-coast game: hold
the anchor, take the nearest flagless target, expand into the two named neighbours, and
concentrate instead of spreading. The timing that decides the scenario is the first
three minutes, because the inlet you do not take before the other players' economy
starts funding it is the inlet you cannot take at all once the crossing it protects
turns into a contested lane. If you are forced to cross the strait early to reach the
flagless target, the crossing costs a unit and the time it takes to ferry it, and the
player on the far side who pre-funds the crossing lane is the one who charges you that
cost twice.

## Scenario two: the compact eight-nation board

The second scenario is the one that most players do not expect, because the same physical
board can show up in the compact map mode and play as a completely different game. The
assumption is that you are in the compact map mode, which ignores the land formula and
uses twenty-five percent of the manifest's nations instead, and on this board that is
eight nations. The board is now eight named nations on the same island, which means the
wide-island logic stops applying and the game becomes a dense, eight-player contest where
every named nation is a neighbour of every other and the interior is claimed from the
first minute. The concrete play is the opposite of the wide-board default. You do not
anchor a west-coast inlet for defence, because with eight nations the inlets are not
defensive, they are just water between two neighbours who are both going to push you.
You anchor the named nation with the most named neighbours, the one in the middle of the
board, because at eight nations the centre is the economy, the centre is where the ports
and factories cluster, and the centre is where the flagless nation sits. The numbers
that matter here are small and specific: eight nations, one flagless target, and the two
or three named nations that share a border with your anchor. You take the flagless nation
first, because at eight nations it is the neutral prize that everyone converges on and
the player who takes it gets a second spawn that the seven others then have to fight
over. You expand into your two or three named neighbours and you do not spread, because
at eight nations the spread is the whole board and there is no far coast to ignore. The
failure here is the over-expansion: the player who takes the centre and then pushes into
all three neighbours at once is the player who is flanked on two fronts, and the counter
is to take the flagless target, hold the centre, and expand into only the two neighbours
who are funding their own economy. The scenario is the whole of the compact game: eight
nations, take the centre, take the flagless target first, and expand into two neighbours,
not three. The compact board is also the one where a wrong first move is hardest to
undo, because the moment you are surrounded there is no open water to fall back across;
the wide board at least leaves a second coast, while the eight-nation board leaves you
the one neighbour you did not expand into, and that is the player who then decides your
midgame for you.

## Failure modes and opponent counterplay

The failure mode that ends most matches on Vancouver Island is the spread, and it is the
same spread on the wide board and the compact board, only at different scales. On the wide
board it is the player who wins the inlet or the coast and then spreads units along the
whole shore instead of concentrating on the two or three named neighbours and the
flagless target, and the counter to that spread is the concentration, which is the reason
the decision framework asks you to name your anchor and its two neighbours instead of
reading the whole coast. On the compact board it is the player who takes the centre and
then pushes into all three neighbours at once and is flanked on two fronts, and the
counter is to expand into two neighbours, not three. The spread also fails the economy on
both boards, because a unit on the far coast or a third front is a unit not funding your
anchor, and the anchor is where the match is decided. The practical fix is the same on
both scales: keep the main screen on the named neighbour or the inlet that matters, use
the flagless target as the second economy, and do not send a unit to a second or third
front unless that front is the one funding the crossing or the push.

The second failure mode is the flagless neglect, which is the player who sees the three
flagless nations and treats them as unimportant because they have no flag, when in fact
they are the neutral prize on the board and the first target of the player who reads it.
The counter to the flagless neglect is the flagless-first rule, which is to name the
flagless nation nearest your anchor in the lobby and plan to take it in the midgame,
because the flagless nation is the second economy and the second spawn, and the player
who takes it first has the board's most contested prize. The third failure mode is the
strait neglect, which is the player who plays the interior and forgets the two
Washington-flagged nations on the American side of the strait, and the counter is to
keep one screen on the strait crossing, because the Washington nations are the only
neighbours who are not BC and they are the only direction you did not read in the
framework. The opponent counterplay on this board is the flagless grab, which is the
player or team that takes the flagless nation before you can, and the counter to that is
the same flagless-first rule run one minute earlier. The three failure modes and the one
counterplay are the whole of the map's tactical content, and the player who can run them
is the player who wins the island.

## Mode and map adjustments

The mode you are playing changes the Vancouver Island read in a small number of ways, and
the map itself changes the mode reading in a few more. The first mode adjustment is the
team split. In a team lobby the island is split between two teams, and the decision
framework changes from which side you pick to how the team divides the side. In a
one-fifty team lobby each team has up to seventy-five players, which is more than the
thirty-two named nations, so the team has to assign named nations to players and the
fight is for the middle of the island, the strait, and the flagless target. The team
framework is the same concentration logic as the FFA, applied to the team's half of the
board: the team anchors a set of named nations, takes the flagless target on its side, and
concentrates on the middle instead of spreading along the whole shore. In a smaller team
lobby the team has fewer players per named nation, so the team can cover more of its side
and the spread failure is less likely.

The second mode adjustment is the nuclear tools. The ports and factories on the coast and
the named nations on the interior make the strait and the inlets nuclear targets, and
the nuclear reading on this board is the same as on any water map, which is that the
crossing is the target and the inlet is the screen. The Vancouver Island version is that
the inlet is a long narrow arm of water, which makes a nuke on the inlet more effective
than a nuke on open coast, because the nuke destroys the one screen the defender has in
that arm. The counter to the nuclear crossing is the second economy, which is the
flagless target and the named neighbour, because the nuke destroys the screen on one
inlet and the crossing still has to cross the next named nation, so the named nations
extend the defence beyond the nuke's radius. The practical nuclear rule is to keep the
flagless target and the named neighbours alive, because the nuke destroys one screen and
the named nations stop the crossing.

The third mode adjustment is the Doomsday Clock. The Doomsday Clock on this board is the
same as on any map, which is that the under-forty-percent side is fighting to survive the
wave, but the Vancouver Island reading is that the under side is usually the side without
the flagless target, because the side without the flagless target is the side without the
second economy. The Doomsday reading is the same as the concentration reading, which is
that the side with the flagless target is the side that can keep expanding to the last
economy, so the under side has to force the flagless side to spend units on the screen.
The map adjustment is the same as the mode adjustment, because the wide board is the mode
on this island, and the mode changes the reading of the three things above, the team
split, the nuclear tools, and the Doomsday Clock. The player who can read both the mode
and the map is the player who plays the island correctly, and the island is the one that
decides the match.

## What to take into your next match

The single takeaway from Vancouver Island is that the board is wide and the decision is
the side, and the side is decided by the tier. The map is two million land tiles with
thirty-two named nations and three flagless ones, and the match is decided by which side
of the island you commit to before the countdown ends. The decision framework is the
tool, which is to read the tier, read the sides, read the inlets, and read the named
anchor, and the scenarios are the two ways the match plays out, the one-hundred-player
FFA on the west coast and the compact eight-nation board. The failure modes are the three
ways the match is lost, the spread, the flagless neglect, and the strait neglect, and the
counterplay is the one way the opponent wins, the flagless grab. The mode and map
adjustments are the last piece, which is that the mode changes how the team splits the
side and how the nuclear tools and the Doomsday Clock read, and the map changes where the
sides, the inlets, and the named anchor are.
