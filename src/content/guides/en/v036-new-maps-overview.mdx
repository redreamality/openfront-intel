## Direct answer: what is the v0.36 new maps overview?

OpenFront v0.36 added eight maps to the live pool, all carrying the official `new` category in the v0.36.2 tag. They shipped across the v0.36.0, v0.36.1, and v0.36.2 releases, so the v0.36.2 tag source and its release notes are the only complete, verifiable list of the full set. The eight maps are Türkiye, the Ottoman Empire of 1683, Zealandia, Paris, London, Mexico, Canada, and the Brahmaputra. Alongside them the World and World 4000 maps were rebuilt with new terrain and waterways, World 4000 replaces the retired Giant World, and a new World 10000 board was added for custom games and tournaments. The most important structural change for map selection is the new Atlas: a world-map tab in the map picker with a pin for every one of the 100+ real-world maps, where hovering a pin shows its preview, description, and designer and clicking it queues the map.

The decision that matters for a player is which of these you want to actually queue into, and which lobby settings get you there. v0.36 ties the public hosted-lobby player cap to the map, lets a host set the share of the map needed to win from 10% up to 99.9% (80% stays the default), and added named presets for your Create Game and Solo settings. On top of the new terrain, v0.36 also reworked annexation so a nuked-off arm is annexed even when the crater stands between it and the sea, and spectators no longer count toward a lobby's player count. This guide walks through every map, how the Atlas and lobby changes work, what changes per map, and how to choose.

The overview matters because the eight maps split into three distinct bands that play very differently. The city-board band, Paris and London, is the densest field in the set, with 60 and 32-plus nations respectively, and rewards micro play across many narrow frontlines. The history-and-geography band, Türkiye, the Ottoman Empire, Mexico, and Canada, is the macro-heavy middle, where large real-world boards reward patience and layered defense. The single distinctive band, Zealandia and the Brahmaputra, is the quieter, more thematically specific set, one a fictional Oceanian continent and the other a two-team Asian valley you can cross by boat almost anywhere. If you only take one thing from this page, take that split: queue public lobbies to play the city and regional maps, open a custom lobby when you specifically want the World 10000 or a particular board, and use the Atlas to discover what you do not yet know you want to play.

## The eight maps at a glance: region, nations, and category

The single most useful fact about the new maps is their scale and their region tag, because v0.36 no longer exposes a numeric per-map rotation weight in its generated data the way v0.35 did. Where the v0.35 guide could list a `multiplayer_frequency` value for every map, the v0.36.2 map `info.json` files for all eight new maps, and for established maps like World 10000, carry no such field. That is a real change, not an omission: it means the rotation is now organized around the Atlas and the category tags (which regions a map belongs to, and whether it is flagged `new`) rather than an integer weight you can read off the data. You cannot rank the eight new maps by a numeric frequency anymore, so the decision has to be made on region, nation count, and what the board physically rewards.

The nation count matters because it sets the population of the lobby you land in and the spread of starting positions. Paris at 60 nations is by far the densest field on any new map, which pairs with a city layout to produce the most simultaneous frontlines and the fastest early-trade and early-pressure decisions of the eight. The Ottoman Empire at 46 is the largest of the real-history boards and the one with the widest geographic spread, tagged to three continents. Canada at 38 and the Brahmaputra at 37 sit in a mid-high band, with the Brahmaputra additionally carrying two special-team nations that give it a distinct team-oriented texture. London at 32 plus 30 additional nations, Türkiye and Mexico at 30 and 32, and Zealandia at 27 round out the set, with Zealandia being the smallest and the only fictional board. If you want a wide field and longer strategic depth, the 46-to-60 nation maps reward patience and macro planning; if you want a smaller, more controllable set of opponents, the 27-to-32 nation maps give you more predictable matchups.

## How the Atlas picker and the lobby rework change map selection

The new maps arrive alongside a rework of how you find and size lobbies, and the rework is the bigger decision change of the two v0.35 and v0.36 cycles combined. The map picker now has an Atlas tab: a world map with a pin for each of the 100+ real-world maps. Hover a pin and you get its preview, description, and map designer; click it and you pick the map. Every map card also gained its own description and stats. This changes map selection from a flat list you scroll through into a spatial discovery surface, so the question "which new map do I want" is now answered by looking at where a board sits on the world and what its description promises, rather than by memorizing a slug or a frequency. For the eight new maps this means you can find them by geography: zoom to the Mediterranean and you see Türkiye and the Ottoman Empire, zoom to Europe and you find Paris and London, to North America you find Mexico and Canada, to the Indian subcontinent you find the Brahmaputra, and to Oceania you find Zealandia. The Atlas is the single most practical change in the cycle, and it is the tool to open first when you want to explore the new boards.

The second change is the lobby cap. In v0.36.2 the player cap for publicly listed hosted lobbies now depends on the map, like public lobbies, instead of a flat 100; the release notes give the Box as allowing 210 players and small maps allowing fewer. The immediate effect is that high-nation maps like Paris at 60 and the Ottoman Empire at 46 can now fill more of their available nation slots before the lobby is full, which raises early-match tempo and the number of simultaneous frontlines, while a compact map like Zealandia stays small and deliberate. The third change is the custom win threshold: a host can now set the share of the map needed to win from 10% up to 99.9%, with 80% staying the default. On a 60-nation city board a higher threshold produces a longer, more contested endgame, while a lower threshold on a 30-nation board can end a match earlier once one side has consolidated. You can also save your Create Game and Solo settings as named presets from the new Presets button in the modal header, which removes the friction of re-setting these values lobby by lobby. The cleanest read is that v0.36 pushes map selection from a rotation you passively wait for toward a board you actively choose through the Atlas and then shape with the win threshold and lobby cap.

## Reading the regions: Europe, the Middle East, North America, Asia, and Oceania

Paris and London are the two European city boards, and they are the densest fields in the entire set. Paris is a 60-nation board laid over the city limits, with the Seine, the canals, and the main boulevards treated as waterways and the 60 nations placed on the city's quartiers; the release notes credit Théophile and RickD004, and the board also carries a set of landmark points including the Tour Eiffel, the Louvre, and Notre-Dame. London is a 32-nation board of Greater London, with its major roads converted to rivers, thin bridges across the Thames, and 30+ borough nations, plus 30 additional nation slots. Both maps are built around micro play: many narrow frontlines, constant trade-route decisions, and a coastline that is really a city grid rather than a continent. Türkiye is the Europe/Asia board at 30 nations, tagged to both continents, and is the natural stepping-stone between the city boards and the larger history maps. The Ottoman Empire of 1683 is the biggest board in the set at 46 nations, placed at the empire's greatest extent in Europe, with 46 eyalets, sanjaks and vassal states and, pointedly, no Suez Canal, a deliberate historical touch that changes which routes exist between its western and eastern frontiers.

Mexico and Canada are the two North American boards. Mexico is a 32-nation country map with every state flag included, and the board runs from the Pacific to the Gulf of Mexico. Canada is a 38-nation board from the Great Lakes to Ellesmere Island, with Alaska, Greenland and the United States modeled as impassable terrain, so the playable board is bounded by frozen land and you cannot simply run north into more room. Both are macro-heavy: long coastlines, inland frontiers, and enough room to layer defense rather than fight a single line. The Brahmaputra is the Asian board and the only two-team map in the set. It is a 37-nation board of the Assam valley with 30 additional nation slots and two special-team nations, and the river splits the valley in two, north bank against south bank, crossable by boat almost anywhere, which makes the map a coordinated two-team fight across a shared waterway rather than a solo FFA scramble. Zealandia is the single Oceanian board and the only fictional map in the set: a 27-nation invented continent that exists to give the Atlas a recognizable Oceania pin. The practical split for a player is that Europe is where the tempo is highest, North America is where the macro is deepest, Asia is where the team play lives, and Oceania is where the set gets small and self-contained.

## The distinctive boards: city density, the Ottoman Empire, Zealandia, and the Brahmaputra

Paris is the standout of the new set, and not only because of its size. At 60 nations it is the densest field on any v0.36 new map, and the city layout compounds that density: the Seine and the canals break the board into many separate landmasses, the boulevards act as waterways, and the 60 quartier nations plus a set of landmarks mean almost every position is contested from two or three directions at once. That density pairs with the higher public lobby cap to produce more early pressure and more frontlines than any other new map, and it rewards players who can hold depth, reroute trade, and reposition rather than players who commit to a single coastline. Community reaction to the city boards has been split: some players call Paris and London dense and choked, while others treat them as the most interesting boards in the set precisely because nothing is safe. If you want the newest map to simply be the busiest to play, Paris is the answer, and it is the map to queue when you want a long, contested, multi-front endgame shaped by the custom win threshold.

The Ottoman Empire of 1683 is the natural opposite pole: a 46-nation board spread across three continents at the empire's greatest European extent, with the Suez Canal deliberately absent. The absence of Suez is a real mechanic, not a trivia detail, because it removes the shortcut between the eastern and western frontiers and forces routes through the wider Mediterranean, which changes how the 46 eyalets, sanjaks and vassal states connect. The board rewards long planning horizons and layered defense the way the city boards do not, and it is the pick for a player who wants a large, slow, macro-heavy history game. Zealandia is the quiet end of the set. As the only fictional map, at 27 nations, it is the smallest and the most self-contained of the eight, which makes it the right choice for a short, deliberate session or for a team that wants a smaller, more controllable field without the density of the city boards. The Brahmaputra is the final distinctive board and the only two-team map in the set. The river splits the Assam valley in two, with the north bank against the south bank and the Majuli and sandbar islands in between, crossable by boat almost anywhere. That makes it a coordinated two-team fight across a shared waterway, and the two special-team nations give specific positions a structural advantage that a generic opening ignores. If you queue the Brahmaputra expecting a standard FFA map, you will be caught off guard by the two-team texture and the river-crossing options; the counter is to plan around the waterway from the start, treating the crossing points and the special-team positions as the high-value objectives. Together the four distinctive boards cover the spread of the new set: Paris for maximum density and tempo, the Ottoman Empire for maximum macro depth, Zealandia for the smallest deliberate field, and the Brahmaputra for coordinated two-team play across a river.

## Decision framework: which new map should you queue into?

The right map depends on what you want the match to be, and the v0.36 data lets you pick deliberately through the Atlas rather than waiting for the rotation. If your goal is simply to play as much new content as possible with the least friction, open the Atlas and queue public lobbies on the city boards: Paris and London have the highest nation counts, so they fill more of their nation slots under the map-dependent lobby cap and produce the most new-map content per session. If you are playing with a squad and want a map that is team-sized and team-textured, the Brahmaputra is your priority: it is the only two-team map in the set, its river-crossing options and two special-team nations are built for coordinated play, and its 37-nation field plus 30 additional slots gives you a controllable opponent set. If you want a short, sharp, high-attention game, Zealandia at 27 nations is the tightest new map and the fastest to reach a decisive end state. If you want a large, slow, macro-heavy game with a lot of room to maneuver, the 46-to-60 nation maps, Paris, the Ottoman Empire, and Canada, reward long planning horizons and layered defense. If you want to test a specific mechanic, the Ottoman Empire is the one to pick because the absent Suez Canal and the 46-eyalet field make it the most macro-distinct board in the set.

A second axis is how you reach the map, and v0.36 gives you more control than any previous cycle. The Atlas surfaces all eight new maps by geography, so discovery is no longer a matter of which map the rotation hands you. When a specific map matters, for a team practice session, a replay of a layout you already understand, or a test of a spawn strategy, build a custom or private lobby and set the map directly, then shape the game with the custom win threshold: set a higher threshold on Paris or the Ottoman Empire for a longer, more contested endgame, or a lower threshold on a 30-nation board to end a match earlier once one side has consolidated. Save the result as a named preset so you do not have to re-set the threshold and the lobby options lobby by lobby. The map-dependent public lobby cap is worth factoring in before you commit to a spawn, because on a high-nation map like Paris at 60 the lobby can reach a larger, fuller population than on a compact map like Zealandia, and that changes the number of concurrent frontlines and the tempo of the opening. The cleanest rule of thumb is: use the Atlas to discover the new maps and let the city boards do most of the discovery for you, and switch to a custom lobby with a set win threshold once you have a specific map and a specific plan you want to execute.

## Failure modes and how to counter them on the new maps

The most common failure on the new maps is queueing a high-density city board and then misreading the tempo. Paris at 60 nations and London at 32-plus produce more early pressure and more frontlines than a typical regional map, so a player who opens with a slow, low-risk economic build is at a structural disadvantage: the larger field reaches the nuke and MIRV thresholds sooner, and a city grid with the Seine and the boulevards as waterways means your coast is not as protected as it would be on a single-layer regional board. The counter is to front-load at least one port or factory in the first minutes and to hold depth, so your economy can keep pace with a denser field rather than chasing it from behind. A second failure is picking the two-team Brahmaputra without an actual team present; it is the only two-team map in the set, its river-crossing options and its two special-team nations are built for coordinated play, so a solo player who lands on it is more likely to be outnumbered by organized groups across the waterway. The counter is either to queue with allies or to treat it as a higher-risk solo pick and play a more defensive, trade-focused line around the crossing points.

A third failure is underestimating the Ottoman Empire's macro scale. At 46 nations spread across three continents with the Suez Canal absent, the board punishes players who plan for a single short front: the routes between the western and eastern frontiers are longer, the 46 eyalets and vassal states connect more slowly, and a player who commits early to one direction is stretched thin across the rest. The counter is to hold the center, keep trade routes open across the wider Mediterranean, and treat the eyalet and vassal states as a layered defense rather than a single line. A fourth failure is assuming a small, specific map like Zealandia will show up in a short public session; at 27 nations and the only fictional board in the set, it is the smallest and the most self-contained of the eight, so waiting for it in public lobbies can waste a large fraction of a session. The counter is to use a custom or private lobby when a specific board is the goal. A fifth failure is ignoring the win-threshold axis entirely: the v0.36 custom win threshold means the same board plays very differently at 10% versus 90%, and a player who queues Paris expecting an 80% game will find the endgame stretched far longer when a host sets the threshold higher. The final failure is ignoring the map-dependent lobby cap: the Box allows 210 players while small maps allow fewer, so the population of the lobby you land in now depends on the map, and the number of concurrent frontlines on a high-nation map is meaningfully higher than on a compact one.

## Mode and map adjustments, plus what to verify in the client

The v0.36 cycle pairs the new maps with several system changes you should account for before you judge any map. The first is the Atlas, which the release notes describe as a world-map tab in the map picker with a pin for each of the 100+ real-world maps; hovering a pin shows the preview, description, and designer and clicking it queues the map, and every map card also gained its own description and stats. When you open a new map, read its description and stats first, because the Atlas is now the primary surface for deciding what a board promises before you commit to it. The second is the map-dependent public hosted-lobby cap, which in v0.36.2 ties the player cap to the map instead of a flat 100, with the Box allowing 210 and small maps allowing fewer. The cap matters most on the high-nation maps, where Paris at 60 and the Ottoman Empire at 46 can now fill more of their nation slots before the lobby caps, which raises the number of concurrent frontlines and the tempo of the opening. On the low-nation maps like Zealandia at 27, the cap has less direct effect because the map fills its nation slots well before a large lobby cap is reached, so those games keep their tighter, more deliberate pacing. The third is the custom win threshold, a host setting from 10% up to 99.9% with 80% as the default, which changes how long any board's endgame runs.
