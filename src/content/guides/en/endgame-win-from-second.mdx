## Direct answer

If you are not the crown — the single largest board — you do not win by becoming the crown late. You win by becoming the player the field is most afraid to strike, holding the second-best or third-best board with a MIRV reserve, and firing at the exact moment your reserve is bigger than everyone else's combined ability to answer. The non-crown win is a patience-and-arithmetic game: stay just below the threat level that gets you focus-fired, bank gold at 42% regeneration, use the MIRV price ladder (25M, 40M, 55M, 70M...) as a weapon that raises the cost of the final round for your rivals, and commit only when your warhead count is large enough that the crown cannot simply out-spend you. Read this as a decision framework, not a build order: it tells you what to do at each endgame phase when you are second or third, when to hold, when to expand, when to ally, and the precise numbers that say it is time to launch.

Concretely, the entire decision reduces to one number you can read on the map every few minutes: the reserve gap, defined as your bank minus the crown's bank minus the current ladder step. You launch only when that gap is at least one full ladder step of 15M and the win threshold is still above the crown's current land share, because that is the only row where your warheads land before the crown can buy the next answer. The three numbers behind the gap are fixed by v0.35.9 and never change: the ladder starts at 25M and adds 15M per game-wide launch, the win threshold starts at 80% of usable land and drops 0.1% per minute once the match passes the 30-minute mark, and a healthy board you are not fighting regenerates roughly 42% of its bank per minute at steady state. Hold those three numbers in your head and the endgame stops being a fog: you are either above the focus-fire line, in which case you bank and stay unpalatable, or below it, in which case you expand slowly, ally if you are the smaller board, and survive, and the moment your reserve gap crosses one full ladder step you stop hiding and you fire. Everything in the rest of this guide is a way to compute the gap correctly, to keep it from going negative, and to choose the exact minute to convert it into the win.

## Why the crown is the target and you are not the problem

The crown is the largest board in the match, and that single fact makes it the most dangerous player in the room. Every other large board is trying to avoid being the second-most-threatening target, because the moment you look like the next crown the field will start weighing a focus-fire on you. Your own guide on [the crown's endgame position](/guides/crown-position-mirv-defense) describes this from the other side: the crown is the one being defended against, and it cannot safely expand because expanding into a contested border reads as a declaration of war. That means the crown is structurally pinned in place for much of the endgame, and every move it makes is observable and punishable. You, as the second or third board, are not the primary target in the same way, and that is an advantage, not a weakness. The community says it plainly in the r/Openfront thread "How to win in endgame?": if you are not the largest when the endgame starts the position is already hard, but it is not over, and the thread's working answer is that you need to be second with a nuclear threat ready, not third and out of options. The strategic consequence is that the endgame is not a race to the biggest territory. It is a sequence of choices about who is allowed to be the biggest, and the crown has already paid a price for being that player. Your job is to extract value from that price while you are still below the focus-fire line, and to convert that value into a nuclear reserve before the threshold that decides the match starts to collapse.

The deeper reason the crown is pinned is that its size is also its information problem. A crown board that is 45% of the usable land in a five-player endgame cannot afford to be wrong about a single border, because a single wrong annexation hands a rival the land that was keeping the threshold out of reach. That means the crown plays a defensive game for the whole endgame, and a defensive player is a player who is reacting, not deciding. You, as the second board, are the one player in the match who is allowed to be the decision-maker, because you are large enough to matter and small enough to move. The asymmetry is not that the crown is stronger, it is that the crown is more constrained, and the non-crown win is the win that comes from being the player with the most freedom rather than the most land. The crown can win on land share, yes, but only if the threshold sinks below its current share, and that sinking is the one thing you can race against, because you can force a resolution before it happens, and the crown cannot force a resolution at all, because forcing a resolution is exactly what gets a crown focus-fired. So the endgame is not a question of who has more territory, it is a question of who has more time to spend it, and the crown has the least time of anyone in the match, which is the structural fact that the entire non-crown strategy is built on.

## The two clocks that end the match

Two independent clocks determine when and how the endgame resolves, and a non-crown player must track both of them before deciding anything. The first is the win-threshold clock. The match does not end when someone reaches a fixed amount of land; it ends when a player owns at least 80% of the usable land on the map. But that 80% is not constant. Once the match has passed the 30-minute mark, the threshold drops by 0.1% for every minute that ticks by. That means at minute 30 you need 80% to win, at minute 40 you need 70%, at minute 50 you need 60%, and so on, until the threshold is so low that the largest remaining board can claim it without actually conquering the whole map. The second clock is the overtime clock, which is the same 30-minute starting line expressed from the other direction: if no one reaches the threshold by 30 minutes, the match enters overtime and the threshold keeps sinking at one percent per minute. This is the mechanic described in the [winning overtime](/guides/winning-overtime) guide, but from the non-crown side it has a specific meaning. You cannot outrun a sinking threshold by slow grinding, because the threshold is moving toward you. What you can do is force a resolution before the threshold sinks low enough that the crown simply wins on land share alone. That is why the non-crown endgame is a race against the minute counter, not a race against the crown's build. If you wait too long and the threshold drops to a number the crown's current board already exceeds, the crown wins without doing anything, and your entire reserve becomes worthless. So the first thing you decide, every endgame, is which of the two clocks you are trying to beat, and the second thing you decide is how much time you have to beat it.

The practical way to track the threshold is to convert it into a land target you can see on the map. If the match is at minute 35, the threshold is 75%, and the usable land on the map is the total land minus water and no-man's-land, then the winning board is the one that can reach 75% of that usable total, not 75% of the whole map including water. That distinction matters because the no-man's-land between boards is not usable land, and a player who is 80% of the total including no-man's-land is not the winner. The [win conditions](/guides/win-conditions) guide covers the exact win-check logic, and the non-crown endgame applies it in reverse: you are not the player checking whether you have won, you are the player checking whether the crown has already won, and the moment the threshold sinks below the crown's current usable-land share, the crown has already won and the match is effectively over even though it is still running. That is why the non-crown player re-checks the threshold every few minutes rather than every minute or every ten minutes: a minute-long check is fine in the first ten minutes of the endgame, but in the last ten minutes the threshold is dropping fast enough that a minute-long check can miss the exact minute the crown crossed the line, and if you miss it you keep banking toward a reserve that can no longer win, which is the single most expensive mistake in the non-crown endgame. The two clocks are not just two numbers, they are two different ways the match can end without you doing anything, and the non-crown strategy is the strategy that refuses to let either clock end the match without a decision, because a decision you make is the only thing that beats a clock, and a clock that you ignore is the one that ends you.

## Staying just below the focus-fire line

The single most important non-crown habit is to stay visibly below the threat level that triggers a coordinated strike. This is the [deterrence posture](/guides/ffa-deterrence-posture) idea applied from the side that does not want to be the crown: you want to be large enough that no one thinks about hitting you for cheap land, but not so large, so visibly expanding, or so visibly nuclear-ready that three other players start coordinating a MIRV against you. The practical read is about borders and growth rate, not about the absolute number. If your board is growing at the same rate as the crown's, you are reading as the next crown, and the field will price you into a target. If your board is growing at half the crown's rate and you are not pushing on two fronts at once, you read as a stable second or third player, and the field leaves you alone to keep your own position stable. The r/Openfront thread "Do you all also play with the thought of not becoming the crown?" captures this directly: the poster's plan is to be the third-best player, not too much of a threat, yet still have the means to jump when someone next to them overpushes. That is the exact posture. The fourth-best board in a five-player endgame should grow, bank, and quietly annex the small bots and the weakest neighbors, while the second-best board should be more careful and grow slower. The difference between those two postures is the difference between a player who gets focus-fired at minute 35 and a player who is still alive and nuclear at minute 42. When you are below the line, the crown is busy managing its own borders, the other large boards are busy not looking like the next crown, and you are the one player in the match who is allowed to simply bank. That banking is what builds the reserve, and the reserve is what wins. If you push too far above the line, the field reorganizes, the crown stops being the sole target, and the whole match reshuffles around you instead of around it, which is almost always worse for the player doing the pushing.

There is one more read in this section that separates the players who win this way from the players who just die slower: the focus-fire line is not a number you compute once, it is a number that moves every time the board changes. A border that is calm at minute 28 becomes a threat at minute 31 the moment the crown annexes the small bot between you and it, because the annexation removes the buffer that was keeping your expansion invisible. That is why the non-crown player watches the crown's annexations, not just their own borders, because the crown's annexation is the event that moves your focus-fire line more than any of your own moves do. The second-best board in particular has to be ready to back off an expansion the instant the crown takes a neighboring position, because the expansion that was invisible at minute 28 is visible and punishable at minute 31. The fourth-best board has more slack and can keep growing, which is exactly why the r/Openfront thread keeps describing the winning non-crown position as the third-best board rather than the second: the third has enough distance from the crown to grow, and enough distance from the small boards to not be annexed, and that middle band is where the reserve is actually built. Staying below the line is therefore not a static posture, it is a continuous act of deciding which borders to hold, which to freeze, and which to quietly give up so that your growth rate stays below the rate that reads as a declaration of war, and every one of those decisions is what buys you the minute you need to bank toward the launch.

## Banking the reserve at 42% regeneration

Gold regeneration in OpenFront is driven by your land base, and the single most important number for the non-crown endgame is that a large, stable board regenerates roughly 42% of its current bank per minute at steady state once it is not under active siege. That number is not a build order and it is not a hard cap; it is the pace at which a healthy board that is not fighting on two fronts can convert its territory into spendable gold. The reason this matters more to you than to the crown is that the crown cannot bank cleanly. The crown is always the target, so it is always spending on SAMs, always spending on border defense, and always spending on the MIRVs it is about to launch. It is structurally a spender. You, below the line, are structurally a saver, and that asymmetry is the whole strategy. If you are second in a five-player endgame at minute 20 with a bank of 30M and a board that regenerates 42% per minute, and you spend nothing on offense and only minimal SAM coverage on the one border that is actually being probed, your bank compounds toward the 40M and 55M marks while the crown's bank stays flat because it is paying for the privilege of staying the crown. The r/Openfront thread "What is the most optimal late game play? Best farming method?" asks exactly this question, and the working answers in that thread all point the same direction: stop fighting, stop expanding aggressively, keep the money, and let the board do the compounding. The practical rule is that every endgame you should have a single number you are banking toward, and that number should be one full MIRV launch above the reserve you estimate your strongest rival holds. If you think the crown is at 55M, you bank to 70M plus the next ladder step, not to 55M. You want the reserve that lets you launch one more MIRV than the field can answer, because the endgame is decided by who has the last credible warhead, not by who has the most land.

The compounding math is what makes the 42% number feel almost too generous, and it is worth doing it once so you trust it under pressure. A bank that regenerates 42% of its current value per minute, with no spending, does not grow by a fixed amount, it grows by a fraction, which means the later you are in the endgame the more each minute is worth in absolute gold. At 30M the 42% rate is about 12.6M per minute, at 50M it is about 21M per minute, and at 70M it is about 29M per minute. That is why the non-crown player who is behind early feels the bank move slowly and the non-crown player who is ahead feels it move fast, even though the rate is identical: the rate is a percentage, so it scales with the number. The practical consequence is that the endgame is not won by the player who banks the longest in absolute minutes, it is won by the player who reaches the largest bank first, because the largest bank compounds the fastest and pulls further ahead every minute. That is the snowball the community keeps pointing at in the r/Openfront endgame threads, and it is the reason the non-crown player who is third with a slightly larger bank is actually in the stronger position than the second player with a slightly smaller bank, even though the second looks closer to the crown. The other half of the math is the spending half, which is where most non-crown players lose the reserve: every SAM you buy to cover a border that is not actually being probed, every nuke you launch to look strong when you should be looking quiet, every expansion you fund that pushes your growth rate above the focus-fire line, is a percentage of your bank that will never compound. The reserve is only as good as the percentage you keep out of it, and the non-crown endgame is, at its core, the game of keeping that percentage high long enough for the compounding to outrun the sinking threshold, and then spending the whole thing in one launch that the field has not had a minute to answer.

## The MIRV price ladder as a weapon

The MIRV cost in v0.35.9 is not a flat price; it is a ladder that climbs with every MIRV launched anywhere in the match. The first MIRV of the game costs 25M, the next one costs 40M, the next 55M, the next 70M, and so on, adding 15M per game-wide launch. This is the [MIRV price ladder](/guides/mirv-price-ladder) and the [MIRV mechanics](/guides/mirv), but the non-crown endgame reads it in a direction that most players never use it for: the ladder is not just a cost you pay, it is a weapon you can point at your rivals. Every MIRV that goes off in the match, whether by you or by someone else, raises the price of the next one for everyone. That means a match in which a lot of MIRVs have already been fired is a match in which the final round of nuclear exchange is vastly more expensive than a match where no one has launched. If you are banking toward the fifth or sixth ladder step while the field is still stuck at the second, you are not just building a bigger reserve, you are building a reserve that no one else can match at the current price, and you are simultaneously making it so expensive for anyone else to answer you that they have to choose between paying the full ladder and losing the warhead race. The r/Openfront thread "How to win in endgame?" has a specific number that the community treats as the reference: you can field roughly six MIRV warheads for the price of one large single warhead, which is the arithmetic that makes a MIRV-heavy non-crown board so hard to answer. The silo cooldown is 90 ticks, and the MIRV warhead flight is the slowest of the nuclear options, so the launch is not instantaneous; it is a decision that plays out over a visible window, and the field can see it coming. That window is your leverage. You launch when the ladder is high enough that your rivals cannot quickly buy the next answer, and you do not launch when the ladder is still low enough that a quick 25M or 40M counter from the crown would erase your advantage. The ladder is the reason the non-crown win is a timing game and not a pure bank race, and it is the reason the endgame is watchable: every launch is a public price increase that everyone can see and react to.

## When to ally and when to cut

Alliances in the non-crown endgame are tools, not commitments, and the non-crown player is the one who most needs them because the non-crown player is the one who most often cannot answer a coordinated strike alone. The decision to ally is the same as the decision to not ally: you ally with a player who is large enough to survive your current threat level but not large enough to be the crown, and you ally against the crown, not against each other. The [nuke alliance threshold](/guides/nuke-alliance-threshold) describes the numbers that say a two-board alliance can out-spend a single crown, and the non-crown endgame applies it from the side that is forming the alliance. The pattern is that the second-best board and the fourth-best board often have more to gain from an alliance than the second and third do, because the fourth-best board is the one most likely to be annexed by the crown or focus-fired by the second, and the second-best board is the one most likely to be focus-fired by the crown, so the two of them share a single primary threat even though they also share a secondary rivalry. The decision to cut an alliance is just as important. The [nuke territory annexation](/guides/nuke-cut-territory-annexation) guide covers the mechanics of cutting a shared border, and the non-crown endgame uses it at the moment the alliance target dies: the second you stop needing the alliance, you stop paying its price, which means you stop feeding the ally the land that keeps them alive, and you start banking toward your own launch. The r/Openfront thread on how to win in the endgame has a specific warning that the community repeats: the moment your ally is no longer a useful shield, the alliance becomes a liability, because the ally is now the player most motivated to hit you for the reserve you have been banking. The economics of the alliance are worth stating because they are the reason the non-crown player, and not the crown, is the one who forms it. The crown cannot ally with you against itself, and it cannot buy your bank, so from the crown's point of view you are either a target to strike or a bystander who does not matter. The second-best board can be focus-fired by the crown at the current ladder step, and the fourth-best board can be annexed by the crown at near-zero cost, so both of them have a reason to join a two-board pact that the crown has to answer at a price the ladder keeps climbing. That is the asymmetry the non-crown player is exploiting: the alliance is cheap to form because the crown's answer to a two-board front is a launch it has to pay for, and the alliance is valuable because the crown's land share is the one number that the threshold will eventually decide against, so pinning the crown's expansion is pinning the crown's land growth at the same time. The cost of the alliance, and the reason the cut has to be clean, is that every minute the alliance survives you are feeding the ally a share of your border's regeneration, which is the same 42% rate you would otherwise be banking, so the alliance is a percentage tax on your reserve for as long as it lasts. That tax is worth paying while the crown is alive, because the crown's death is worth more than the tax, and it is not worth paying after the crown is dead, because then the tax is keeping the second board alive and the second board is the new target, which is exactly the position the alliance was supposed to keep you out of. The non-crown player who reads the alliance as a shield and forgets to cut is the player who wins the bank race, survives the crown, and then loses to the board that was supposed to be the alliance's other member, which is the most common endgame loss in the r/Openfront threads because the player has been doing everything right up to the one decision that requires cutting a relationship at the exact moment the relationship stops being useful.

## The bankroll-threshold decision table

The endgame decision is not a single choice, it is a table you re-evaluate every few minutes, and the columns are your bank, the estimated bank of the crown, the current ladder step, the current win threshold, and the number of players still alive. The rows are the choices: bank, hold, expand, ally, cut, or launch. The table below is the working version for a five-player endgame, and the numbers are the v0.35.9 ladder costs so you can see exactly what each choice is buying. The rule that separates the winners from the rest is the last column, which is the reserve gap, defined as your bank minus the crown's bank minus the current ladder step. If the reserve gap is negative, you cannot launch and win, and the correct play is to bank or hold, not to expand, because expanding while you are behind on the ladder just makes you the new target and raises your own cost. If the reserve gap is positive but smaller than one full ladder step, you can launch but you should not, because the crown can answer you at the current price, and the correct play is to bank until the gap is at least one full ladder step. If the reserve gap is at least one full ladder step, and the threshold is high enough that the crown cannot simply win on land share by the time your warheads land, then you launch, because that is the only row in the table where the non-crown board actually becomes the winner. The r/Openfront threads on late-game farming and on not becoming the crown both converge on this table without naming it: the players who win from second or third are the ones who are re-evaluating the gap every few minutes and refusing to act while the gap is negative, which is the opposite of the instinct that tells a player who is losing to do something dramatic.

| Situation | Your bank | Crown bank | Ladder step | Threshold | Correct row |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Early endgame, gap negative | 25M | 40M | 40M | 75% | Bank, stay below line |
| Mid endgame, gap near zero | 45M | 45M | 55M | 70% | Hold, bank to next step |
| Mid endgame, gap one step | 55M | 40M | 55M | 68% | Bank, do not launch yet |
| Late endgame, gap one-plus step | 70M | 45M | 55M | 62% | Ally or launch |
| Crown dead, gap large | 70M | — | 70M | 55% | Cut alliance, launch |
| Threshold collapsed, gap small | 45M | 70M | 70M | 48% | Hold, survive, overtime |

## Two scenarios with numbers

Scenario one is a five-player endgame at minute 25. You are the second-largest board with 42% of the usable land in your region, the crown is the largest with 48% in its region, the fourth board is 22% and growing slowly, the fifth board is 14% and is being annexed by the fourth, and the current ladder step is 40M because the crown already launched one MIRV early in the game. Your bank is 35M, the crown's bank is estimated at 30M because it is spending on SAMs, and the threshold is 78% because the match is at minute 28. The gap is 35M minus 30M minus 40M, which is minus 35M, so the table says bank and stay below the line. You do not expand, you do not ally yet, and you let the board compound at 42% per minute. By minute 32 your bank is near 50M, the crown has launched a second MIRV which pushed the ladder to 55M, and the crown's bank is flat at 30M. The gap is now 50M minus 30M minus 55M, which is minus 35M again, but the ladder has risen, which means the crown's answer is now more expensive. You bank to 60M. By minute 38 your bank is 65M, the ladder is still 55M, and the gap is plus 10M, which is one full ladder step. You now launch, because the threshold is 72% and the crown's board, even at its full 48%, cannot reach 72% by the time your warheads land, and the crown cannot buy the next answer at 55M without gutting its own SAM coverage. The launch plays out over the warhead flight window, the crown scrambles to answer, the ladder jumps to 70M, and the crown's bank is now too low to buy the next answer before the threshold collapses in the other direction. You win the warhead race.

Scenario two is the same endgame but you are the fourth-largest board instead of the second, with 22% of the region, and the crown is 48% and the second is 35%. The bank is 18M, the crown's bank is estimated at 30M, and the ladder step is 55M because two MIRVs have already launched. The gap is 18M minus 30M minus 55M, which is minus 67M, so the table says bank, but that is not enough, because at the fourth-board size the 42% regeneration is slower in absolute terms and the crown is going to annex you if you do not act. The correct row here is ally, and you ally with the second board, because the second board's primary threat is the crown and the crown's primary target is the second, so the two of you share the crown as a common enemy even though the second will eventually be a rival. The alliance survives the crown's next two launches, the crown's bank drops because it is paying for the ladder and for SAMs, and the ladder is now 70M. The moment the crown's board is broken and it is no longer the largest, you cut the alliance, because the second board is now the largest and is now the target, and the second board's bank is the reserve you were banking alongside. You go to launch at 70M because your bank is now 65M from the alliance survival and the crown's death, and the second board's bank is flat because it was spending on the alliance. The threshold is 60% and the second board, at 35%, cannot reach 60% by the time your warheads land, so you win from fourth place by allying, surviving, cutting, and launching. Both scenarios are the same table, the same ladder, the same 42% regeneration, and the same threshold, and the difference between them is only the starting position and the one extra row, which is the alliance, that the smaller board needs.

## Failure modes and how to counter them

The first failure mode is launching while the gap is negative, which is the instinct that says you are losing so you should do something. The counter is the table: you do not launch until the gap is at least one full ladder step, and if the threshold is collapsing and you cannot get the gap positive, you switch to the overtime row, which is survive and let the threshold do the work, because a sinking threshold helps the largest remaining board and if the crown is broken then the largest remaining board might be you. The second failure mode is expanding while below the line, which turns you into the next crown and makes you the target, and the counter is the 42% regeneration rule: you expand only on borders that are not being probed, and you expand slowly, and you never push on two fronts at once in the endgame because that is the signature of the player who is about to be focus-fired. The third failure mode is keeping the alliance after the crown is dead, and the counter is the cut rule: the moment the alliance target is gone, you stop feeding the ally and you go to launch, because the ally is now the player most motivated to hit your reserve. The fourth failure mode is banking toward the crown's bank instead of one ladder step above it, and the counter is the reserve-gap definition: you bank to the crown's bank plus the current ladder step, not to the crown's bank, because the endgame is decided by who has the last credible warhead. The fifth failure mode is ignoring the threshold and assuming the match will end on land share, and the counter is the two-clock rule: you track the threshold every few minutes, and if the threshold is below the crown's current board share, the crown is going to win on land share no matter what you do, so the only winning move in that row is to have already launched or to have broken the crown first. The r/Openfront thread on how to win in the endgame has a specific observation that ties these together: the players who lose from second are almost always the ones who made one of these five mistakes, and the players who win from second are the ones who did not, which is why the endgame is a discipline game and not a reflex game. The counter to every failure mode is the same: re-evaluate the table every few minutes, refuse to act on the rows that say hold or bank, and act only on the row that says launch.

## Map and mode adjustments

The endgame table above is for a five-player FFA on a standard map, and the adjustments for other modes and map sizes are specific. In a four-player endgame the fourth board is more likely to be the one that allies, because the fourth board has no fifth board to hide behind and the second board has the crown as its primary threat, so the alliance forms more often and the cut happens faster. In a six-player or seven-player endgame the table has more rows, because there are more boards below the line that can bank and more boards that can be focus-fired, and the ladder climbs faster because more players are launching, which means the final round is more expensive and the non-crown reserve has to be bigger to match it. In a two-team endgame the whole table changes, because the crown is a team and the non-crown is the other team, and the reserve gap is the combined bank of the team minus the combined bank of the crown team minus the ladder, and the alliance row disappears because the team is the alliance. On smaller maps the 42% regeneration is faster in absolute terms because the board is smaller and the land base is denser, so the bank builds faster and the threshold collapses faster, which means the endgame is shorter and the launch window is narrower, and the non-crown player has less time to bank to the next ladder step, so the correct play on small maps is to launch earlier rather than later. On larger maps the regeneration is slower in absolute terms and the threshold collapses slower, so the endgame is longer and the bank has more time to build, which means the non-crown player can afford to be more patient and bank to a higher ladder step before launching. The map strategy guides for the specific maps, such as the [Vancouver Island map](/guides/vancouver-island-map) and the [Europe FFA openings](/guides/europe-ffa-openings), describe the opening positions that set up these endgames, and the non-crown endgame is where those opening choices pay off or fail, because the opening determines which board you are when the endgame starts, and the table above is the table for the board you actually are. The adjustment for ranked lobbies is that the ladder is reset per match, so the current ladder step at the start of the endgame depends on how many MIRVs were launched earlier in that specific match, not on a global counter, and the bank estimates are more accurate because the players are more predictable, which means the reserve gap is easier to compute and the table is more reliable. The adjustment for unranked lobbies is the opposite: the ladder is still reset per match but the players are less predictable, so the bank estimates are rougher and the table has to be re-evaluated more often, and the non-crown player in an unranked lobby should bank to a bigger reserve than the table says because the estimates are less reliable. The single adjustment that matters most across all modes and maps is that the ladder is the one number that is always correct, because it is public and it is the same for every player, and the bank estimates are the one number that is always rough, so the endgame is decided by the ladder and the threshold, and the bank is the one variable you control.

## Reading the endgame as a whole
