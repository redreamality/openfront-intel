## Direct answer: what ranked is and the number that changes your decision

Ranked is the competitive side of OpenFront, reached from the Ranked button on the main menu, and it runs two independent ladders: one for 1v1 and one for 2v2. Those two ladders do not share a rating, so a player who is strong in the solo queue can be completely unrated in the team queue, and the server keeps the two numbers separate and shows a separate leaderboard for each. If you only want one paragraph of advice from this page, here it is: ranked is a metered, high-stakes queue, and the number that should change how you play it is your daily free-match budget. Unless your account holds the unlimited ranked subscription perk, ranked consumes a fixed number of free matches per day, and when that pool is empty the ranked menu simply stops offering matches until the pool refills the next day.

That single constraint is what separates ranked from a public lobby you can queue into as often as you like, and it is the reason the decision this page is built around is not "how good am I at ranked" but "how should I spend a limited set of attempts today, and which of the two ladders should I climb." A day in which you burn your entire free pool on a ladder you only wanted to sample is a budget mistake you cannot recover until the next refill, so the practical reading of ranked is that it rewards a player who has a plan for the attempt before the first game starts, not a player who queues and improvises. Two more facts belong in this direct answer because they shape every queue you will press. First, ranked does not let you choose the map; the server rolls one from a weighted pool and draws Australia about forty percent of the time, so the map you land on is a roll rather than a choice, and the map that matters most to prepare for is the one you will see most. Second, the game starts on a short fifteen-second pick deadline and ends instantly if a player does not pick in time, which means the very start of a ranked game is fragile in a way a public lobby is not, and a slow pick can cost you an attempt you will not get back that day.

Everything below is in service of that plan: the two ladders, the matchmaking and start deadline, the map and spawn settings, the win conditions, and the budget, each explained so you can make the attempt budget a deliberate choice. Read the section on the two ladders first so you understand that your 1v1 and 2v2 numbers are separate and never transfer, then the section on the free-play limit so you know the real budget you are working with, and only then the map and win-condition sections so you know what you are spending the budget on. The goal of this page is to let you walk into the ranked menu with the number, the ladder, and the plan already settled, so the queue becomes a tool you use on purpose instead of a cost you notice only after the daily pool is empty and the day's ladder work is over.

## What ranked is and how the two separate ladders work

Ranked is a matchmaking-based competitive mode, not a lobby you host. You press the Ranked button, choose 1v1 or 2v2, and the server searches a pool of other queued players and pairs you with opponents of comparable rating. In 1v1 the game is a two-player contest in which the two of you are the only humans on the map, surrounded by a field of bots that provide the territory, the pressure, and the targets you would otherwise need to face. In 2v2 the server assembles two teams of two and, where it can, pairs you with a teammate; it will prefer to put you with clanmates when the pool allows it, and it will cancel a match that does not fill rather than drop you into a broken, under-populated lobby. Because you are matched by rating instead of by a shared lobby, ranked plays very differently from public lobbies, which mix skill levels, map settings, and player intent in a way that often produces self-sorting problems, where players pick fights they cannot win and losses come from the lobby you landed in rather than from a real skill gap.

The second thing to understand is that the two modes are separate ladders, not one ladder with two queues. The server stores a distinct rating for each mode and tracks your peak rating separately for each one. This has a concrete, testable consequence: a player who has only ever queued 1v1 will show a 1v1 rating and a 1v1 peak, with no 2v2 number at all, and the 2v2 leaderboard will simply not list that player until they queue 2v2 and earn a rating there; the exact same thing is true in reverse for the solo queue. Community reports put the 1v1 ladder in the low-to-mid thousands, with players commonly describing the range from about 1750 to 1850 as a solid, clearly-above-average level, and with the higher tiers getting noticeably more aggressive and less forgiving. The precise ELO formula and the starting rating assigned to a brand-new ladder are set by the server and are not published in the open, so you should treat any exact number you see attached to a specific player as a point on that player's own ladder, not as a transferable score that carries across modes. What matters for your decisions is the shape: rating rises on wins, falls on losses, matchmaking pairs you by proximity so you are generally matched against nearby ratings, and the number is shown to you only when you are logged in with the correct account, which is the reason behind a well-known community gotcha where a player is not signed into the account they expect and it can look as if their rank has vanished when it has not changed at all.

## Matchmaking flow, the 15-second start deadline, and games that end instantly

After you queue, the server searches for an opponent and, in 2v2, fills both teams. Two things happen at the queue stage that are worth watching. The first is how long the search takes, and the community reports it can range from a few seconds to several minutes depending on how many players are online at that hour, so the queue is itself variable and you should not assume a match is imminent the moment you press the button. The second is what happens to a 2v2 that does not fill: the server will prefer to pair you with a teammate, it will prefer to fill with clanmates when the pool allows it, and if it cannot fill the lobby it will cancel the match rather than drop you into an under-populated one, which protects you from a broken match but does mean a 2v2 queue can also end in a cancel rather than a game.

The moment a match is actually found, it is created and the players are given a short, fixed window to pick their starting position on the map, and that window is fifteen seconds. If a player does not pick within the window, the match is cancelled rather than forced to start, and a cancelled match ends with no declared winner at all. Because the match and its timer are live the instant the match is found, there is no grace period to load in late, and a cancelled or one-second result still counts against your attempt pool. The practical implication is that the very start of a ranked game is fragile in a way that public lobbies are not: if your opponent is slow, on a bad connection, or simply does not register the pick, the match can end without a single shot being fired, and you will have spent one of your daily attempts on a result that is not a real read on your skill.

This is the single most-cited ranked annoyance across the community threads. Several players report that in a meaningful minority of ranked games one of the players is unable to select a starting position and the game ends almost immediately, often showing up as a one-second result on the record. The reasons reported cluster in two places. The first is the queue itself: a player who minimizes the game or drops during the wait can return to a result that was already decided before they noticed the match had started. The second is deliberate timing: some players wait until the last possible second to place their spawn, treating the delay as a tactic, so that a missed pick throws the whole game. If you are mid-task when the match pops, the safe move is to place your spawn immediately. The "last-second" placement that some players rely on is a real tactic, but it is also the fastest way to throw a game you never intended to throw, and on a metered budget the cost of a thrown game is higher than it would be in a public lobby.

## Map pool, map-size roll, and the bot population you actually face

Ranked does not let you choose the map; the server rolls one from a fixed pool, and that pool is weighted rather than uniform. In both 1v1 and 2v2 the same four maps are available, but Australia is weighted twice as heavily as the rest, so it comes up roughly forty percent of the time while Iceland, Asia, and Europe Classic each come up about twenty percent. The practical read of that is that you will see Australia far more often than any other ranked map, so the map-specific habits that matter most in ranked are the Australia habits: its spawn geometry, its land borders, and the way its bot field is distributed. If you only prepare for one ranked map, prepare for Australia, because the odds say you will be on it almost half the time you queue.

The reason the pool is weighted instead of uniform is to keep the ranked environment consistent and testable across both ladders: the server wants the two modes to draw from the same four maps so that a map you have studied in 1v1 transfers directly to 2v2, and it over-weights Australia so that the most-played ranked map is the one you will see the most. The side effect is that the three lighter maps are not dead, they just appear in short bursts, and a player who only prepares for Australia will be surprised by a sudden run of Asia or Iceland that they have not planned for. The sensible preparation is to treat Australia as the default and to keep a lighter read on the other three, so that a non-Australia roll is an adjustment rather than a relearn.

The server also rolls a map size, and it rolls one differently per mode. In 1v1 a compact map is chosen about twenty percent of the time, which leaves an eighty percent chance of the normal size; in 2v2 a compact map is chosen about fifty percent of the time, so the team ladder is a coin flip between compact and normal. A compact map shrinks the world and tightens the opening, which pushes the game into faster, more direct aggression and removes the slow expansion paths a normal map allows. The bot population scales with that size too: a normal map seeds roughly four hundred bots while a compact map seeds roughly one hundred, so a compact ranked game has far less bot territory to farm and punishes slow, patient expansion much more than a normal map does. The two rolls combine in a way that makes map preparation a real decision rather than an afterthought. In 2v2, a forty percent Australia roll combined with a fifty percent compact roll means you will frequently face a small, fast Australia mirror, and preparing specifically for that shape is the highest-value map work you can do before you queue. In 1v1 the picture is calmer, an eighty percent chance of the normal size, so the 1v1 map environment is more predictable and the map-specific work is less urgent than it is on the team ladder.

## Spawn immunity window and win conditions: how the game actually ends

When the game starts you are protected at your spawn for a fixed window before anyone can attack you, and that window is shorter in the solo ladder and longer in the team ladder: thirty seconds in 1v1 and sixty seconds in 2v2. The difference is not cosmetic, it changes the entire opening. In 1v1 the thirty-second window is tight enough that the opening is decided almost immediately; by the time your immunity is lifted you have had only a short burst to read the map, place your first expansion, and commit to the border you intend to hold, so the players who win solo ranked are the ones who know what they are going to do before the clock starts. In 2v2 the sixty-second window gives your team a full minute to set up lanes, split the map, and position before either team can invade, and the common 2v2 opening is to use that full minute to establish the lane split and the first border before the immunity lifts. The immunity clock also interacts with the fifteen-second pick deadline in a subtle way: the protection window starts once the game is actually running, so a slow pick does not extend your protection, it only means the game has already consumed part of its life before you were safe. The carry-over risk is real: if you bring 2v2 patience into 1v1 you will be too slow to secure the first border and the game will be decided before you are ready, and if you bring 1v1 speed into 2v2 you will rush the lane split and hand your teammate the wrong half of the map.

Ranked is won the same way the core game is won, but the fixed settings make the win conditions stricter and the decision faster. A match ends when a player or team takes a dominant share of the map, or when the timer runs out and the player or team holding the highest land share wins. In 2v2 the win is aggregated across the team: the server sums the land the two teammates control and compares that total, so one teammate holding a large core and the other holding the bridge or the second front both contribute to a single team number that is what the server actually checks. In 1v1 the comparison is purely between the two of you, with no team number to fall back on. The timer behavior is the part most new ranked players get wrong, and it is the part the community threads keep coming back to: players describe losing 1v1 games where the opponent held only a small majority of land, with the explanation that the clock ran out and the opponent simply had more. That means a ranked game you believe you are leading can be lost simply because the lead was never converted into a decisive share before the timer ended. The counter is to treat the timer as a hard constraint from the very start, not as something to check near the end: a fifteen percent land lead with time still on the clock is not a win, and a slow lead that is quietly bleeding territory is a loss already in progress. In 2v2 the aggregation adds a second thing to watch, because you should be tracking the combined team total rather than your own personal share, since the server decides the game on the team number even when your personal share is low.

## Free-play limits and the unlimited ranked perk

The defining constraint on ranked is the daily free-play limit, and it is the one number you should check in your own account before you queue on any given day. Every account has a fixed number of free ranked matches per day; each ranked game you play draws down that pool, and when it is empty the ranked menu stops offering matches until the pool refills the next day. The exact count of free matches per day is set by the server and can change between updates, so the reliable thing to do is to read how many ranked matches you can play right now, because that number is the real budget for your ladder work and it is the number that decides how much you can actually climb today. The daily reset is the reason a ranked budget behaves like a subscription refill: it is a fixed allowance that tops back up on a schedule, not a pool that shrinks and stays shrunk, so the decision you face each day is how to allocate a known, small number of attempts rather than how to save a large one.

The unlimited ranked perk removes the cap for accounts that hold it: with the perk, ranked does not consume a daily free-match budget at all, and you can queue as often as the matchmaking pool will let you. That perk is one of the more meaningful subscription benefits for a competitive player, because it converts ranked from a metered queue into an open one and changes the economics of climbing in a way that no single gameplay improvement can match. When you are actively trying to climb a ladder, the daily free limit is the thing that throttles you, because climbing requires volume and a fixed daily pool caps the volume you can put in; the unlimited perk is what removes that throttle, and it is the reason a serious climber often finds the perk worth far more than its price relative to the other subscription options. The decision it drives is practical and easy to state: if you want to play enough games to measure your own trend over time, the free limit will cap you and the perk removes the cap, whereas if you only want to queue ranked occasionally, the free pool is fine and you should be deliberately sparing about when you spend it. Either way the budget is real, it is the thing that most limits your ranked time, and you should plan your queue around it rather than discover it empty at the end of a day you had planned to grind, because the difference between a planned queue and an unplanned one is the difference between ranked being a tool you can use and ranked being a cost you only notice once it is gone.

## Which ladder to queue and when to skip ranked

The two ladders reward different skills and draw from the same kind of budget, so choosing between them is a real decision rather than a default. 1v1 is the purest test of individual skill: you are the only human on the map, there is no teammate to rely on and no teammate to let down, the opening is fast because of the short thirty-second immunity window, and every win or loss maps almost directly onto your own play, which is what makes the solo ladder the best tool for sharpening your own decisions and reading your own improvement without a teammate variable muddying the signal. The trade-off is that it is the most exposed ladder, with the most aggressive players and the least forgiveness, and community reports consistently describe the higher 1v1 tiers as very aggressive, with a few common tactics such as repeated small naval harassment being dominant enough to make long runs feel repetitive. If your goal is to sharpen your own decisions and see a clean read on your own improvement, queue 1v1, because the absence of a teammate is a feature here, not a bug, and the rating you earn is a number that belongs only to you.

2v2 adds a teammate and a sixty-second setup window, which softens the opening and turns the game into one about lane splitting, donating, and reading the map in concert with a partner; the trade-off is that your result now depends partly on the teammate and on the environment, and the fifty percent compact roll plus the forty percent Australia roll mean the team environment is fast and specific. If you want to practice team play, practice the donation mechanic, or you simply find the solo ladder too grindy, 2v2 is the better use of your attempts. The case for skipping ranked altogether is also a real one: if your daily free pool is the only budget you have and you would rather spend it on public lobbies to practice the broader game, ranked is a metered cost, not a free one, and the broad game is where most of your improvement actually happens. A sensible pattern is to queue the ladder that matches your current goal, spend a defined number of attempts, review the results, and only then decide whether to spend more, because in ranked every attempt is one you cannot get back today. When you do queue, the pattern that works is to check your free-matches-left number first, pick the ladder that matches the skill you want to test that day, set the number of attempts you will spend, and stop when you hit it, so the metered queue becomes a deliberate training tool rather than a drain you discover at the end of the day.

## Failures, counters, and where the rest of the guides fit

The failures in ranked cluster around the settings, not around your build choices, and the good news is that each one has a known counter. The first is the start: a missed or late spawn pick can end the match instantly, so pick immediately and treat the fifteen-second deadline as live the moment the match is found, because there is no grace period and the "last-second" habit is a real tactic that is also the fastest way to throw a game. The second is the timer: a lead is only a win when it becomes a decisive share before the clock ends, so track the timer and the land share together from the start and convert leads early rather than assuming a slow majority will hold. The third is the environment roll: a compact map, with one hundred bots instead of four hundred, punishes slow expansion, so when the compact roll happens you should expand and commit faster than you would on a normal map. The fourth is the budget: a day in which you spend the entire free pool on a ladder you only wanted to sample is a budget mistake, so set an attempt budget before you queue and stop when you hit it. The counters are the same facts pointed back at you: in 1v1 hold the first border and read the opening before it is decided because the thirty-second window leaves little time, and in 2v2 use the full sixty seconds to set the lane split and the first border before the immunity lifts. Watch the team total in 2v2 rather than your personal share, because the server decides the game on the team number, and remember the ladder separation, because improving in 2v2 does not move your 1v1 rating and you should measure your progress on the ladder you are actually playing.
