## Direct answer: pick the side of the river you want, then plan the first minute

On **Yangtze River** in a public FFA, the spawn screen hands you one of nine Chinese
openings: eight on a single large connected continent and one true island. Do not treat the
country label as the decision. Confirm the lobby selected Yangtze River, count how many
other humans and Nations sit within reach, check how close you are to the river spine and to
a real Port, and decide whether your first move is to clear neutral Nations, secure the
nearest Port, or buy a defended second direction before you spend hard. Because nearly every
mainland opening can reach water within a short hop, the deciding factor is usually
**adjacency** — who arrives beside you — more than which side of the map is “better.” A
center-river opening (Changzhou) buys option density and the fastest boat, but it also puts
you where the most neighbors converge on the same neutral land. A corner (Wuhu or Shanghai)
buys fewer exposed borders and a slower, quieter start, but it defers the central river fight.
Daishan Island buys safety and a self-contained opening, at the cost of a slow, boat-gated
path back onto the mainland. Name your primary direction and an independent fallback before
the first attack, and switch when the crossing, target, or home reserve fails. Use the
four-letter check **RIVE**: **R**ead the map and neighbors, **I**nspect river and Port,
**V**alidate the first target, **E**xit when it stops paying. The four letters are an
editorial memory aid, not a game mechanic, but they force the same sequence every opening
needs: observe the live board, verify water and income, prove the first target is a bounded
capture, and have a trigger that ends the branch. The whole point of the framework is to make
the first minute a decision rather than a habit, so that the country you are handed becomes a
starting condition you test instead of an identity you keep. That single shift — from “I am
Changzhou” to “Changzhou gives me the fastest Port but the most neighbors, so here is the
first target and the fallback” — is the difference between a player who adapts to the lobby
and one who re-reads the same thumbnail and re-picks the same first move. In practice that
means writing the choice down in one line before the first action — “I am Changzhou, I take
the nearest unclaimed central Nation, and if it is contested I pull to the opposite bank” —
so the fallback is a committed route with a cost and a trigger, not a feeling that forms only
after the first attack lands.

## The map geometry that the nine openings share

The shipped `map4x` resource is 1,920 by 1,276 tiles with 464,270 passable land tiles; the
full-resolution `map` is 3,840 by 2,552 with 1,877,550 land tiles. The map is a pipe of river
bent into an upside-down U, with land on both arms and a broad delta at the bottom. A local
parse of `map4x.bin` confirms eight of the nine default openings sit on one giant connected
land component (the ninth is an island), and that every mainland opening is within 24 to 83
tiles of water. That last number is the heart of the FFA problem on this map: you almost never
choose between “has a port” and “landlocked.” You choose between **who is next door** and
**which bank of the pipe you open onto.** The river is not a wall here; it is a shared road.
Both arms of the pipe open onto the same central water, so a Port or a boat on either side
can reach the central fight, which means the “safe” side is not a side with no water — it is a
side whose water is contested less. That reframes the whole opening problem. On most maps a
good start is defined by what it keeps away from you: no early human, no forced crossing, no
thin border. On Yangtze River a good start is defined by what it **lets you choose**: the
direction of your first expansion, the bank you open onto, and the second target that stays
open if the first one is taken. A map that gives you more directions is not automatically
better, because more directions also means more neighbors who can reach the same neutral land
in the same window. A map that gives you fewer directions is not automatically worse, because
a quiet corner with two borders can out-survive a center whose three neighbors all clear
neutral land on the same first move. The nine default nations, with the parsed distance from
the spawn anchor to the nearest water: Changzhou 24, Wuhu 38, Taizhou 43, Zhenjiang 54,
Nantong 58, Nanjing 78, Ma'anshan 33, Shanghai 83, and Daishan Island 17 (on its own
~701-tile island). Read that list as a spectrum of water access and position, not as a fixed
quality ranking. A closer river is not automatically safer; it can put your first expansion
onto the same bank a neighbor is pushing onto. A farther river is not automatically worse; a
corner with two quiet borders can out-survive a center start whose three neighbors all clear
neutral land in the same window. The official [map manifest](https://github.com/openfrontio/OpenFrontIO/blob/v0.34.0/resources/maps/yangtzeriver/manifest.json)
and [generator metadata](https://github.com/openfrontio/OpenFrontIO/blob/v0.34.0/map-generator/assets/maps/yangtzeriver/info.json)
define the anchors; the live match decides who is actually beside you. Treat the thumbnail as
an orientation aid only, and re-run the parse if a release changes the binary.

## Turn the five spawn factors into a Yangtze decision

The reusable spawn framework evaluates five things: **pace**, **terrain**, **pocket**,
**proximity**, and **map characteristics**. On Yangtze River these collapse into a short
per-opening checklist. Pace: does this start put you on the river bank earlier, where you can
reach a Port or a boat faster, or in the interior, where your first useful move is land?
Terrain: the map is mostly low, fast land with limited highland; prefer the broad green strips
along the river arms for fast movement and lower troop loss, and be careful with narrow
interior necks that slow a counterattack. Pocket: is the first land you can take wide enough to
defend, or is it a thin strip that a neighbor can out-flank? Proximity: how many humans and
Nations can reach your first target in the same few minutes? Map characteristics: on this
pipe-river map, the center is where the arms meet, so a center start has more directions but
also more neighbors converging on the same neutral land.

Apply the checklist as a two-line note per opening. For Changzhou, the note is: “closest to
the pipe (distance 24), so my Port and boat are fastest, but three neighbors can contest the
same central land — I keep the far arm as a fallback.” For Wuhu, the note is: “southwest
corner, two quiet borders, distance 38 to water, so I grow land slowly but my neighbor
pressure is lowest — I trade early tempo for fewer fronts.” For Daishan Island, the note is:
“no mainland neighbor can reach me without boats, distance 17 on every side, so I am safe but
slow and I must save for the crossing.” Each note ends with the **event that changes it**: a
human landing on the far bank, a neighbor capturing the central Nation, or the river mouth
closing. If the note cannot name that event, the opening is not actually decided yet. This
turns an abstract “center is better” into a testable, per-start plan that survives a lobby you
did not expect. The two-line note also has a second purpose: it is the record you update after
the first capture. The first line states what the opening was supposed to buy; the second line
states the condition that invalidates it. When you capture the first Nation, you do not ask
“is the map getting better?” — you ask whether the second-line event has happened and whether
the first-line benefit still exists. If the neighbor has taken the central Nation, the
Changzhou note's fallback clause is now the primary, and you rewrite the note in the same
sentence. If the corner has stayed quiet, the Wuhu note keeps its primary and you simply
extend the neutral clearing. The note is cheap to keep and expensive to lose, so the habit of
updating it after every capture is worth more than any fixed opening plan, because it is the
only artifact that survives a lobby that does not match the thumbnail.

## Mainland openings: a comparison you can act on

The table below compares the eight mainland openings by what each one tends to buy, not by a
fixed score. Distances are parsed from the shipped `map4x` binary; neighbor counts and
ownership are match-specific, so re-read the board. The columns are: the tendency the opening
gives you, the first thing to verify on the live board, and the stop signal that should end
that first branch.

| Opening | Tendency | First check on the live board | Stop signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Changzhou (center-east, dist 24) | Fastest port/boat; most directions | Which neighbor is pushing the same bank first | A neighbor holds the central Nation, or two neighbors can reach your core at once |
| Nanjing (center-west, dist 78) | Interior, slower water, more land | Whether the interior neck is wide enough to defend | The west neck closes, or a neighbor outpaces your land clear |
| Zhenjiang (north-central-west, dist 54) | Balanced north bank | Which north-bank Nation is the first target | A north neighbor seals the bank, or the Port is unreachable |
| Taizhou (north, dist 43) | North arm, moderate pressure | How fast the north arm clears to a second target | The north arm is contested by two humans, or the water is blocked |
| Nantong (NE, dist 58) | NE bank, port-friendly | Whether the NE bank is defensible after the first capture | A NE neighbor takes the Port first, or the arm narrows |
| Ma'anshan (west, dist 33) | West bank, close water | Whether the west bank opens a separate second target | The west bank is a dead end, or a neighbor crosses to it |
| Wuhu (SW corner, dist 38) | Corner, fewest exposed borders | Whether the corner land is wide enough to out-scale | The SW corner is pinched from two sides, or the water is unreachable |
| Shanghai (SE corner, dist 83) | Corner, far water, delta reach | Whether the delta coast opens a real second shore | The delta Port is taken, or the corner has no inland fallback |

Do not convert these rows into a fixed troop percentage. The right first commitment depends
on how many neighbors are clearing land, how thick the first target's border is, and whether
your reserve can answer the closest response while you are away. When two rows look equally
good, prefer the one whose failure you can **see and reverse** before your reserve is gone:
the opening where the stop signal is a visible, checkable object (a captured Nation, a blocked
bank, a lost Port), not a vague feeling that “the center got crowded.” The table is
deliberately asymmetric: a center row stops on neighbor convergence, a corner row stops on
pinch, and a bank row stops on Port denial. That asymmetry is the map's logic, not an
arbitrary split. After any capture, run the table again — a taken Nation can open a coast that
was not visible at spawn, and a human on the far bank can delete the assumed fallback. The
goal is one credible next move when the first is denied, which is the difference between
optionality and overextension. For a full board scan once an opening has started, use
[Threat assessment](/guides/threat-assessment/); for the generic opening timing behind this
map, see [FFA opening](/strategies/ffa-opening/).

## Daishan Island: the one opening that is its own economy

Daishan Island is the only opening that is not on the giant mainland component. The parsed
island is about 701 passable tiles with water within 17 tiles in every direction. In FFA terms
that is a different game: no mainland neighbor can reach your core without paying for boats and
a defended landing, so your first pressure is time and your own income, not a neighbor. That
is a real advantage — you are effectively unattackable for the opening window — and a real
cost — you are also slow to reach the contested central land and any Port fight that decides
the mainland.

Treat the island as a defensive choke rather than a fast start. Your first target is the
island's own Nation and neutral land, which you clear without a defender; your first serious
decision is when to fund the crossing. Too early and you spend your reserve on boats before
the mainland's Port fight is decided, possibly arriving to an already-owned far bank. Too late
and you have a safe but small economy while eight players have cleared the central land and
started pushing toward each other. A useful middle is: clear the island, build the first Port
that funds the crossing, then send the smallest boat force that can hold the landing you
choose — not the whole reserve. If the far bank is already contested, the crossing is an escape
or a late pivot, not a first move. This differs from the mainland logic, where a Port is often
worth buying early because water access is nearly universal there. On the island, water is
your only road, so a Port is a lifeline, not an option. Keep the mainland table in mind, not
against you: the eight mainland players will be deciding among themselves, and your island
position is best judged by when it can join that fight at a defensible point, not by matching
their early tempo. For actual naval allocation and escort once you cross, use [Transport
landings](/guides/transport-landings/); for whether a Nation pressure is worth answering, see
[Nations pressure](/guides/nations-pressure/). One more practical difference between the
island and the mainland: your income clock is the only pressure. On the mainland you can
measure the enemy — a neighbor's reserve, a Port landing, a captured Nation — and time your
move against an object. On the island the only object that ever changes is your own Gold and
your own boat readiness, so the decision is purely internal: “can I fund the crossing and
still hold the landing?” That makes the island a good opening for a player who wants to avoid
the first-minute scramble, and a bad opening for a player who needs the central land to win,
because by the time the island's economy can send a defended force, the mainland's Port fight
is usually decided. The island is best used as a delay-and-pivot, not as a first strike: hold
the water, fund the smallest crossing that holds one landing, and re-decide from the far bank
with a reserve intact.

## The first-minute playbook by archetype

The same three questions apply to every opening, but the answers differ by archetype. A
**bank** opening (Changzhou, Zhenjiang, Nantong, Ma'anshan, Taizhou) is close enough to water
that a Port is realistically open early. Its first-minute move is usually to verify the
nearest Port or boat landing and decide whether to buy it before a neighbor does, while still
clearing the first neutral Nation. A **corner** opening (Wuhu, Shanghai) has fewer exposed
borders and a slower water approach; its first-minute move is usually to grow compact land and
take the corner Nation first, deferring the Port until the second target is safe. An
**island** opening (Daishan) defers both; its first-minute move is to clear the island and
fund the crossing.

Within those archetypes, use a ratio ramp rather than a fixed number. A player's common
calibration is to start around a low attack ratio and push toward the mid-range as neutral
land thins out, but the ratio is a planning index, not a rule: it is set by how thick the
first target's border is and how close the neighbor is. The build order follows the same
logic. If you are landlocked behind the first Nation, a City that holds capacity and defends
the border is the first big buy. If water is directly beside you, a Port that funds movement
and trade is often the first big buy — the repeated community failure is to skip the Port, run
out of income, and starve before the second target. A defensive structure that stops a
nuclear strike is worth buying once you can afford it, but it is a poor first buy on the
island, where there is little to defend against early and the crossing is the real priority.
Clear neutral Nations early and often, because a surrounded Nation disappears at a minimal
troop cost and extends your income without a fight; but do not over-send, because pushing a
large force at a modest target can spend most of your reserve for a small gain. End the first
minute with a held first target, a recorded current reserve, and a named second target, so the
next decision is a reaction to the board rather than a repeat of the first move. If you only
have time to check two things before the first attack, check them in this order: first that
the nearest Port or boat landing is still open to you, and second that a second, independent
target is named so a failed first move does not strand the whole opening.

## Failure modes and their counters

The openings fail in a few repeatable ways, and each has a concrete counter. **Dying without a
Port:** you grow land but skip the water, your income stalls, and the second target is
unreachable. Counter: on any mainland opening, keep a named water target and buy the Port
when the first Nation is secured; if the Port is contested, take a smaller land pocket and
re-approach from the other side rather than abandoning water. **The neighbor steals the city:**
a nearby human takes the first central structure before you arrive, removing your intended
second target. Counter: keep the first target compact and defended, and carry the reserve that
can retake it or pivot to the fallback; do not chase the lost structure if the home border
cannot answer. **Over-sending:** you push most of your troops at a Nation and spend far more
than the target is worth, leaving your core exposed. Counter: size the commit so the reserve
can still answer the closest response, and stop the attack if the target's border is thicker
than assumed. **Defense-post lockout:** a neighbor spreads defensive structures across your
expansion direction and shrinks your options. Counter: rotate to the bank or corner direction
where their posts are thinner, and stop pressing the locked face. **Boxed-in adjacency:** you
treat a close neighbor as comfortable and end up sharing a border that they control. Counter:
read adjacency as a threat by default, keep an independent fallback that does not cross their
land, and extend neutral clearing rather than attacking them head-on until you can afford the
counter. Each of these has a visible trigger you can watch in the match — a captured Port, a
lost structure, a spent reserve, a spread of posts, a shared border — so the counter is a
reaction to an object, not a guess. When a fight is already active and you need allocation and
retreat, switch to [Land combat](/guides/land-combat/); when you must answer a dangerous
Nation border before the next expansion, use [Nations mechanics](/mechanics/nations/) for the
rule boundary and [Map strategy](/guides/map-strategy/) to re-read the topology. Two more
failure modes are Yangtze-specific because of the pipe shape. **Wrong-bank expansion:** you
open onto the bank that both your neighbors also push onto, so your first target is a
contested strip rather than a clean pocket. Counter: before committing, check which bank your
nearest neighbors are actually moving toward and prefer the quieter bank, even if it is a few
tiles farther from your Port. **Delta congestion:** on the bottom arm, several mainland
openings (Shanghai, Nantong, Taizhou) converge on the same delta coast, so the “second
shore” you assumed exists is already owned. Counter: verify the delta Port and its landing
before treating it as a fallback, and keep an inland (not coastal) second target so a lost
delta does not remove your fallback. Each of these has a visible trigger you can watch in the
match — a captured Port, a lost structure, a spent reserve, a spread of posts, a shared
border, a contested bank, an owned delta — so the counter is a reaction to an object, not a
guess.

## Scenario A: a center start on the Changzhou bank

Assume a public Yangtze River FFA with manual spawn available, roughly 50 human slots, and
your legal start on the Changzhou anchor. These are **scenario assumptions**, not a lobby
promise or a fixed coordinate. Changzhou is the closest default opening to the river (distance
24), so your Port and boat are the fastest on the map, but the central land is also where the
north and east arms meet, so at least two other humans can reach the same neutral Nation in
the same window. Assume the nearest neutral Nation is a short land push away, a Port sits on
the near bank, and a second neutral pocket lies past the river on the far arm. You have 100
units of current Troops as a planning index, and you refuse to leave fewer than 45 available
to answer the nearest human border.

Read: confirm the lobby is Yangtze River, mark the two neighbors who can contest the central
Nation, and note the Port on the near bank. Inspect: verify the Port landing is reachable and
whether the far arm is already claimed; do not buy the Port because the thumbnail shows water.
Validate the first target as a bounded land capture of the nearest neutral Nation with a
reassessment at the Nation border, and name the fallback as the far arm **only if** the
landing there is still open and does not cross a neighbor's held land. The first move commits
at most the portion that leaves the 45-unit response reserve; it does not have to spend all 55.
After the pocket, compare the new shape: did it shorten the approach to the Port, or did it
create a long front beside the northern neighbor? If the neighbor takes the central Nation
while you measure the Port, defend the core and stop the Nation branch — a captured structure
is not worth a lost exit. If the far arm is unowned and the neighbors are committed elsewhere,
a small boat can preserve an escape to the far bank.

Suppose the Port is legal but the far arm is already claimed by the eastern neighbor. The water
fallback is now false; rewrite the note so the primary is to hold the neutral pocket and probe
the Nation border, and the fallback is the northern arm if that neighbor has not sealed it.
If both are closed, the decision is defense or a different front, not repeated boats. The
success test is not “own the center.” After two decisions you should have a held pocket, a
recorded reserve, a verified second destination, and an answer to both neighbors. If you
gained land but cannot name a second route, the center's proximity has not yet become control.
If you kept the reserve but never tested a route, you have spent the opening without deciding
it. For the actual wave size once a crossing begins, use [Transport landings](/guides/transport-landings/).

## Scenario B: a southwest corner start in Wuhu

Assume a different public Yangtze River FFA with 40 human players, a legal manual start on the
Wuhu anchor, an open first land pocket, and a coast that reaches water at distance 38. These
are **explicit hypothetical match state**, not a fixed rotation or a guaranteed coordinate.
Wuhu is the southwest corner: two quiet borders and the lowest immediate neighbor pressure of
the eight mainland openings, but a slower path to the central river fight. The trade is tempo
for fewer fronts — you clear less contested land early, but you have fewer directions a
neighbor can hit you from. A player's comment that the opening is “where luck is the biggest
factor” is the reason the corner is attractive: with fewer neighbors, the opening window is
less likely to end in the first two minutes.

Read: confirm the selected map is Yangtze River, then mark the two corner borders, the first
neutral or weak Nation toward the interior, and the nearest coast. Inspect the coast
separately: is the water continuous to a useful destination, and can you reach the Port
without exposing the starting City? If the answer is not yet visible, make the interior land
path primary and leave the coast unpurchased. Assume a planning ledger of 300,000 Gold and a
100,000 emergency reserve; those numbers illustrate a spending boundary, not a current
Factory, Port, or City price. Name the primary lane as a connected interior land expansion with
a stopping point at the first human-controlled neck. A corner can accumulate safe tiles while
the center contests Nation structures, but an unguarded narrow line toward the center can
invite a counterattack that erases the gain. Keep the conquered shape compact. At each pocket,
check whether the next border has a defender, whether the adjacent human can arrive before
your Troops recover, and whether a City would help capacity or only pin Gold in a lost
corridor. Name the fallback as the coastal route **only after** entry and landing are
verified.

Now suppose the closest human captures the first interior neck while you are still building.
The original interior route has failed its stop test. Do not throw a larger force down the
same line. If your coast offers a defensible landing and the home reserve survives, test a
bounded crossing; if it requires a long trip under a hostile ship, keep the emergency Gold and
reinforce the corner core while searching a different land border. If a Nation Port falls to
you on the way, value it by current connectivity and settlement, not by a remembered Port
count. The comparison with Changzhou is the lesson: the corner pays for safety with slowness,
the center pays for options with exposure, and neither has a fixed length in minutes. With 40
humans rather than 50, encounter order changes again and Nations may hold different borders. A
good Wuhu opening ends the first phase with a protected land core, a checked interior path, and
a genuinely separate coastal alternative. A bad one ends with a long thin line and a
hypothetical Port that cannot answer a human attack. Preserve the alternative, then use
[Land combat](/guides/land-combat/) when the fight is active.

## Exit signals, version limits, and the next decision

Exit is a verb, not a post-match apology. End the first branch when the next target is owned
by a stronger human, the only crossing is not navigable or defensible, the Nation response
consumes the reserved force, or the core can be attacked faster than the expansion pays back.
A route can also finish successfully: once it reaches a durable second pocket, stop calling it
an opening and move to the broader board scan. The purpose of an exit rule is to stop past
spending from becoming a reason for more spending. If the route is blocked, write the new
primary and fallback before sending another wave.

The most common Yangtze mistake is treating the country label as the answer. The official
manifest and generator file define the nine anchors; they do not say who is beside you, which
Port is reachable, or which bank is open in your lobby. The second mistake is assuming a
center is always best because it is on the river; the center's advantage is option density,
and its cost is neighbor convergence. The third is skipping a Port on the mainland because
water “looks optional,” when in fact nearly every mainland opening can reach it and income
depends on it. The fourth is buying a defensive structure on the island before the crossing,
which pins Gold where it does not help. Each has a test: read the live board, verify
ownership and settlement, name the current reserve, and check the independent fallback.

Mode changes matter. In a random-spawn lobby you cannot preselect Changzhou or Wuhu and must
re-run the per-opening checklist after placement. In a room with different modifiers or a
different map size, encounter order and the distance numbers shift, so re-read the board
instead of scaling a remembered timestamp. In a team room, a teammate can guard a different
exit, but the independence of two lanes must be evaluated with shared borders and roles; for
shared positioning use [Team spawn formation](/guides/team-spawn-formation/). This page is
verified to the upstream commit that added Yangtze River (`35a3692d`, PR #4807) and the v0.34
resources as of 2026-09-29; future releases can change map assets, rotation weights, and
Nation behavior, so re-check the [official release notes](https://github.com/openfrontio/OpenFrontIO/releases) when a new version lands.

Before queueing, make a four-line note: selected opening, primary lane, independent fallback,
and stop signal. After the first capture, update all four. If the fallback no longer exists,
reduce exposure until you find one. If the primary gives you a protected second pocket, stop
optimizing the opening and decide what the whole board now requires. The next useful reading
is [FFA opening](/strategies/ffa-opening/) for generic timing, [Map strategy](/guides/map-strategy/)
for other layouts, [Threat assessment](/guides/threat-assessment/) for a full board scan, or
[Transport landings](/guides/transport-landings/) for an actual naval operation. The Yangtze
answer is complete when the player can choose, disprove, and replace a route in the live match
rather than repeat somebody else's start.
