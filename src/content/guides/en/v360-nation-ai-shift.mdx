**Short answer:** Three Nation behaviors changed in v0.36.0 and all three are decision-relevant. On Medium, Hard, and Impossible an island nation with no land front now boats up to 3 nearby bots across the water before it bothers with players, so a bot you were relying on is no longer safe. A single tick can no longer spend a nation's whole army, because every same-tick send now deducts from a shared reserve — a "dump its army" trick no longer works. And with atom bombs disabled, the nation saves its gold toward a hydrogen bomb instead of a warship fleet, so you see fewer ships but a delayed, heavier nuclear threat. Read the island nation as a cross-water probe, expect a bigger home reserve to re-engage after it pulls back, and track its gold as a bomb timer.

If your old notes say "the nation just sits and builds" or "one push flushes the whole nation," those notes are now wrong for v0.36.0 and later. The change is not that nations got a bigger army — they spent the same army differently, and in a way that changes three of your calls: which targets you trust, whether a single commitment beats them, and what you are actually defending against when there are fewer warships out. This guide walks through each of the three changes with the exact thresholds, then turns them into a reading habit and a set of moves.

## What changed and why it matters to you

The v0.36.0 release ships three distinct changes to Nation attack behavior, and they look like a patch note but they are really a re-tuning of how the nation turns its army into pressure. The first is the island fallback: previously, a nation sitting on an island with no land neighbor to fight would skip bots entirely when choosing its overseas target, so it only ever boated a human or another nation. Now, on Medium, Hard, and Impossible, when it has no bot nearby on land and no non-allied land neighbor, the nation pulls a shortlist of the 3 nearest bots within boat range and boats the first one it can reach and afford. The practical effect is that an island nation will build a foothold on a nearby bot instead of immediately pressing you, and that changes the map state in ways you did not have to model before.

The second change is the same-tick reserve. Before, a nation that triggered two attacks in the same tick — a beachhead follow-up plus a regular attack, for example — could spend troops it had already committed, effectively sending its whole army and leaving its home base nearly empty. Now every send goes through a shared accounting where the home reserve is the troops minus whatever has already left this tick, so the nation cannot over-commit in a single frame. The nation you were trying to flush with one big push now keeps a meaningful core at home and recovers faster.

The third change is the hydrogen hoarding. When atom bombs are disabled in a lobby, the nation previously spent its gold on warships to counter an assumed naval threat. Now it saves that gold toward a hydrogen bomb and only counters a warship infestation when one is actually real. Fewer warships, a bigger and later bomb. These three are separate enough to reason about one at a time, but they share a theme: the nation is no longer a predictable, single-minded actor that empties itself into one target. It probes, it reserves, and it banks — and your old counterplay was built against the pre-v0.36 version of all three. The unifying point is that none of the three makes the nation weaker in the way a nerf usually does; each one changes where its pressure comes from and when, so the right response is to change what you watch, not to build something bigger to hit a target that is now somewhere else.

## The island nation boats nearby bots first

This is the change that will surprise you most because it changes who gets attacked, not how strong the attack is. The rule, exactly: on Easy nothing changes. On Medium, Hard, and Impossible, if a nation has no bot bordering it by land and no non-allied land neighbor — that is, it is effectively an island or a sea-cut nation — it builds an overseas target list. That list is the 3 nearest bots whose gap from the nation's largest cluster is within boat range. The boat range is 300 tiles on Hard and 500 on Impossible, and the existing boat cap and the transport-ship unit availability still apply, so if the nation is at its max number of boats or transports are disabled in the lobby, the list is empty and it falls back to the old behavior.

The nation then boats the first bot on that list it can reach and afford. If the nearest bot is too strong to take right now, it skips it and moves to the next one on the list. It also keeps a record of which bots one of its boats is already sailing to, so it does not send boat after boat at the same bot. This matters for three of your decisions. First, a bot that was "safe" because it was across water from an island nation is no longer safe — the island nation will quietly annex it and turn it into a launch point. Second, the nation is now choosing its overseas target by proximity and affordability among bots, which means the weakest and the nearest bot is the one in danger, not the player you were defending. Third, the bot attack troop budget resets every time this strategy runs, even when the nation has no neighboring bots, so an island nation no longer carries over a stale budget that made its first overseas attack oddly sized.

What to do about it: if an island nation is sitting across water from a bot you care about, treat that bot as already lost on Medium and above. Either grab the bot first yourself, cut the island nation's boat route, or accept that the nation will soon have a second landmass and a new front. The 300/500 tile range means on Impossible an island nation can reach surprisingly far across the map, so on large or many-nation lobbies the "safe bot" you parked your strategy around can be inside its 500-tile window even if it looks like the far side of the map. Notice also that the list is rebuilt each time the strategy runs, so as the map evolves — a bot annexed, a new structure built — the shortlist can change, meaning the bot the nation targets at minute ten can differ from the one it targeted at minute two even though nothing you did changed the rules.

## No whole-army double attack in one tick

The second change is quieter but it directly breaks a specific trick that worked before: committing your full force at the moment the nation committed its full force, to flush it and take its home. That trick depended on the nation being able to send its whole army in one tick. It could, because the two attacks it triggered that tick — a beachhead follow-up and a normal attack, or two fronts at once — were both measured against the full army rather than against what was left after the first one left. So a double commit could take the nation's home troops down to near zero, and your well-timed strike could then delete it.

v0.36.0 closes that. The engine now computes the home reserve as the nation's current troops minus the troops it has already sent this tick, and every send goes through a single function that deducts from that reserve. So the second attack in the same tick only sees the remainder. A nation that would have emptied itself now keeps whatever is left after the first send, and the total it can commit in one frame is bounded by the troops it actually had at the start of the tick. This is not a nerf to the nation's army size; it is a guardrail against the nation shooting itself in the foot. And because the nation no longer flushes itself, your counterplay that waited for the flush is now waiting for a moment that does not come the same way.

The decision this forces: stop timing your strike around the nation's commitment. You no longer get the "it just emptied its home" window, so a single all-in against the nation's home is less likely to catch it soft. Instead, plan for the nation to hold a reserve. That means your attack has to beat the nation's home plus a reserve, not just whatever it had parked there. Practically, that is a stronger push than before, or a two-phase push where you take a front, wait for the nation to feed it, and then hit the home that is now thinner but never zero. The [cross-water commitment guide](/guides/cross-water-commitment/) covers when a full commitment is still worth it; the new rule is that "full" now means full against a reserve that will always be there, which raises the bar. The practical test of the new rule is simple: after the nation's first big send, look at its home troops. In v0.35 and earlier that number could be close to zero; in v0.36.0 it is visibly higher and bounded by what the nation actually had at the start of the tick. That visible reserve is the whole change made concrete, and it is the number you now plan your push around.

## Hydrogen hoarding when atom bombs are off

The third change only shows up in lobbies where atom bombs are disabled, but in those lobbies it completely changes the naval picture and the nuclear picture at the same time. Before, the nation assumed it was facing a warship threat and spent its gold on warships to counter it — so a no-atom lobby was full of nation warships, and your defense was a naval one. Now, the nation saves that gold toward a hydrogen bomb instead, and it only counters a warship infestation when there is a real one on the map. The release note is blunt about the swap: "with atom bombs disabled, nations save up for hydrogen bombs instead of spending it all on warships, and only counter real warship infestations."

So in a no-atom lobby the nation fleet shrinks and the nuclear threat becomes a single, delayed, big one. Fewer warships is good for you on the surface — you have less to screen and less to worry about on your coast. But the saved gold is not gone; it is being converted into a hydrogen bomb, which is the heavier strike. The timing is the tell: early in the game a no-atom nation looks quiet at sea, mid-game it looks quiet at sea and a little richer, and then the bomb comes. The decision this forces is a re-prioritization of what you defend. You no longer need a standing warship screen against a nation fleet that is mostly not there, but you do need to read the nation's gold and its bomb build, and you need a plan for the moment the bomb is ready. The [nuke warning circles guide](/guides/nuke-warning-circles/) tells you how to read the blast radius once the bomb is in the air; this change tells you to expect the bomb itself in a no-atom lobby.

Practically, track the no-atom nation's gold the way you would track a bomb timer. A nation that is hoarding gold and not fielding ships is not weak — it is saving. Do not mistake a quiet coast for safety. If you can, pressure the gold source or the bomb's funding chain, because the most durable counter to a hoarding nation is to make it never reach the bomb, not to out-gun the bomb once it exists. And if the lobby has atom bombs enabled, none of this applies — the nation still fields warships the old way and the hydrogen-hoarding branch is dormant.

## Version boundaries: what is new, what carried over

It is worth being precise about what v0.36.0 changed versus what was already true in v0.35, because the community's memory of "how nations work" is still mostly v0.35. What carried over unchanged: the 1% boat / beachhead sizing rule (a beachhead sends 1% of the nation's troops, at least 1), the warship checks the nation runs before it commits, the 300-tile Hard and 500-tile Impossible boat-range limits, the annexation and wall-in logic on Hard and Impossible, and the general bot-priority behavior that puts bots owning structures at the top of the target list. None of those moved.

What v0.36.0 added: the overseas bot fallback for island nations (the 3-nearest-bots-within-boat-range shortlist, bots-first targeting, the skip-already-boat rule, and the per-run budget reset), the same-tick home reserve (the `homeTroops` minus `troopsSentThisTick` accounting and the single send path), and the hydrogen hoarding when atom bombs are disabled. The difficulty boundaries matter. The island fallback applies to Medium, Hard, and Impossible only — Easy is explicitly unchanged, and an island nation on Easy still only boats humans or other nations the way it always did. The same-tick reserve and the hydrogen hoarding are not difficulty-gated the same way; they apply across difficulties whenever their trigger is present (a double commit in one tick, or atom bombs disabled). If you are playing on Easy with island nations, the only one of the three you will notice is the same-tick reserve; the island-boat change is invisible to you there.

One more boundary: all three changes are in v0.36.0 and carried into v0.36.2, the current stable. They were not touched by the v0.36.1 or v0.36.2 follow-up patches, so if you are on any v0.36.x build, the behaviors above are the live ones. If you are on v0.35 or earlier, the old behaviors are still live and this guide's "what changed" framing does not apply to you. The version in the game's settings or the lobby header is the thing to check, not a guide's publish date. One practical consequence of the boundaries: because the island fallback is difficulty-gated while the other two are trigger-gated, the same lobby map can produce a completely different strategic picture at Easy versus Hard — the map is identical, but the island nations behave differently at each difficulty, so your notes about a given map should always carry the difficulty they were made under.

## Decision framework: read the nation before it moves you

Tie the three changes into a habit you can run in under a second each time you look at a nation. The habit has three reads, one per change. Read one — the island read: is there an island or sea-cut nation across water from a bot I care about, at Medium or above? If yes, that bot is on the nation's shortlist and the nation will go for it before me. Act on it now: grab the bot, cut the route, or move your plan off that bot. Read two — the reserve read: am I timing a strike around the nation emptying its home? If yes, stop — the flush no longer comes, the nation holds a reserve, and my push has to beat the reserve, not just the parked troops. Read three — the gold read: is this a no-atom lobby and is the nation hoarding gold without fielding warships? If yes, the threat is a delayed hydrogen bomb, not a fleet, so I defend the bomb build, not the coast.

The framework is really about replacing three old assumptions with three new ones. Old assumption one: "a bot across water is safe from an island nation." New: it is not, on Medium and above. Old assumption two: "if I hit the nation right after it commits, it is soft." New: it is not soft, it has a reserve, and the commit is now bounded by what it actually had. Old assumption three: "no atom bombs means no nuclear threat and a normal fleet." New: no atom bombs means a smaller fleet and a bigger, later bomb. Each replacement changes a concrete move you make. The first changes which target you trust. The second changes the size and timing of your push. The third changes what you spend your defensive budget on. None of them require a new build or a new map; all three are reads you can add to the moment you already spend looking at a nation, and each one is cheap to check because the signal (an island across water, a timing habit, a gold line with few ships) is visible on the board.

A useful way to hold the whole framework is to remember that the three reads map to three different timescales. The island read is now — a bot in range is going to be boated soon. The reserve read is the next few moments — your single push will not catch a flush. The gold read is the whole match — the bomb is coming, so spend the match on the funding chain. Mixing those timescales up is the most common error players make after this patch, because they react to the fast change (the island boat) and ignore the slow one (the hoarded gold), or they prep for the bomb and get caught by the fast probe. Keep them separate and you cover all three.

## Scenario A: the island nation is boating the bot I wanted

Set the situation with numbers. You are on Impossible in a 30-nation lobby on a map with a lot of open water. You are a mid-sized nation hugging a coast, and 400 tiles to the east is a smaller island nation at roughly your strength. Between you and the island nation, and slightly closer to the island, sits a bot with a single structure. Your old plan was to let the island nation and the bot fight each other or to ignore the bot and push the land nation on your left. That plan is now broken, and you can see why in about ten seconds.

On Impossible, the island nation's boat range is 500 tiles. The bot is well inside that window, and it is the nearest bot to the island nation. Because the island nation has no land neighbor (it is an island) and no bot bordering it on land, its overseas strategy is now active: it pulls the 3 nearest bots within 500 tiles, and this bot is first or second on that list. It can reach it and it can afford it — the bot is smaller than the island nation — so the island nation will send a boat for that bot now, and if the bot has a structure the bot is even higher priority because a bot with a structure is one the nation wants to take and delete. The bot you were counting on to soak the island nation's first attack, or to give you a distraction, is instead becoming the island nation's second landmass. Once the island nation holds that bot, it has two fronts and a new launch point, and your "let them fight the bot" plan has become "now I fight the island nation and its bot at once."

The moves, in order of preference. Best: beat the island nation to the bot. If you can boat that bot faster than the island nation can, grab it and deny the nation's foothold entirely — you now own the structure the nation wanted, and the nation is back to a single landmass. This usually works when the bot is closer to you than to the island nation, or when you have a transport already in position. Good: cut the route. If you cannot reach the bot first, place a warship or a defense position on the water between the island nation and the bot so the island nation's boat cannot get through; the nation's overseas strategy skips a bot it cannot reach, so you push it down the list to a weaker, less useful target. Acceptable: re-plan off the bot. If neither is in time, stop relying on that bot in your plan and treat the island nation as already having a second front. You do not have to fight it this tick, but your map model now has the island nation with two landmasses, and you spend the next few minutes deciding whether to cut that second landmass off before it grows. The key is that you noticed within a tick or two, because the island nation's boat is sailing and you can see it.

## Scenario B: no-atom lobby, the nation is hoarding and I have no fleet answer

Now the second scenario, with numbers. You are on Hard in a 12-player, 60-nation custom lobby where atom bombs are disabled. You are a large coastal nation with a Port and a couple of cities. For the first ten minutes the nation on your coast is doing almost nothing at sea — one warship, maybe two, and a steady gold line climbing. Your old instinct from a v0.35 no-atom lobby is "field a warship screen, this is a naval fight," but the nation is not fielding warships. On v0.36.0 that is the tell, not an oversight: because atom bombs are off, the nation is saving its gold toward a hydrogen bomb and only counters a warship infestation when one is real. There is no infestation, so it is not building ships. The gold is going to the bomb.

Here is the arithmetic you can roughly read off the board. The nation's gold line is the proxy for the bomb timer. A hydrogen bomb is the expensive strike, so the nation has to accumulate a large gold pool before it can build it, and it will not spend that pool on warships. Early, the nation looks weak at sea and you are tempted to push its coast because it has no fleet. That push is the trap. The nation's land is well-defended because it is not bleeding gold into a fleet, and if you push and stall, the nation keeps saving while you spend troops. By the time you have spent a third of your army on a stalled coastal push, the nation's gold line is high and the bomb is close to buildable. The decision the scenario forces is the same every time: do I spend the match making the bomb never build, or do I spend the match defending against the bomb once it is ready? The first is almost always the better trade, because a bomb you prevent costs you a pressure campaign, while a bomb you answer costs you a structure and a front.

The moves. Pressure the gold source, not the coast. The nation's gold comes from territory, cities, and its Port, so the most durable counter is to take or threaten the gold line: push a front that costs the nation troops to defend (so it has to spend, not save), or cut the Port and Factory that feed the bomb. Screen the coast minimally, not maximally — you do not need a standing fleet against a nation that is not fielding one, so a single warship or a defense post is enough to stop a stray landing, and you keep the rest of your budget for the pressure front. Watch the gold line and the bomb build indicator, and the moment the nation crosses into the range where the hydrogen bomb is buildable, that is your signal to either strike the bomb's funding hard or to prepare the blast-radius defense the nuke-warning-circles guide describes. The whole scenario is about not mistaking a quiet, ship-light coast for a safe coast: the quiet is the saving.

## Failure modes and counterplay

The three changes each have a failure mode — the way a player will get it wrong — and each has a counter. The island fallback's failure mode is over-reaction: you see an island nation across water and you commit half your army to a bot that is not actually on its shortlist because it is out of the 300/500 range, or you are on Easy where the change does not apply at all. The counter is the cheap check before you move: confirm the difficulty is Medium or above, confirm the gap is inside boat range, and confirm the island nation actually has no land front. If any of those fail, the island nation is not going to boat that bot, and your army is better off where it was.

The same-tick reserve's failure mode is under-reaction: you keep waiting for the flush that no longer comes, and because you never strike, the nation feeds its fronts and grows while you hold your "perfect timing" that is never right. The counter is to change the trigger for your strike. You no longer strike on "the nation just committed everything." You strike on "the nation has fed a front and its home is thinner than it was," which is a smaller but more reliable window, or you run a two-phase push that deliberately thins the home by taking a front first and then hitting the reduced home. The move is to accept a smaller edge for a real edge, instead of chasing the big edge that the patch removed.

The hydrogen hoarding's failure mode is mis-reading the quiet coast as safety and pushing into a well-defended, gold-rich nation, bleeding your army against a nation that is not bleeding gold. The counter is the gold-line read and the pressure-the-source move from scenario B. The fourth, cross-cutting failure is mixing the timescales: reacting to the fast island probe with a fast response and ignoring the slow bomb, or vice versa. The counter is to keep the three reads — island now, reserve next-moments, gold whole-match — explicitly separate, and to spend your attention in proportion to each timescale. The most common post-patch loss is the player who handles the island probe fine, handles the reserve fine, and then gets deleted by the hydrogen bomb they never tracked because the coast looked so quiet. A final, cross-cutting counter that covers all four: keep a running note on the nation you are most concerned with, updated every thirty seconds with just two numbers — its gold and its home troops. The gold line tells you the bomb timing and whether the coast is quietly saving; the home troops tell you whether a strike is about to pay. Two cheap numbers, checked on a rhythm, are what actually make the three reads stick instead of staying three separate paragraphs you have to recall from memory mid-match.

## Mode and map adjustments

The three changes interact with mode and map in predictable ways, so adjust your default play by lobby type. On FFA with many nations and open water — the classic big-water lobbies — the island fallback is the one that matters most, because there are lots of island or sea-cut nations and lots of bots inside a 500-tile window on Impossible. In those lobbies, check every bot you were going to use as a buffer or a distraction: any of them inside a Medium-or-above island nation's boat range is a candidate for the nation's first overseas move, and you should either take it first or not plan around it. On FFA with atom bombs off, the hoarding change stacks on top: the nation fleets are small and the bombs are coming, so the whole lobby is running on the gold-line read.

On 2v2 or team modes, the same-tick reserve matters more than the island fallback, because your fights are more about when you and your teammate strike a shared nation. The old "we hit the tick the nation committed" timing is gone, so coordinate around the thinner-but-present home instead, and time a combined push so the nation has to feed one of your fronts and then answer the reduced home. On small maps or maps with little water, the island fallback is barely visible — there are few or no island nations and few bots across water — so the reserve and the hoarding are the two reads you will actually feel, and the island read becomes a "there is no island nation here, skip this one" decision.

On maps where atom bombs are off by lobby design (custom or tournament lobbies that disable them), the hydrogen hoarding is the dominant change and it changes your whole defensive posture: fewer warships to screen, a bomb to track, and a funding chain to pressure. On default FFA where atom bombs are on, the hoarding branch is dormant and the nation still fields warships the old way, so your naval defense is the same as it always was. The map's water share is the single best predictor of how much the island fallback will matter to you: a map that is mostly land with a few straits will see very little island-boat action, while a map that is a scatter of islands and open water will see it on almost every nation. So read the map first, then decide which of the three reads to weight heaviest, and let that weighting drive your first few moves rather than reacting after a bot is already being boated.

## Verify it yourself in a lobby

You do not have to take the thresholds on faith; you can check all three in a practice lobby in about five minutes, and verifying beats trusting because the exact range and the exact gold behavior can feel different on a real map. For the island fallback, start an FFA on a water-heavy map at Hard or Impossible with a decent nation count, and find an island nation with no land neighbor. Watch it for a few minutes. On v0.36.0 you will see it send a transport toward a nearby bot rather than sitting idle or immediately boating a player, and you will see it pick the nearest affordable bot and skip any bot it already has a boat going to. If you repeat the same lobby on Easy, the island nation does not do this — it only boats players or other nations — which is your direct confirmation of the difficulty boundary.

For the same-tick reserve, put a nation under pressure on two fronts at once, or watch a nation that runs a beachhead follow-up, and look at its home troops in the moment it commits. On v0.36.0 the home does not drop to near zero the way it used to; it keeps a reserve equal to whatever it had minus what it just sent, and it recovers. The fastest way to feel it is to try the old "flush and hit the home" move once and watch the home survive at a level that makes the move not pay. For the hydrogen hoarding, start a custom or a no-atom lobby and watch a coastal nation's gold and its warship count for ten minutes. You will see the warship count stay low while the gold line climbs, which is the hoarding signature, and if you can watch long enough or check the bomb build state, you will see the gold converting to the hydrogen bomb instead of to ships.

The reason to do this check is not that the guide is wrong — the thresholds are verified against the release and the tag source — but that your own confirmation turns the numbers into a feel. Once you have seen the island nation boat a bot with your own eyes, the island read becomes a reflex instead of a rule you have to remember, and the 500-tile window on Impossible stops being an abstract number and becomes a concrete "that bot over there is in range." Three minutes of watching each change is the cheapest way to make the next real lobby feel like you knew it was coming.

Use the table the way the guide means it: it is a check, not a recipe. The "first move" column is the default, and you adjust it with the scenario moves above when the specifics differ. If you find yourself stuck on a single nation in a lobby, pick the one read that applies — almost always the island read first, because it is the fastest to confirm and the fastest to matter — and work the other two reads only if the first one is quiet. The goal of the table is to make the three reads automatic enough that the next lobby does not surprise you, and that you spend your attention where the nation is actually spending its army. If you want to drill it before a real game, set up the practice lobby from the verification section and run through the table once per change: confirm the island read against a real island nation, watch the reserve after a real commit, and track a no-atom nation's gold for ten minutes. Three short drills, each mapped to one row of the table, are enough to turn the guide's framework into muscle memory rather than a reference you have to open mid-match.
