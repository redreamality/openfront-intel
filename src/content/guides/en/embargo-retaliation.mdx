When a rival embargoes you, the trade route between the two of you is already severed on **both** ends: at v0.34.24 `canTrade` returns false if either player has an embargo against the other, so your trade ships stop the moment they act and you do not need to mirror them to feel the loss. Retaliation is therefore a choice among three real moves — mirror the embargo (cheap, zero gold, a diplomatic signal), attack the rival's dispatching port (expensive, structural, removes their rebuild cost), or de-escalate with a gold donation (one-way gold, the only move that actually improves your relation) — and the right one depends on whether their embargo was temporary or permanent, whether they are a partner you want to keep, and what your share of the route's gold-per-arrival is.

## What the embargo actually does to your economy

The first thing to internalize is that an embargo is a **trade filter, not a military sanction**. At v0.34.24 the only gameplay effect of holding an embargo against another player is in `GameRunner.canTrade`, which returns false the moment either side holds one. That single gate means every trade ship that would otherwise sail between you and the rival stops being dispatched, and no new dispatches start once the embargo is in place. It does **not** block troop movement across the same water, it does **not** block gold donations, it does **not** block attacking their cities, it does **not** stop you from building a port next to them, and it does **not** prevent warships from patrolling the route — it is purely a trade gate. This distinction frames your entire option set: an embargo removes exactly one income stream, the shared trade route, and the rest of the economy keeps running on its normal schedule, which is why the correct response is almost never "panic" and is almost always "re-price what the route was worth to you." The damage number you will compare every retaliation against is your personal share of the route's gold-per-arrival, and it takes a minute to compute, so do it before you read on.

The second thing to internalize is the **asymmetry of loss**, and it is the reason the route's length matters more than its prestige. The payout formula at `Config.ts` is `75_000 / (1 + exp(-0.03 * (dist - 300))) + 50 * dist` per completed arrival, with the short-range debuff set at 300 tiles. A 1,000-tile route pays roughly 125,000 gold per arrival, a 600-tile route about 105,000, a 300-tile route about 52,500, and a 2,000-tile route about 175,000. Your personal share is a fraction of the full figure — closer to half when the two of you are the only users of the route, and less when nations or a third player also sail it. The "damage" of the embargo is your half of those numbers, and the "response cost" of any retaliation is a separate set of numbers: the 125k–1M port cost of a rebuild, the troops committed to an attack, or the gold burned on a donation. If the route was worth only a small fraction of a 300-tile route's payout to you and your available options cost 125k gold or a city's worth of troops, the retaliation is not paying for itself on the economy axis and the correct first move is the cheapest one available, which for most players is the mirror.

## The temporary-versus-permanent timer

The most overlooked variable in any embargo dispute is **time**, and it is the single most common reason players over-respond. At v0.34.24 a temporary embargo lasts exactly 3,000 ticks, and at the standard game speed of 10 ticks per second that is **5 real minutes** — the source comment on `temporaryEmbargoDuration` even says `// 5 minutes.` — after which the temporary embargo lifts on its own and trade resumes without either side having to act. A **permanent** embargo has no such timer: it persists until the rival clears it, you pressure them hard enough that they cannot maintain it, or the match ends. This split matters because it caps the damage window of one of the two types and leaves the other unbounded. When the rival used the temporary option, the maximum loss is fixed and known in advance: whatever your share of the route is, multiplied by the handful of arrivals you will miss during those five minutes. When the rival used the permanent option, there is no such cap — the loss compounds every arrival until someone breaks the deadlock.

The practical rule is to **read the embargo type before you spend a single resource on a response**. If it is temporary, the default correct move is often to do nothing: wait out the five-minute timer while your existing workers keep accruing gold and your trains keep running, and let the route re-open on its own. Five minutes of lost trade income is a number you can usually afford to absorb — your passive gold accrual, which `goldAdditionRate` sets at a base of **100 gold per tick** for a human player (50 for a bot), keeps running the whole time the trade route is dark, so even the baseline passive accrual keeps banking gold over that window while the route pays nothing. Any retaliation that costs more than the route's lost arrivals during those five minutes is a net loss. If it is permanent, the calculus flips: because the loss is unbounded, the structural responses — building your own port or attacking the rival's dispatch port — stop being wasteful and become the way to end a leak that would otherwise run for the rest of the match.

One more subtlety worth stating: the timer only helps if you **know** the embargo was temporary, and the game surfaces that distinction in the trade menu, so spend the two seconds to confirm it rather than assuming. A rival who opens a temporary embargo is often testing your reaction, waiting for you to over-respond, or simply probing whether you depend on the route; a rival who opens a permanent one is usually signaling that they intend to keep the route closed as a long-term economic pressure. Treating the two the same way is the error that turns a five-minute inconvenience into a 500k-gold port attack you should never have made. The same action can be the right or the wrong move purely on the axis of duration, which is why the timer is the first question in the decision tree at the end of this guide, and the cheapest piece of information you can collect in the entire dispute.

## Whether to mirror the embargo

A mirror embargo is the lowest-cost retaliation and the move players reach for by default, so it is worth understanding precisely what it does and does not do. It costs **zero gold** and takes no meaningful action: you open the trade menu against the same rival and select Embargo, and your player now holds an embargo against them. Because `canTrade` already required **both** directions to be clear, the route's state does not change at the moment you mirror it — you are not doubling the damage, you are adding a second lock. The two real effects are downstream. First, if the rival's embargo was **temporary**, the route stays closed even after their embargo expires, because your mirror becomes the active blocker; that is a genuine, if modest, safeguard against them simply waiting out the timer and immediately re-trading. Second, if the rival later clears their own embargo hoping to re-open trade on one side, your mirror makes that one-sided clearance useless — they must either wait for you to clear yours or escalate.

The cost of a mirror is diplomatic, not economic: it is a visible statement that you have crossed into symmetric hostility. Other players and nations watching can read it as a signal that you will escalate if they embargo you, which is exactly the deterrence value it is bought for. But it also removes the option of a quiet de-escalation, because a mutual embargo is a harder state to unwind than a one-way one: the rival now has to either accept the closure or make a visible move — a gold donation, an attack on you, or simply waiting you out — to change the situation. If their embargo was a mistake or a bluff, mirroring it converts a one-sided error into a two-sided stalemate. The right use of a mirror is therefore narrow and specific: the rival has a **permanent** embargo and is clearly not going to lift it on their own, the route was not worth much to either of you so the permanent closure costs you little, and you want the deterrence signal to register on the wider map. If the route was a large share of your income and the rival is a partner you still want to trade with, the mirror is the wrong tool — use the economic options in the next section instead. The one situation where the mirror is unambiguously correct is the cheap-insurance case: the rival's temporary embargo is about to expire and you can see them building a warship or massing troops, so you mirror at the moment of expiry to hold the route closed through the five-minute gap without spending a single gold of rebuild cost or a single troop of attack.

## Attacking the rival's trade port

When the embargoed route is a large share of your income and the rival is not a partner you want to keep, the high-impact retaliation is an **attack on the trade port that dispatches the route** — not the route itself, because the route is a geometric feature, not a buildable object you can destroy. At v0.34.24 a port costs the rival `min(1_000_000, pow2(numPorts) * 125_000)` to build, where `numPorts` is the number of ports they already own: 125k for a first port, 250k for a second, 500k for a third, and 1M for a fourth and beyond. That means the **third** port on a long map is a 500k-gold investment the rival can be made to lose, and destroying it also removes the **dispatch node** for every route that originates there, so the economic damage is the rebuild cost plus the lost dispatch for every route that shared the node — a compounding hit that goes well beyond the single embargoed route you started with.

The cost of the attack is your own: you must be able to survive the rival's retaliation against the troops you commit. A port is a single structure with limited defence, so a concentrated troop burst from a nearby city can usually take it if the rival is not garrisoning it; the real risk is that the rival **is** garrisoning it, or that your troops are caught out of position and counter-attacked while your home cities are exposed. The attack also has a timing cost: your committed troops are unavailable for anything else for the duration, so if the rival has another front open, the attack can be punished by a counter-move on your exposed cities.

Two worked scenarios make the arithmetic concrete. **Scenario A — mid-match, you hold 12 cities and the rival holds 9.** Your total trade income is about 1,500 gold per tick across three routes, of which the embargoed one contributes about 600. You have just fielded a fresh army from your 11th and 12th cities; the attack on their second port costs about 800 troops and a few minutes of positioning. If you take the port, the rival loses 250k gold in rebuild plus the dispatch node for their two shortest routes; the expected value is strongly positive for you and negative for them, so the attack pays for itself within a few minutes of the trade loss they suffer. **Scenario B — endgame, both of you hold 15 or more cities and the map is saturated.** The rival's ports are all 1M-capped and garrisoned; your troops are already committed to a two-front defence. Here the attack cost exceeds the route's value to you, and the correct move is to de-escalate or build your own port instead of forcing the fight. **Scenario C — the rival just built their first port (125k) and is lightly garrisoning it.** This is the cheapest attack window in the entire game: the rebuild cost is at its floor, the garrison is thin because the rival is still spreading troops across their early expansion, and the dispatch node for their shortest route is a single point of failure. A 500-troop burst from a nearby city takes the port in minutes, and the rival faces either a 125k rebuild or a re-route that costs them 30–40% of their trade income. Scenario C is the one to memorize: it is the highest expected-value port attack in the game, and it is the one players most often miss because they are too focused on the rival's economy and not on the timing of the port build.

## De-escalating with a gold donation

When the rival's embargo is a **bluff** or a **mistake** rather than the opening of a hostile campaign, the only action that actually improves your standing with them is a **gold donation**, because it is the only action in the v0.34.24 action list that raises your relation with the recipient while the embargo is in force. The donation **passes straight through the embargo** because embargoes only filter trade, not diplomacy, so you can send gold to the rival the same tick their embargo is active. The relation update is computed in `DonateGoldExecution.calculateRelationUpdate`: your sent gold is divided into "chunks," each complete chunk grants +5 relation, and the total is capped at +100 per action. That makes the donation a precise, low-risk de-escalation tool — you buy a small, bounded amount of relation, you can watch the rival's reaction, and you can stop spending the moment the signal has been received — which is exactly the property you want in the middle of a trade dispute.

The donation is also the cheapest way to **test the rival's intent** before you commit to a more expensive retaliation, but the numbers are more constrained than players expect. Each donation is converted into "chunks" of gold — and the chunk size is set by the match difficulty: 2,500 gold on Easy, 5,000 on Medium, 12,500 on Hard, and 25,000 on Impossible — with each complete chunk granting +5 relation and a hard cap of +100 per action. The effective chunk size also **grows with game progression**, scaling with the number of ticks elapsed, so the same gold buys fewer relation points late in the match than early. The practical reading: a 5,000-gold donation on a Medium match is exactly one chunk, worth +5 relation; on Impossible the same 5,000 gold is not even a full chunk, so it does nothing to your standing. If you want a meaningful de-escalation signal, size the donation to at least the difficulty's chunk — 5,000 gold on a standard Medium match — and know that buying the full +100 cap is a much larger expense than the per-chunk arithmetic suggests, because the chunk grows with progression. The cost is your own gold, and the donation is **one-way** — you do not get it back unless the rival reciprocates — so the size of the donation should track the relationship, not the route: a one-chunk test for an unknown rival, a multi-chunk signal for a rival you genuinely want to keep as a partner, and no donation at all for a rival you have already decided to fight. If you have no gold to spare — the common case in the early game — the donation is simply unavailable and you are choosing between the mirror and the attack; in that situation the mirror is almost always the correct cheap first step, because the attack's troop cost exceeds the route's value when you cannot afford to spare the units.

## Failure modes and counter-moves

The most common failure is **retaliating into a superior economy**. If the rival has more cities, more workers, and more gold than you, every retaliation you make — mirror, port attack, or donation — is a cost they can absorb and you cannot. The mirror is cheap enough to be survivable, and the donation is survivable if you have the gold; the port attack is not, because it commits troops the rival can simply trade away and counter-strike from a position of strength. The counter is to **not retaliate on the axis where you are weak**: if you are economically outmatched, the mirror is your entire retaliation budget, and the rest of your resources should go to building your own port or your own independent trade routes so that the embargoed route becomes a smaller share of your total income over time. This is the slow version of the response: the embargo removes 600 gold per tick from your income today, but it does not stop your passive accrual — the base 100 gold per tick for a human keeps banking gold while the route is dark — so over the five minutes of a temporary embargo you lose at most a couple of route arrivals while your baseline economy keeps producing, a number that shrinks relative to your total economy as you keep growing.

The second failure is **retaliating hard when the embargo is temporary and you did not check the timer**. A temporary embargo expires after 3,000 ticks — 5 real minutes — regardless of what you do; if you spend 200k gold on a port attack in the first five minutes to "fix" a five-minute problem, you have burned more than the problem was worth. The counter is to **always confirm the embargo type before retaliating**: if it is temporary, the default correct move is to wait out the timer and let your passive economy absorb the loss; if it is permanent, the structural responses — a port attack or an own-port build — become rational because the loss is unbounded in time. The third failure is **confusing the embargo with a war declaration**. An embargo at v0.34.24 does not change the relation between two human players: the `embargoMalus` of -20 relation only applies to nations via `updateRelationsFromEmbargos`, and a human's embargo is a pure trade filter. So the rival's embargo is not, by itself, a reason to expect an attack from them next; it is a trade action, and your retaliation should be sized to the trade loss, not to a hypothetical war. If their messages or their troop movements show hostile intent beyond the embargo, that is a different decision — but the embargo alone is not the trigger for it. The fourth failure is **assuming the mirror does more damage than it does**. Because `canTrade` already required both directions clear, the mirror adds no new trade loss at the moment of action; it only locks the route closed after a temporary embargo expires or after the rival tries a one-sided clearance. If you expected the mirror to double your economic damage and it does not, you may misread the situation as "the mirror failed" when in fact it did exactly what it was designed to do. The fifth failure is **donating to a rival you have already decided to fight**, because the donation is one-way and the relation it buys does not survive a port attack; if your decision tree has already reached "adversary you want to punish," the donation is a sunk cost and the correct move is the port attack or the own-port build, not the donation.

## Mode and map adjustments

The correct retaliation shifts with the mode. In **No Alliances**, the mirror is the strongest signal you have, because there is no alliance system to de-escalate through; a mutual embargo in No Alliances is a durable state, and the rival cannot "call in" help to pressure you into clearing it, so the deterrence value of the mirror is at its highest. In **standard alliance modes**, the mirror is weaker, because a rival who is alliance-friendly to a third party may prefer to keep the option of a diplomatic exit open, and your mirror removes that exit — which can push the rival into an alliance with the third party against you, the exact opposite of the deterrence you were buying. The donation is the mode-agnostic de-escalation: it works identically in every mode, because it is a direct player-to-player action, but its value is highest in the modes where the rival is a potential ally you would lose permanently if you close the diplomatic door on them. The port attack is the mode-dependent move: in No Alliances it is a pure cost-and-benefit calculation with no third party to call on, so the threshold for a rational attack is lower; in alliance-heavy modes the same attack risks drawing in a third party who is allied with the rival, so the threshold is higher and the own-port build becomes the safer structural response.

The map matters just as much as the mode. On a **short map** — small radius, few trade nodes — the routes between any two players are short and low-value; a 300-tile route pays only about 52,500 gold per arrival, so an embargo removes a small stream and the retaliation should be the cheap mirror at most; attacking a port for a short-route dispute is almost never rational. On a **long map** — large radius, many trade nodes — the routes are long and high-value; a 1,500-tile route pays about 150,000 per arrival, so the embargo removes a large stream and the structural responses, a port attack or an own-port build, become rational. The own-port build is the **asymmetric** retaliation that no mode or map adjustment changes: at 125k for a first port it is always affordable for a player with more than five cities, and it converts a zero-sum trade dispute into a strictly positive move, because the new port opens routes to **every** other player and nation on the map, not just the rival who embargoed you. If you have the gold, the own-port build dominates every other retaliation on both the short and the long map, and the embargo was really a free port-construction incentive the rival handed you. The port-attack threshold also shifts with map size: on a long map the rival's second port (250k) is worth attacking because the dispatch node covers multiple long routes; on a short map the same 250k port only covers short, low-value routes, so the attack window narrows to the first port (125k) only.

## The decision tree and the numbers at a glance

The final tool is a single decision tree that turns the facts above into a concrete move. Ask, in order: **Is the embargo temporary or permanent?** If it is temporary, the default is to wait out the 3,000-tick timer (5 real minutes) and let your passive economy absorb the loss; only escalate if the route was worth more than about a third of a 1,000-tile route's per-arrival payout to you and you can genuinely afford the retaliation. **Is the rival a strategic partner you want to keep?** If yes, the donation is the correct first move — a 1-chunk test to confirm their intent, then a multi-chunk signal if the test is received — and the mirror and the port attack are off the table because they close the diplomatic door permanently. **Is the rival an adversary you want to punish?** If yes, the port attack is the correct move when their port is cheap — first or second, 125k to 250k — and lightly garrisoned, and the mirror is the fallback when the port is 1M-capped and heavily garrisoned. **Do you have the gold to build your own port?** If yes and you hold more than five cities, the own-port build dominates the other options on any map and in any mode.

| Retaliation | v0.34.24 cost | Direct effect | Pays for itself when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mirror embargo | 0 gold | Blocks route re-opening after a temporary embargo; signals deterrence | Rival's embargo is permanent and the route was low-value to both sides |
| Port attack | Troops + positioning time; rival loses 125k–1M rebuild | Removes the dispatch node for the route and every route sharing it | Rival's port is 1st/2nd (125k–250k) and lightly garrisoned; route worth a large share of your income |
| Gold donation | Difficulty chunk: 2,500 / 5,000 / 12,500 / 25,000 gold (Easy/Med/Hard/Imp) | +5 relation per chunk, capped at +100 per action; passes through the embargo | Rival is a strategic partner, or the embargo may be a bluff or mistake |
| Own-port build | 125k gold (1st port) | Opens routes to every other player and nation; ends the dispute asymmetrically | You have > 5 cities and ≥ 125k gold; works on any map and in any mode |

The single number that decides most of these choices is your **share of the embargoed route's gold-per-arrival**: if it is under a 300-tile route's payout (about 52,500 per arrival), the mirror is your entire retaliation and the structural options are not paying for themselves; if it is over a 1,000-tile route's payout (about 125,000 per arrival), the port attack or the own-port build becomes rational and the mirror alone is an under-response. Work that number out from the route length before you commit to any action, and the embargo stops being a moment of spite and becomes a small set of arithmetic choices.
