**Direct answer.** On Madagascar the server rolls a coin at match start: fifty
percent of the time the water-nuke modifier is on, and then every nuke converts
its crater's land to water permanently for the whole match. Default to the
on-branch. Keep a second crossing lane alive, cross by boat before the enemy's
nuke reaches your lane, and the instant a rival craters the Channel, turn on
them while their economy is exposed.

Madagascar is the v0.35 map that turns the water-nuke modifier into the central
question of the lobby. It carries a forced-modifier entry of
`isWaterNukes:50`, which means the server rolls a coin at match start and, fifty
percent of the time, turns the water-nuke rule on for every player in that game.
When it is on, a nuke does not just kill the territory it hits: it converts the
crater's land into water, and on this build that conversion is permanent for the
length of the match. Because the map is split into two large landmasses that
meet only across the Mozambique Channel, a single well-placed water nuke can
delete a crossing lane that everyone is relying on. This guide gives you a
repeatable framework for the four decisions a Madagascar lobby actually forces:
will the modifier roll, what the crater will do to a lane you need, which spawn
survives the geography, and the exact moment to cross by boat.

## What the 50% water-nuke roll means before the match

The number `50` in `isWaterNukes:50` is not a damage value, a radius, or a
cooldown; it is a percentage. The server-side match setup reads the map's forced
modifier entries, splits each on the colon, and applies the modifier when a
random draw lands under that percentage. Concretely, on Madagascar the water
nukes are active in roughly half of all games and inactive in the other half, so
you cannot assume the rule will be on or off. That single fact reframes the whole
lobby: you must treat every Madagascar game as a coin flip that resolves at match
start, and your strategy has to contain a "water nukes on" branch and a "water
nukes off" branch before the first tile is contested, because the branch you
cannot afford to be wrong about is the on branch. When the roll is on, every nuke
you launch becomes a territory-erasing tool and every nuke your rivals launch
becomes a lane-cutter, so the value of a nuke in your own hand rises and the
threat value of a nuke in someone else's hand rises even faster.

There are two mechanics around the roll that change what you should expect in
the modifier list. First, the forced modifier is handled separately from the
normal random modifier pool: it is excluded from that pool so it does not get
rolled twice, and it counts against the same three-modifier cap that limits
other modifiers. That means a water-nuke roll can displace a modifier you might
otherwise expect to see, such as a starting-gold boost or a hard-nations
modifier, so the rest of your modifier set may be thinner than a normal lobby.
Second, there is a hard exclusion relationship: `isWaterNukes` and
`isNukesDisabled` are mutually exclusive, so if the water-nuke roll lands the
random pool can no longer produce a nuke-disabled lobby. In practice this means
you will never see a Madagascar game where water nukes are forced and nuclear
weapons are simultaneously banned; the two states cancel each other out at the
point of the roll.

Before you even choose a spawn, glance at the lobby's active-modifier list the
instant it appears and read it as the coin's result. If `isWaterNukes` is
present, the map enters its dangerous mode and every decision in the rest of this
guide runs on the on-branch. If it is absent, the map plays like an ordinary Nuke
Wars lobby and the water mechanic never triggers, so the Channel is fixed terrain
and the crossing is a normal boat problem. The entire rest of this guide is
organized around that binary, because it is the binary that the server itself
decides for you at match start, and the fastest way to lose on Madagascar is to
play the off-branch in a lobby that rolled on and discover it only after the
first crater has already landed.

## What a water nuke actually does to the map

When a water nuke detonates, the engine builds the crater from a set of radius
samples spread around the impact point, one sample for each of sixteen angular
positions, each drawn between the nuke's inner and outer squared radii and then
smoothed against its neighbours so the boundary undulates gently instead of
spiking. The design note in the source is explicit about why: the smoothing
exists so the crater edge does not scatter isolated land pixels that players
would have to boat to one by one just to reclaim a single tile. Every land tile
inside the resulting boundary is then converted to water, while impassable
terrain is left alone. On a normal nuke the crater destroys structures and kills
the tile contents; on a water nuke the crater additionally rewrites the map's
passability so that what was land is now sea, and that is the part that changes
the meta rather than the damage.

There are exactly three nuke types that can produce a water crater, and their
magnitudes define the crater's outer radius. The MIRV warhead makes the
tightest crater at an outer radius of 18 tiles, the atom bomb widens it to an
outer radius of 30 tiles with the same 12-tile core, and the hydrogen bomb makes
a crater so large that its inner radius is already 80 tiles and its outer reaches
100 tiles. The cost curve is steeper than the damage curve: the atom bomb costs
750,000 gold, the hydrogen bomb 5,000,000, while a warship caps at one million
and a SAM launcher at three million. That means the atom bomb is a cheaper
crater than the defenses it can erase, which is why players spend it to cut a
lane even when it is not strong enough to finish the target it is aimed at, and
the hydrogen bomb is an order of magnitude more expensive, so it is bought to
rewrite the map rather than to trade a lane. The strategic weight is therefore
not in the damage numbers but in the permanence and the size range: an atom bomb
buys a local roadblock while a hydrogen bomb buys a new lake, and the gap
between them is large enough that the same lane can be either blocked or fully
severed depending on the nuke spent. The MIRV warhead, despite being the
smallest crater, is the most expensive to deliver because it rides a MIRV silo
that costs 25,000,000 gold plus 15,000,000 for every MIRV already launched, so a
player reaching for a MIRV is spending the most gold for the least water.

Because this build converts land to water with no scheduled revert, a crater that
an atom bomb carves into a narrow coastal lane is gone for the entire match.
The lane does not heal, does not flood back, and cannot be reclaimed without
re-capturing the surrounding land by normal military means and rebuilding the
crossing from scratch. That permanence is what converts a normal Nuke Wars
lobby, where nukes trade territory back and forth and the map is mostly stable,
into a map where a single nuke can reshape the viable routes for every player.
The practical read is that a water nuke is less a weapon and more a map editor:
the player who detonates it is redrawing the Channel, and everyone else has to
re-plan around whatever shape it drew.

## The two-landmass geography and the Channel

Madagascar's land is not one contiguous blob. It is two large landmasses that
face each other across a stretch of open water, with the Mozambique Channel
sitting between them, and the map's named geography is built around that split.
The manifest lists thirty-four named nations plus twenty-nine additional
nations, for sixty-three nations in total, and those nations are distributed
across the two sides and the islands in between. The theme is set to Africa and
the layout is deliberately wide so that a lobby of many nations has room on both
banks. This geography drives the entire meta and it is the reason the water
nuke is so powerful on this map rather than on a normal one: on a single-bank
map a water nuke erodes a flank, but on a two-bank map it can delete the only
way between the two halves of your plan.

The core mechanic that follows is that any nation on one side that wants to
project onto the other side must cross the Channel by boat, because the water in
between is impassable on foot. Boats are the only bridge between the landmasses,
which means every offensive plan across the map depends on a reachable coast, a
functioning fleet, and a safe crossing window, and any one of those three failing
stops the offense. A water nuke that lands on the near bank can erode the very
coastline you were using as a launch point; a water nuke that lands on the far
bank can destroy the beachhead you were building on the other side; and a water
nuke that lands in the middle of a narrow channel can widen the gap so much that
your fleet has to take a longer, more exposed route. The map rewards players who
think in terms of the Channel as a movable obstacle, not as fixed terrain, and it
punishes players who memorize one crossing route and commit to it.

If the water nukes roll on, treat the Channel width as a variable that your
enemies can increase on command, and build your plan so it still works when the
Channel is wider than you saw it at match start. If the roll is off, treat it as
fixed terrain and plan the crossing like any other [coastal boat
defense](/guides/coastal-boat-defense/) problem. Either way, the two-landmass
shape means there is no central high ground to rally on; the high ground is
whichever bank still has a usable crossing at any given moment, and that can
change the instant a crater lands. The single most important habit on this map is
to continuously re-evaluate which crossing lane is still open, because the
answer to "where do I cross" can be invalidated by one nuke at any point in the
match.

## Choosing your spawn across 63 nations

With sixty-three nations on a wide two-bank map, spawn quality is decided by
three properties working together: which bank you are on, how close you are to a
usable crossing point, and how many strong neighbours you have on your own side.
A bank-side spawn with open coast and a short hop to the other landmass is the
most flexible position, because you can either defend your home side or launch a
crossing without having to reposition first. A spawn buried against three or
four strong nations on the same bank is the weakest, because a water-nuke crater
anywhere in your cluster can isolate you while you have no crossing to fall back
on, and the isolation is permanent for the match. The middle-island spawns sit
closest to the action and to the danger at the same time: a water nuke that cuts
the channel near you can strand the island itself, turning your home territory
into a peninsula or an island with no easy resupply line.

Because the server assigns spawns and you often cannot control the exact tile,
your real choice is whether to accept the assigned spawn or to push early toward
a better one, and the value of that early push depends entirely on the coin. On
a forced water-nuke lobby, the value of an early push toward open coast rises
sharply, because a spawn that is comfortable now can become a trap the first
time an enemy detonates across the channel. Use the lobby modifier list as your
tie breaker. If `isWaterNukes` is on, weight "distance to a second crossing
option" above raw starting population, because you want an exit from any bank
and a second option is what keeps you from being stranded. If it is off, weight
starting position and neighbour strength the same way any [spawn
choice](/guides/choose-your-spawn/) would, because the Channel is stable and the
usual spawn heuristics apply.

The general rule that covers most cases is that on Madagascar you should never
be happy with a spawn that has only one way out of the water, because that
single lane is exactly what a rival's water nuke can erase. A spawn with two
viable crossing options, even if it starts with slightly less population, is
worth more on a water-nuke lobby than a richer spawn with a single lane, because
the second option is insurance against the one event this map is built to
produce. That insurance is not free, which is why the next section makes the
crossing decision concrete.

## Decision framework: boat timing and the nuke window

The core decision on Madagascar is when to cross by boat relative to the water
nukes, and the way to make that decision is to recognize that two independent
clocks are running against each other. One is your economic clock: the time it
takes you to raise enough gold to field a fleet that can hold the crossing, which
on a normal start means pushing enough population to afford warships and enough
troops to land behind them. The other is the enemy nuke clock: the moment a
rival has enough gold and a nuke to target your lane, measured from the last
time you saw them buying. Because water nukes are permanent on this build, the
correct posture is to cross before the enemy's nuke clock reaches your lane, not
after, because after that moment the lane may no longer exist and the crossing
you were planning may have become a two-hundred-tile detour with an undefended
beachhead window at the other end.

A concrete decision rule follows. If the water-nuke modifier is on, commit your
crossing as soon as your fleet can defend the near bank and the far bank at the
same time, because every extra tick you spend building on one bank is a tick an
enemy can use to crater the only lane connecting them. If the modifier is off,
you can afford to build up on one bank and cross later, because the Channel is
fixed terrain and cannot be widened from under you, so the economic clock
dominates and you want the larger force across. The framework also needs an
explicit abort condition, which is the part most players skip. If an enemy nuke
detonates on your near bank and the crater severs your launch coastline, you
should not keep pushing the fleet across the now-longer route with the same
force; you should cancel the crossing, pull the fleet back to the still-intact
side, and either rebuild the coast by recapturing land or find a second crossing
point.

The discipline that community players describe as "target them in priority"
applies here in reverse: the player cratering your lane is your number-one
target, because while they are redrawing the Channel they are the player least
able to contest your next crossing. Treating the nuke window as a hard timeline,
the way experienced players treat the impossible-difficulty nuke window, is what
separates a successful Madagascar lobby from one where you spend the whole game
boat-hopping a lane that keeps getting erased. The framework's final output is a
number, not a feeling: how many waves you are willing to commit before you accept
that the lane may be cut, and that number should be the largest fleet that still
leaves you able to contest the rebuilding of your own coastline.

## Scenario A: crossing before the crater

Assume a mid-lobby FFA on Madagascar with the water-nuke modifier rolled on, a
standard economy start, and you holding a bank-side spawn with open coast about
four tiles from the narrowest part of the Channel. You are at roughly 30,000
troops and have fielded a small warship screen plus two boat waves, while the
strongest nation on the far bank sits near 70,000 and has one nuke in the
pipeline that costs 750,000 gold and is currently two minutes from ready. The
crossing window is those two minutes before the nuke is ready, and the whole
scenario is a race between your boat waves and their single prepared nuke. The
plan is to land the first boat wave on the far bank now, before the nuke is
ready, to establish a beachhead and a SAM so the far bank is defended at the
moment your fleet arrives.

If you wait, the nuke lands first and the crater can erase the near-bank
coastline that your remaining waves depend on, forcing you to take a longer
route with an undefended beachhead window at the far end, which is the worst
combination of the two. The numbers that make the on-time version work are
specific. The first wave must be large enough that a single wave does not get
wiped by the beachhead defense, because a wiped first wave buys the far-bank
nation a free SAM upgrade and pushes your real crossing later into a smaller
window. And the warship must be positioned to intercept a retaliating fleet from
the far-bank nation, because the moment your beachhead is visible they will send
boats to contest it. The assumption to test is whether the far-bank nation will
spend its nuke on your lane or on the map center. If it is a rational Nuke Wars
player with its own offense, it may crater a different lane and leave yours open,
in which case you can keep crossing on schedule and the window is generous. If
it targets your lane, the crater lands on the near bank, you lose the launch
coastline, and the scenario flips into Scenario B. The decision you are making is
not "should I cross" but "how many waves do I commit before I accept that the
lane may be cut," and the answer is the maximum number of waves that still leaves
enough force on the near bank to contest the rebuilding of the coastline.

## Scenario B: holding the near bank after a crater

Now assume the nuke landed on your near bank and the crater converted a strip of
coast to water, severing the launch point your fleet was using. You still hold
the majority of the near bank, the far-bank beachhead from Scenario A is thin and
under pressure, and the enemy that cratered you has its nuke spent. The posture
here is defensive on the near bank and opportunistic on the far bank, and the two
halves have to be managed separately because they are now connected only by the
longer route. Because the crater is permanent on this build, you cannot wait for
the coast to come back; you must either recapture the surrounding land by normal
military means and rebuild the crossing from the newly claimed tiles, or abandon
that coastline and move your crossing to a second point further along the bank.

The decision hinges on the ratio of your remaining near-bank troops to the
enemy's defended coastal line. If you can field a force at least as large as the
enemy's coastal defense, commit to recapturing the cratered strip, because
holding the crater line lets you deny the enemy the same lane and keeps your
second crossing option alive, which is the real prize. If you cannot field that
force, pull back behind the second crossing, keep the far-bank beachhead
supplied by the longer route, and use the fact that the cratering enemy has just
spent 750,000 gold to your advantage. In the minutes after a nuke the cratering
player is the most vulnerable to a counterattack, because their economy is
behind and their nuke is gone, which is the exact inverse of the state they had
before the detonation. The counterplay is to attack the cratering player's coast
while they are rebuilding, forcing them to defend instead of launching their next
crossing, and that counterattack is the move that decides whether Scenario B
becomes a recovery or a collapse.

The general principle that covers both branches is that the player who lands the
crater is momentarily the weakest player on the map, not the strongest, because
a water nuke spends the most expensive single asset in the game to buy a
one-time map change. Exploiting that moment is the core skill on this map, and
the [nuclear target-cutting](/guides/nuke-cut-territory-annexation/) meta is what
makes the crater a lane-cutter in the first place, so the two guides read as one
strategy: cut the lane, then exploit the moment the cutter is exposed.

## Failure modes and counterplay

The most common failure on Madagascar is treating the water-nuke roll as a
detail instead of the central branch point. A player who builds the whole
economy on one bank and then discovers `isWaterNukes` is on, with a nuke in the
enemy pipeline, has a one-lane economy and no second crossing option, which is
exactly the position that a crater exploits, because there is nowhere for the
player to go when the single lane is erased. The counter is to always keep a
second crossing option alive on a forced water-nuke lobby, even at an economic
cost, because the second option is what lets you reroute after a crater and is
the difference between a setback and a loss.

A second failure is over-committing the crossing: sending the entire fleet across
and leaving the near bank undefended, so that when the crater lands the enemy has
an undefended coast they can take and your fleet is stranded on the far bank with
no supply line. The counter is the abort condition described earlier: keep enough
force on the near bank to contest any recapture of the cratered coast, because a
stranded fleet is a fleet that has to cross back through the same water it came
from, and on a water-nuke map that return journey is as dangerous as the original
one. A third failure is misreading the crater size. A MIRV warhead's crater is a
local roadblock, but an atom bomb's crater is a new lake that can split a bank in
two; a player who assumes every nuke produces a tight crater will plan around a
lane that an atom bomb has actually deleted, and that misread is invisible until
the detonation. The counter is to size your crossing route against the largest
nuke the enemy can afford, not the smallest, and to treat any lane that an atom
bomb or hydrogen bomb can reach as a lane that will be cut.

Finally, the failure of ignoring the permanent nature of the crater. On a build
where the land reverts after a few minutes, a crater is a temporary obstacle you
can wait out; on this build it is a permanent one, so any plan that says "wait for
the coast to come back" is a plan that will not execute, and the player who
waits spends the whole match on a detour while the enemy who planned for
permanence holds the open lane. Every failure here traces back to one of the three
verified facts: the 50% roll, the permanent conversion, and the two-landmass
Channel, and the discipline that prevents all of them is the same habit: re-plan
around the crater, never wait for it.

## Mode and map adjustments

The framework changes with the mode and the lobby composition, so it is worth
making the adjustments explicit rather than assuming the Nuke Wars version is the
only version. In Nuke Wars the crater is the central asset trade, and the whole
guide applies as written, because the mode is built around the exact event the
map is built to produce. In FFA the same principles hold, but the cratering
player is less likely to be a coordinated rival and more likely to be any of
several players, so you should treat "who will crater my lane" as a probability
spread across the lobby rather than a single target, and keep the second crossing
option even more alive because any player can spend a nuke on your lane. In team
modes, your team should agree which bank you hold and which bank you cross to
before the match, and the water-nuke roll should be called out in team chat the
instant it appears so the whole team switches from a fixed-Channel plan to a
movable-Channel plan together, because a team that does not know the roll landed
plays the off-branch in an on-lobby.

In ranked or laddered play on this map, the 50% roll means your match can swing on
luck at the coin, so the skill signal is not whether the roll lands but whether
your team has the abort and reroute discipline to handle both outcomes, and a
player who only wins when the roll is off is not playing the map. The map's width
also means lobby size matters: a fuller lobby fills both banks and the islands,
increasing the chance that a cratered lane has an alternate, so in a sparse lobby
the Channel is narrower and more dangerous because there are fewer crossings to
fall back on. Treat the [lobby size and map interaction](/guides/lobby-size-map-driven/)
as a second dial you control: a larger lobby on Madagascar is generally safer for
the player defending a bank because there are more crossing points, while a
smaller lobby concentrates the crossing onto fewer lanes and raises the value of
the water nuke. The net adjustment is that on Madagascar the map is doing half the
work of the Nuke Wars meta for you; your job is to make sure you are on the right
side of the coin flip, on the right bank of the Channel, and crossing on the right
clock, and to keep a second option alive so the crater cannot turn your plan into
a detour.
