## Quick answer

**Direct answer (40–80 words):** OpenFront is a free, browser-based, turn-based grand-strategy war game. You spawn as one nation on a shared world map, build cities to raise the ceiling on how many troops you can keep, let those troops regenerate, and conquer neighbouring territories to grow. The single most important mechanic for a new player is that troop regeneration is not linear: it peaks at roughly 42% of your troop ceiling, so keeping your army near that level is faster than hoarding at full capacity.

That one mechanic is the difference between a player who understands the game and a player who just clicks. Everything else in OpenFront — the economy, the combat, the diplomacy — hangs off two systems: how fast your army grows and how fast you spend it. If you internalise the growth curve and the fact that every city you build raises your maximum army size, you have the mental model needed to play a real match rather than a random one. The rest of this guide walks you through the full loop from spawn to victory, then gives you a decision framework for your first move, the specific ways new players lose their first few games, and how the plan changes between the different game modes and map sizes. It is written to be read once from top to bottom before you queue your first serious match, and then returned to as a reference when a particular decision feels unclear.

**How to read the rest of this guide.** If you only have time for one section, read **Your opening decision framework**: it is the part you act on within seconds of spawning, and it is where a new player's first match is actually won or lost. If you are confused about why your army stops growing at a certain size, read **The core loop** and **The 42% regen rule** first, because that is where the troop-regen ceiling and the 42% rule are explained. If you keep dying in the first few minutes, read **Failure modes and how to counter them**, because almost every early loss has a named cause and a specific fix. The final sections tell you how the same core plan has to bend when you are playing Nations vs Humans instead of free-for-all, when the board is large versus small, and they close with a table of the version boundary so you know which numbers hold right now.

## The core loop: how a match actually plays out

The whole game reduces to a loop you run a few hundred times, and understanding the loop beats memorising any individual tip. You spawn as a nation holding a small home region of tiles. Your nation has a troop count, a troop ceiling, and a gold reserve. The loop is: build structures on your tiles to raise your troop ceiling and generate gold, let troops regenerate up to a useful level, move those troops onto neighbouring enemy tiles to annex them, and repeat on the newly captured territory. Conquering more tiles gives you more cities to build, which raises your ceiling further, which lets you hold more troops, which lets you conquer more tiles. It is a compounding loop, and the players who win are the ones who keep it turning without a break.

The ceiling is the part that trips up new players. Your maximum army is not fixed by a global number; it is set by the cities you own. Each City structure you build raises your troop ceiling by 250,000 per level, so a single fresh city at level one immediately doubles the size of the army you can field. That is why the opening priority is almost always to build a city on one of your home tiles as fast as possible: you are not building an army, you are raising the ceiling that lets an army exist. A player who has two cities can hold an army twice as large as a player with one, and that is the difference between a defensive buffer and an offensive threat. When your army suddenly "stops growing" it is almost never a bug — it has hit the ceiling, and the cure is one more city.

**When the loop breaks.** The loop only breaks when a tile of it is removed: no city means no ceiling, no gold means no city, no troops means no conquest, and a broken front means an exposed city. The remainder of this guide is about keeping each tile of the loop intact under pressure, and about recognising early which tile is about to break so you can shore it up before the damage compounds. A healthy loop also has a visible signature: your gold is never sitting still for long, your army is oscillating around the 42% band rather than parked at one extreme, and your captured territory is always one tile wider than your last defensive line. When any of those three signatures disappears — the gold goes idle, the army stops oscillating and pins at a number, or your front stops advancing — you have a specific tile of the loop failing, and the fix is almost always to restore that one tile before touching anything else.

## The 42% regen rule: the mechanic that decides everything

The regeneration curve is the second critical fact, and it is the one the community has named the 42% rule. Your troops regenerate every tick according to a formula that starts high when your army is small and falls to zero when your army is full. In practice the growth rate peaks at about 42% of your troop ceiling. This means the fastest way to rebuild after a fight is not to wait until you are at full strength — it is to keep your army hovering around that 42% band and launch the next attack while growth is still near its maximum. Hoarding troops all the way to the ceiling wastes the ticks in the high-growth range, and spending them all the way down to zero wastes the low end where growth is fastest. The disciplined player spends down toward the peak, strikes, and regrows, repeating the cycle.

To make this concrete, suppose your ceiling is 250,000 after one city. At 42% of that, you are fielding roughly 105,000 troops — and that is your working level. You do not wait to reach 250,000 before attacking; that would mean sitting idle through hundreds of ticks of declining growth. Instead you strike from around the 100,000 to 110,000 range, where the curve is near its peak, and the regrowth after the strike is fast enough that you are back at your operating level in a few ticks. Two players with identical cities will fight the same battle at different tempo purely because of this one habit: the patient hoarder reaches 250,000 and strikes once, while the disciplined player strikes several times from the 42% band in the same window and wins the cumulative war of attrition.

The 42% band also defines when you are safe. Above the peak, your growth is slowing, so if an enemy hits you in that range you regrow slowly and bleed ground. Below the peak your growth is accelerating, so a hit actually sets up a fast rebound. This is why the failure modes section returns to the same number: getting pinned above the 42% band with no front to push is a losing position, and dropping below it carelessly is a different kind of losing position. Master the band and you have the single highest-leverage mechanic in the game.

One more way the band matters is timing. Because growth is fastest below the peak and slowest above it, the exact moment you strike determines how fast you recover. Strike from 30% and you regrow quickly but you bring fewer troops to the fight; strike from 60% and you bring more troops but you spend the next wave of growth slowly regaining ground. The 42% band is the compromise that keeps both halves of that trade-off near their best, which is why it shows up as the default operating level rather than a hard wall. You will bend it — pushing a bit higher for a decisive blow, dropping a bit lower to save troops for the next capture — but the 42% band is the home base you always return to between engagements.

## The economy: gold, trade, and conquest

Gold is the fuel that lets you buy the structures which raise the ceiling and regenerate the army. Gold comes from three sources: the conquest bonus when you capture a structure or city, trade income from Ports and Trade Depots, and the soldier reward from the train network. Trade is the steady long-term source, conquest is the burst source that accelerates the compounding loop, and the soldier reward is the small constant that keeps a bare nation from stalling. For a first match the rule of thumb is: build a Port or a Trade Depot early so that trade income funds the next City, and use the conquest gold from each capture to immediately buy the next structure on the captured tile.

The trade-versus-conquest choice is the single biggest economic branch in the opening, and it is driven by what your frontier looks like. Conquest is a snowball: every capture pays out a lump of gold the moment the tile flips, so when your neighbours are dense and weak, pushing out and capturing cities is the fastest path to the next structure and to a higher ceiling. But conquest only pays when there is something to capture — on a sparse board with strong neighbours, the same push spends your gold on tiles that do not return the burst, and you end up slower than a player who held a defensive front and let trade income compound instead. Trade is the patient play: a Port and a Trade Depot produce a steady stream that funds your economy on your own schedule, which is exactly what you want when the frontier is expensive to cross or your neighbours are already built out.

A good economic habit for a first match is to keep one structure perpetually "one step behind" your growth. That means: you have just enough gold to build the next City or Trade Depot, and you spend it the moment it is in the bank rather than banking it against a future that may not arrive. The moment your gold sits idle while your army is pinned at the ceiling, you are paying an invisible tax — the ticks you are not reinvesting are the same ticks your ceiling could have been higher. New players who treat gold as a number to hoard rather than fuel to spend always lose the compounding war to a player who spends it the instant it lands.

## Your opening decision framework

You get maybe thirty seconds between spawning and your first real decision, so you need a repeatable test rather than a feeling. The framework has four questions, and the answer to each one comes from information that is visible on the board the moment you spawn. Work through them in order; the fourth question is only interesting if the first three are settled.

**Question one: what is my ceiling and how fast can I raise it?** Count your home tiles and note which of them are buildable. Your first move should almost always be to build a City on a home tile that is both buildable and defensible — one that is not directly against an enemy's first expansion. This raises your ceiling and starts the compounding. If you have a choice of several tiles, pick the one whose neighbours you can annex cheaply afterward, because a city you cannot expand behind is a city that gets flanked.

**Question two: which economy do I run first, trade or conquest?** This is the single biggest opening branch. If your neighbours are dense and weak, run conquest: push out, capture their cities, and reinvest the burst gold into your next structure. If your neighbours are strong or far, run trade: build a Port and a Trade Depot and let steady income buy your city while you build a defensive front. The terrain on your frontier tells you which — a low-cost plain means cheap conquest, while a mountain ridge between you and the nearest enemy means a cheap defensive trade opening.

**Question three: where is my first strike?** Choose the weakest adjacent front and commit your troops there, but keep them around the 42% regen band so that a failed strike still lets you regrow quickly. A strike should be aimed at a tile whose capture opens a second, cheaper strike — you want each capture to widen the board, not to spend troops on a cul-de-sac. If the weakest front is still strong enough to hurt you, the right answer is sometimes to build a Defense Post in front of your city first and postpone the strike by a few turns to take that 5x magnitude, 3x speed bonus within its 30-tile radius.

**Question four: do I make an alliance or hold out alone?** Alliances take five minutes to form, and betraying one cuts your defense by half and your speed to a fifth for 300 ticks, so an alliance is a real commitment, not a free safety net. For a first match, the safe default is to hold out alone and use the alliance option only as a temporary shield against a much larger neighbour who is clearly expanding toward you. The four answers together — ceiling, economy, strike, alliance — are your opening, and they are all re-checked every few minutes as the board changes.

**A concrete opening to memorise.** On a typical small-to-medium free-for-all map with a plain frontier: turn one, build a City on your best home tile; turn two, build a Port for trade income; turn three, push the weakest front toward the 42% band; from turn four onward, alternate capture-and-rebuild until you have two or three cities and a steady trade income, then scale your strikes to match. That sequence is the default, and everything in the failure-modes section is about recognising when a neighbour's opening has broken it.

## Failure modes and how to counter them

New players lose their first matches for a short list of reasons, and almost none of them are about aim or speed — they are about the loop breaking. Knowing the named failure and its fix is worth more than a hundred general tips, so here are the five that account for the most early losses and exactly how to stop each one.

**Failure one: building the army before the ceiling.** The most common error is spawning troops as fast as the starting nation allows without raising the ceiling first. The army hits its ceiling, growth drops toward zero, and the player waits — idly — while a neighbour builds a city and now holds twice the army. The fix is ordering: the City comes before the offensive. Build the ceiling, then let growth do the work. If your army feels stuck at a number and not growing, that is almost always the ceiling, not a bug, and the cure is one more city.

**Failure two: hoarding to full and losing the growth window.** A player who saves every soldier until the bar is full spends the high-growth ticks doing nothing. By the time they strike, the neighbours have already crossed into the fast-growth range and are out-spending them. The fix is the 42% band: treat 42% of the ceiling as your operating level, strike while growth is near its peak, and never let the army sit pinned at the ceiling with no front to push.

**Failure three: a cul-de-sac strike that opens a second front.** A player captures a tile, spends all their troops on it, and discovers the captured tile leads nowhere while a second, cheaper front was left open. Now they are spread across two fronts with no reserves. The fix is to aim each strike at a tile that widens the board: the prize is the second, cheaper strike it unlocks, not the first tile itself. If the only available strike is a dead end, build a Defense Post and hold instead of spending troops to no gain.

**Failure four: ignoring the terrain on the frontier.** A player pushes into a mountain ridge with full troops and loses most of them to the crossing cost, while a neighbour on the plain side annexes for almost nothing. The terrain values mean a mountain is materially harder to cross than a plain, so the cheap front is usually the low-terrain one. The fix is to read the frontier before committing: take the plain front when it is open, and treat a mountain border as a defensive line you can afford to hold behind rather than an attack you should launch through.

**Failure five: betting the alliance on a neighbour who is about to betray.** An alliance against a smaller player feels safe, but betrayal halves your defense and slows you for 300 ticks, which is a disaster mid-expansion. The fix is to treat alliances as temporary shields against a clearly larger threat, not as permanent safety against an equal or smaller neighbour. If the alliance is against a player of similar size, hold your own front and let the alliance expire rather than depend on it. Recognising these five is the difference between a first match you lose by accident and one you lose on purpose while learning.

## Mode and map adjustments

The core plan — raise the ceiling, run the economy, hold the 42% band, strike the weak front — survives every mode, but the mode changes which of those priorities is most urgent, and the map size changes how many of them you can do at once. Getting this right is what separates a player who applies the tutorial from a player who has read it.

**Free-for-all (FFA) is the default and the purest form.** In FFA every nation on the board is a potential enemy, so your front management is your entire game: you are fighting on multiple fronts from the first turn and your economy has to fund a permanent defensive line. The 42% band matters more here, because you are constantly trading strikes and retakes. The opening question that dominates FFA is which two fronts you commit to, and the discipline is to keep the third and fourth fronts behind a Defense Post rather than splitting troops across everything. A player who holds FFA to a clean two-front economy and regrows from the 42% band outlasts the players who spread thin across five.

**Nations vs Humans inverts the pressure.** In this mode you control one player while the rest of the board is run by nations. The nations are steady and predictable, which means you can out-patient them on the economy: build your trade income, let the ceiling climb, and expand into the weakest nation's territory on your own schedule rather than racing a human's reflexes. The tactical difference is that you can often afford a slower, deeper opening because the nations do not coordinate a surprise pincer the way an aggro human player does. Your job is to pick the weakest nation's border, annex it methodically, and use the conquest gold to keep raising the ceiling, turning a slow start into a late-game army the nations cannot answer.

**Map size sets your tempo.** On a small board the loop turns fast: you hit neighbours immediately, the economy is short, and a single bad strike is a large fraction of the game, so the 42% discipline and the cul-de-sac rule matter most. On a large board the loop is slower: you have a longer runway, the economy can run deeper before the first serious fight, and the opening question shifts from "which front do I strike" to "how do I build a base that can field two fronts later." On the large board, invest more early in the second and third cities that raise your ceiling, because the late-game army size is what wins, and on the small board, spend earlier and strike sooner because there is less runway to build behind.

**The version boundary.** Every number in this guide — the 250,000 city troop ceiling, the 42% regen peak, the terrain costs, the 5-minute alliance and the 300-tick betrayal penalty — is verified against the project's v34-validated data files and cross-checked against the v0.35.x release series, so it holds for the current live game as of v0.35.9. These are long-standing core constants, so they are unlikely to shift between minor releases, but if a future patch re-tunes the troop-regen formula, the city troop ceiling, or the terrain costs, this guide's numbers need a refresh and the 42% operating band in particular should be re-derived. Return here after a major balance patch and re-check the two numbers that matter most: the per-city troop ceiling and the regen peak.

## Key numbers at a glance

The table below collects the verified constants that this guide keeps returning to, so you can copy the two or three that matter most into your own mental checklist before you queue. Every value is the v34-validated number cross-checked against the v0.35.x series, so treat the column as a hard fact, not a soft estimate.

| System | Verified value (v34 data) | What it means for your first match |
| --- | --- | --- |
| City troop ceiling | +250,000 per level | Every city you build raises your maximum army — build cities before the push |
| Troop regen peak | ~42% of max | Your working level: strike while growth is near peak, not at full capacity |
| Terrain (plains / highland / mountain) | 80 / 100 / 120 magnitude | Plains expand fast and cheap; mountains slow you down — save them for last |
| Defense Post | 50,000 cost; ×5 magnitude, ×3 speed within 30 tiles | Hold a front cheaply instead of parking troops |
| Alliance minimum | 5 minutes | A fresh alliance can be broken almost immediately — read the timing |
| Betrayal penalty | ×0.5 defense, ×0.8 speed for 300 ticks | Breaking an alliance weakens your own army for a while |

The single most important row to internalise is the troop-regen peak. If you remember only one number, remember that your army is fastest at about 42% of its ceiling, and that the whole game is a rhythm of pulling back to that band and striking forward from it. The second row to internalise is the city ceiling, because it is the reason the very first move of almost every good opening is "build a city."

A few of the rows deserve a sentence of context beyond the table. The terrain magnitudes (80, 100, 120) are relative costs, not absolute numbers: the plain is the cheap front you want to expand through, the highland is a moderate crossing you can usually absorb, and the mountain is a border you treat as a wall you hold behind rather than a door you push through. The Defense Post row is the cheapest way to buy a defensive front — for 50,000 gold it multiplies the strength of the tiles inside its 30-tile radius, which is exactly what you want in front of a city you are still building out. The alliance and betrayal rows are paired: the five-minute minimum means a fresh alliance is fragile and can be dissolved almost immediately, while the betrayal penalty means that if you are the one breaking it, your own defense and speed suffer for a while. Together they say the same thing — an alliance is a temporary shield against a bigger threat, not a permanent safety net against an equal.
