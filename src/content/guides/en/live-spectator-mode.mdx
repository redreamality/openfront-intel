## The Direct Answer: Watch Without Taking a Seat

Open any lobby you can reach by code or a shared game link, and choose **Spectate** instead of taking a player seat. When you enter that way, the game does not spawn you, does not assign you a team, and does not count you against the lobby's player cap. You watch the live match in real time, connected to the same lobby the players are in, from a spectator state that is separate from any player seat.

This is the whole point of the feature, and it answers a long-standing community request: before spectator mode existed, the only way to "watch" a friend or a top player was to join the game as a player, which changed the match's population, could push the game into a different mode or a different lobby pool, and made the viewer effectively a participant rather than an observer. Spectator mode removes that coupling. You can sit in a six-person FFA lobby and watch all six real players fight, or watch a team game where every seat is already taken, without adding a seventh actor to the simulation. The match you watch is the match the players actually play, not a lobby that quietly changed because you showed up.

Use spectating when your goal is to learn from a live game rather than to control one. It is the natural bridge between reading a guide and playing the next match: you can watch how a strong player opens, how a nation handles a border dispute, how a trade route is defended, or how a clan coordinates a push, all while the game is still in progress. It is also the right choice when you want to monitor a specific player's game without joining it, when you are streaming and want to watch a match without affecting it, or when a lobby is full and you still want to see what is happening.

The decision is simple: if you want to act in the match, take a seat. If you want to observe the match without changing it, choose Spectate. Everything below explains exactly how to do that, what the feature does and does not let you do, and when the right answer is to watch live versus to review the replay afterward.

A quick way to feel the difference is to picture a lobby that already has all of its seats filled. Before this feature, showing up to that lobby as a viewer would have made you a player and turned the match into a bigger game. Now you can enter the same full lobby as a spectator, watch every real player, and leave the population exactly as it was. The match you watch is the match the players actually play, and your presence changes nothing about it. That single shift, watching without taking a seat, is what the rest of the guide unpacks into a usable decision.

## What Changed and When: The Version Boundary

Spectator mode is not a gradual feature; it arrived as one concrete change and was then hardened in a later release. The boundary matters because the exact behaviour you can rely on depends on which build you are running, and the v33, v34, and v35 series all have a stable answer here.

The core ability shipped in **v0.33.7**, released on 2026-08-21. That release is the one that added the ability to join any game to watch without playing, tracked as the feature request that had been open in the community for months. In v0.33.7, the defining facts were already present: entering a lobby in spectator mode skips spawning and team assignment, a spectator takes no player slot, and a finished match still opens as a replay. If you are running v0.33.7 or any later stable build, the basic "watch without taking a seat" flow is available.

The next meaningful change came in **v0.34.0**, released on 2026-09-14. That release hardened the Play/Spectate seat toggle and added the clan-overflow behaviour. Specifically, v0.34.0 made the server explicitly refuse to seat a spectator (turn spectate off) once the match has started or ended, because the player list is frozen at start; it added allowlist, trust-gate, and lobby-pool guards on the seat path so a spectator can only become a player where the lobby actually allows it; and it introduced the rule that in team mode with clan tags enabled, players who overflow the allowed per-clan count are converted to spectators instead of being kicked. If you are running v0.34.0 or later, those refusal rules and the clan-overflow conversion are in effect.

From v0.34.0 through the current stable **v0.35.4**, released on 2026-10-24, the spectator behaviour has not changed: the v0.35.0 through v0.35.4 release notes do not mention spectating, and the spectator code is present and unchanged in the v0.35.4 build. In practice, that means the behaviour described in this guide holds across every stable build from v0.33.7 forward. The practical guidance is: on any current build you get the full feature, and the only thing that changes between a v0.33.7-era build and a v0.34.0-or-newer build is how firmly the server enforces that a spectator cannot be seated into a full or started game. On the newest builds you are on the safest side of that line.

## How to Join as a Spectator

The flow is the same whether you are watching a friend's game, a public lobby, or a team match, and it has three reliable entry points. The underlying rule is that a spectator joins a lobby the same way a player does, then opts out of taking a seat.

The first and most common way is to enter the lobby code with the Spectate option selected. When you have a lobby code, open the join flow and choose **Spectate** rather than the normal Join Lobby action. The game connects you to that lobby in spectator state. You do not need an account in the match or a free player slot; the only requirement is that the lobby exists and is reachable. This is the path to use for a friend's game you know the code for, or for a public lobby you found in the lobby list.

The second way is through a shared game link. The same code or link that a player uses to join a lobby also lets a viewer join to watch. If someone shares their lobby link, you can open it and take the spectator route rather than a seat. This is useful when a player hands you the link so you can watch their game specifically, rather than picking a lobby yourself. The link routes you to the live lobby while it is running, and to a replay once the match has finished.

The third way is the Play/Spectate switch inside the lobby itself, available while the match has not started. Once you are in a lobby, the lobby screen offers a switch between playing and spectating. You can use it before the host starts to change your intention: if you joined to play but now want to watch, or joined to watch and a slot opens, you can toggle. This switch is shown for any joined lobby, including a host-started private lobby that has no scheduled start time, so it is not limited to public lobbies.

Three practical notes. First, you can arrive mid-game: a spectator who joins after the start does not miss the game, they simply watch the match as it is happening, including any state that was already on the board. Second, a host-started private lobby can be spectated, not just public scheduled lobbies. Third, on current builds the switch is hidden after the game has started, because the player list is frozen at start and the server would refuse to seat anyone new. If you are in the lobby before start, you can still decide at the last moment whether to play or to watch.

## What "No Seat" Actually Means

The phrase "spectators take no seat" is doing real work, and it is worth spelling out exactly what it implies, because it is the difference between spectating and joining. A spectator has no entry in the match's player list, no spawn, no team, and no player count against the lobby cap. That has several concrete consequences you can actually feel while watching.

A spectator never fills a seat, so the lobby can be at full capacity and still admit a viewer. If a six-seat lobby already has six players, a new spectator can still join and watch; the match does not become a seven-player game. This matters when you want to watch a full lobby and there is no room for a player. The spectator is not competing for a seat at all.

A spectator is not in the player list, so the players in the match do not see you as a participant and the match's population stays exactly what it was. The game's start information, which records which players hold which seats, has no line for a spectator. This is why a caster or a friend can watch a specific member's game without the viewer appearing as an extra actor. The match is played by the same set of players whether you are watching or not.

A spectator does not take a team, so there is no team to lose and no side to be loyal to. You are not assigned to any faction, and the game does not track your performance, your territory, or your score. This is what makes spectating safe for learning: you can watch a match without any of the pressure of being a participant, and you can watch a team game from a neutral vantage point instead of from inside one side. You can study how a player you respect plays without putting your own result on the line, and you can watch a team game from the outside instead of from inside one side, which gives you a neutral reading of how both teams are positioned relative to each other.

The "no seat" design is deliberate, and it is what distinguishes spectating from every other way to see a match in the game. A player in the lobby occupies a seat, is tracked by the match, and can be acted on by the game's rules; a spectator occupies none of those things. That is why the feature can admit a viewer into a lobby that is otherwise at full capacity without changing the match, and why a spectator can watch a pooled member's game from the lobby they were given a link to. The design is the reason the feature is safe to use in the situations you actually want to use it in, and it is the reason the boundaries in the rest of this guide are the way they are.

| | Spectator (watch) | Player (join) |
|---|---|---|
| Takes a seat in the lobby | No | Yes |
| Spawns / assigned a team | No | Yes |
| Counts against the lobby player cap | No | Yes |
| Appears in the player list | No | Yes |
| Can act in the match (orders, buildings) | No | Yes |
| Can switch to Play before start | Yes | N/A |
| Can switch to Play after start | No | N/A |
| Can join a full lobby | Yes (viewer only) | No |
| Is converted from clan overflow (team mode, tags on) | Can be | Yes (source) |
| Sees the live board | Yes | Yes |
| Extra god-view / hidden state | No | No |
| Subject to anti-attention checks | Yes (connected client) | Yes |

The table is the compact reference for the whole guide. If you remember one thing, remember the first four rows: a spectator takes no seat, spawns no unit, has no team, and does not count against the cap. Everything else in the guide, the switch rules, the refusal rules, the replay fallback, the clan-overflow conversion, is a consequence of those first four rows being true. If you are deciding whether to spectate or to join, the table tells you the cost of each choice. Spectating costs you the ability to act in the match; joining costs you a seat and a result. The right choice follows from whether you want to act or to observe, and the feature is built so that the choice is explicit and reversible up to the start of the match.

A spectator is, however, genuinely connected to the live lobby, not watching a separate offline viewer or a recorded clip. The match streams to you in real time from the same lobby the players are in. That connection is why the feature is "live" spectating and why it has real limitations, like the anti-attention checks that can fire for a connected client even though a spectator is not an active player. It is also why a mid-game arrival works: you are joining the live lobby, not loading a recording. The combination is the important part. You are fully in the live game's state, but not in any of its player seats. That is what lets you watch without changing the match you are watching.

## When You Can Switch, and When You Cannot

The most common confusion is about the Play/Spectate switch: when is it available, and what happens if you try to use it too late. The rule is tied to the moment the match starts, and it is worth internalizing because it decides whether watching can still become playing.

Before the game starts, the switch is fully available. If you are in the lobby waiting and the host has not pressed start, you can move between Spectate and Play freely. This is the window where your decision is still reversible: you can watch the lobby fill up, see which players are joining, decide you actually want to play, and take a seat before the match begins. The lobby screen shows the switch during this window for any joined lobby, public or private.

The moment the match starts, the switch disappears and the decision locks. The player list is frozen at start, so the server will not seat a new player after the game is underway. On current builds the switch is hidden entirely once the game is running, because keeping it visible would let you click a control that the server would immediately refuse. If you are spectating when the match starts, you stay a spectator for the whole match. If you are playing when it starts, you stay a player. There is no in-match demotion to spectator and no in-match promotion from spectator to player through this switch.

There is one path where a spectator can become a player, and it is a deliberate action by someone with authority in the lobby, not a self-serve toggle. A host or moderator can seat a spectator, but only where the lobby's rules allow it. The server refuses to seat a spectator when the game has started or ended, when the lobby is at full capacity, when the spectator fails the lobby's allowlist, when the lobby is trusted-only and the client is not trusted, or when the lobby's pool pins the client to a different member. Administrators and moderators bypass the allowlist and trust gates so they can reach and moderate any lobby, which is why a mod can intervene in a game you are watching. So in practice, on a normal public lobby after start, a spectator cannot become a player at all, and before start it is the switch that decides.

The practical takeaway: decide to watch or to play before the start button is pressed. If you are unsure whether you want to commit, spectating is the safer default, because it keeps the option open for as long as the lobby is waiting, and it does not cost you a seat you might not want. Once the match is live, the only way out of a spectator view is to leave the lobby and, if you want to play, join a different lobby that actually has an open seat.

## Failure Modes and What a Spectator Is Not

Spectating has a few edge cases where it does not behave the way a new player expects, and knowing them in advance prevents frustration. These are the places where "watching a live game" and "being a player" diverge in ways that matter.

The first is the anti-attention or multitab warning. Because a spectator is genuinely connected to the live lobby as a client, the game's anti-attention checks can fire for a spectator even though they are not playing. A real player reported getting the false-positive multitab warning while spectating, and the reason is structural: the spectator is a connected client in the lobby, so the same attention heuristics that are meant to catch AFK players can flag a pure viewer. The practical implication is that a spectator is not in a separate, passive, offline viewer mode. You are connected, the game is streaming to you, and some of the live-client checks still apply. If you are going to watch a long match and step away, expect the same kind of warning a player would get, and do not assume that because you are only watching you are exempt from the live-client behaviour.

The second is the full-lobby case in the other direction. A spectator takes no seat, so a full lobby can still admit a viewer, but that is asymmetric: being able to watch a full lobby does not mean you can also play in it. If the lobby is at capacity, you can spectate, but you cannot take the last seat, because there is no seat to take. The no-seat rule protects the viewer from being locked out, but it also means the lobby being full is not a signal that a player spot is available.

The third is the finished-match case. Once the match has ended, there is no live lobby left to watch, so opening the same game link or code routes you to a replay instead of a live spectator view. This is not a failure; it is the intended fallback. A match you watched live is not lost when it ends, because it is still available as a replay you can review afterward. The distinction that matters is timing: while the match is running you are spectating live; after it ends you are reviewing a replay. The same link does both, and the game decides which one applies based on whether the match is still going.

The fourth is the pool-redirect case. In lobbies that route members to a sibling or pooled lobby, a normal player can be redirected to the lobby the pool pins for them. A spectator is exempt from those redirects, which is exactly what lets a caster or a friend watch whichever member they were given a link for, even if that member's actual match is in a pooled lobby. So if you were handed a specific link and the match you expected is not where you landed, it is worth checking whether the game opened a replay or routed you to a pooled member's live match, rather than assuming the link is wrong.

Finally, a spectator is not a referee and not a participant. You cannot issue orders, take a building, or affect the match in any way; the match proceeds exactly as the players run it. If something looks off, you can watch it, you can flag it later, and a moderator can intervene, but the spectator state itself gives you no control over the outcome. That limitation is the feature, not a bug: it is what makes watching a way to learn rather than a way to interfere.

## Mode and Map Adjustments for Spectating

Spectating works the same in every game mode and on every map, but the behaviour you get differs by mode in a few specific ways, mostly around team mode and clan tags. Knowing these differences helps you pick the right kind of game to watch and to set expectations about what you will see.

In public FFA and standard public lobbies, spectating is straightforward: you watch the real players, you take no seat, and the player count is unaffected. This is the mode where the feature is most useful, because a public lobby can fill with real players and still admit a viewer who changes nothing. If your goal is to learn how other players open an FFA map or handle a contested border, this is the cleanest environment to watch in.

In team mode with clan tags enabled, there is an extra spectator rule that does not exist in the other modes. When a clan's members overflow the allowed per-clan count, the overflow players are not kicked out of the lobby; they are converted to spectators instead. So in a team game where one clan has too many tagged members, the surplus players stay in the lobby watching rather than being removed. This is a real decision-relevant behaviour: a clan that fields more members than the limit can still keep those members connected as viewers, and a team game you are watching may include players who are technically spectators because they overflowed their clan's cap. This conversion does not happen when the game is ranked, when names are anonymized, or when clan tags are disabled, so it is specific to the non-ranked, clan-tagged team-game configuration.

In ranked matches, the conversion rule is off, so a ranked team game will not silently turn overflow clan members into spectators. If you are watching to learn ranked play, keep in mind that the roster you see is the actual playing roster, and there is no clan-overflow-to-spectator behaviour to confuse your reading of who is playing and who is watching.

Map choice changes what you can see but not how spectating works. Spectating a large map lets you follow the whole board in real time, which is useful for learning front-line management; spectating a small map lets you track every player's position closely, which is useful for learning micro and opening choices. There is no spectator-only map, and no map where spectating is disabled. The one map-specific thing to keep in mind is that a spectator sees the live board exactly as it is, with no special camera or extra information; you are not given a god-view overlay that the players cannot see, and you are not given hidden state. You see what a connected client sees, from a neutral seat. So map choice is about what is on the board, not about what the spectator feature reveals.

## Watch Live Versus Review the Replay

The final decision this guide has to make is timing: when should you watch a match live as a spectator, and when is it better to let the match finish and review the replay afterward. Both are available, and the right answer depends on what you are trying to learn.

Watch live when you need the state of the game while it is happening. Spectating is the only way to see a match in real time without being a player, and that real-time view is what you need when you are learning a live skill: reading a developing front, watching a trade route get defended, following a nation's border decision as the minutes pass, or observing how a clan coordinates a push before it resolves. If the thing you want to learn only exists in the flow of the game, you have to watch it live, because a replay is a reconstruction, not the moment.

Review the replay when you need the evidence of a completed game. The replay path is a separate, well-documented system: a finished match can be opened from a public profile's game history, has a Stats view, can copy a normal game link, and opens the player information panel in replay spectator state. If your goal is to find the exact moment a plan broke and test a one-variable change for your next match, the replay is the better tool, because it is stable, repeatable, and lets you jump to the turning points instead of waiting for the game to play out. The [replay-review guide](/guides/replay-review/) owns that after-action method in full.

The two are complementary, not competing. A common, effective pattern is to watch a strong player's live game as a spectator while it happens, take notes on the decisions you want to understand, and then, after the match ends, open the replay to verify the specific moments you flagged. The live view teaches you the feel and timing; the replay gives you the evidence. Spectating is the entry point that lets you watch without taking a seat, and the replay is the follow-up that lets you study what you watched.

Three scenarios make the choice concrete. First, you want to learn how to open an FFA map, so you spectate a public FFA lobby while it runs and watch the first five minutes of three different players; you do not want to play because that would make you the fourth actor. Second, you are watching a team game to study how one clan coordinates a push, and that clan has eight tagged members in a six-seat team lobby, so the two overflow members are watching as converted spectators; you join to spectate and watch the whole push without adding a seat. Third, you are reviewing a match you just lost and you need to find the exact minute your second front collapsed; the live view is gone, so you open the replay from your game history and step through the territory threshold decision. In each case, the choice between watching live and reviewing the replay follows from what only one of the two views can give you, and the live-spectator path is the one that keeps you out of the match while it is still happening.
