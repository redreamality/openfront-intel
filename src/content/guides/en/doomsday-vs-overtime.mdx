## Direct answer: which breaker did your lobby roll, and what does that flip?

OpenFront can end a stalled match two different ways, and they pull in opposite directions. Overtime lowers the territory win threshold over time and ends the game when one side is strictly above it; it is on by default in public FFA lobbies. The Doomsday Clock is the opposite machine: a rising map-share bar that, when you fall below it, drains your troops down to a floor and then rots your territory until you are eliminated; it lands in public Special lobbies through a weighted modifier rotation. They never appear in the same game: Overtime is an FFA win-condition and the Doomsday Clock is a Special-playlist modifier, and a default public Team lobby uses neither. So the first endgame decision is not where to attack; it is to read which breaker the lobby actually rolled before you queue, and to flip your plan accordingly. An Overtime game is a hold-or-finish race against a falling threshold, and the correct posture is to defend a lead or convert cheap land at the right moment; a Doomsday game is a survive-the-wave race where the leader is immune and every challenger is on a visible countdown, and the correct posture is to push for the crown or convert enough land to stay above the next wave. The reason the read matters is that the two endgames are the inverse of each other: the same army, the same map, and the same minute can be a comfortable hold under Overtime and a death sentence under the Doomsday, so a player who guesses the breaker wrong spends the last ten minutes playing the wrong game. Reading the lobby once, in the first minute of the late game, is cheap and deterministic; guessing from the in-game number under pressure is the single most expensive mistake this page exists to prevent. The payoff of the read is concrete and measurable: under Overtime you are spending your late-game economy defending a one-point margin against a bar that keeps sinking, so every troop you commit to an unnecessary push is a troop you do not have to re-grow when the challenger tests your border; under the Doomsday you are instead spending that same economy either to take the crown or to buy the map share that keeps you above the next wave, and the moment the read is wrong you are funding a plan the lobby does not reward. Players who skip the read do not lose on strategy in the final minute; they lose on arithmetic, because the same army and the same land are worth a hold in one lobby and a forced push in the other, and the two arithmetic answers differ by more than the margin they can recover in the time they have left.

## Version boundary and where each breaker lives

This guide is verified against the `v0.34.19` tag of the OpenFrontIO repository on 2026-09-26, and it does not treat later upstream changes as live rules. Both anti-stalemate breakers are present in that tag, and the numbers below are the ones that tag ships, so a reader who is on a slightly different build should treat the exact minute marks as approximate while the posture and the relative structure stay the same. Overtime is a win-condition, not a modifier that shows up in a lobby list: the public FFA playlist enables it by default with no start-minute input, so every public FFA game carries it, while public Team and public Special lobbies do not. The Doomsday Clock is a Special-playlist modifier, rolled through the Special modifier pool with a weight of four in a pool of thirty-eight weighted slots, so it lands in roughly one in ten public Special games; when it lands, the lobby rolls one of four speeds — slow, normal, fast, veryfast — so the pacing varies from game to game. That placement is the single most important fact in the guide: a default public FFA match uses Overtime and does not use the Doomsday Clock, and a default public Team or Special match uses neither by default. The only variable from game to game is which breaker the specific lobby rolled, and the breaker is fixed once the match starts, so there is no mid-match switch that would change the machine you are playing against. Treat the two breakers as mutually exclusive endgame modes, not as two timers that might both be ticking, because the strategy they imply is the inverse of each other and you cannot safely play both at once. If you are on a private or singleplayer lobby, a host can enable either breaker by hand, and in that case the lobby modifier list is the only authority and the default-on rules for public play do not apply to you; the public-play schedules still describe what the host's hand-rolled settings are approximating, so the same posture logic holds and only the specific minute marks and bar values change. For the FFA win threshold and the land-share arithmetic that the Overtime bar operates on, the [Team Victory Threshold guide](/guides/team-victory-threshold/) is the reference, and for the full Doomsday wave table and decay math that this guide summarizes, the [Doomsday Clock guide](/guides/doomsday-clock/) is the deeper page. Two final scoping notes keep the version boundary honest: the guide treats the Overtime start at thirty minutes as the public default, so a private host who sets a different start minute changes the clock's anchor but not its two-points-per-minute fall or its no-floor rule, and the Doomsday ladder numbers are per-mode, so a Team lobby shows the denser seven-step ladder while an FFA lobby shows the sparser one; both facts are tag-verified and are the reason the guide states the numbers by mode rather than as a single shared table.

## How to tell which breaker is active before and after you queue

You get the signal twice: in the lobby before the match, and in the game after the start minute or the grace period. In the lobby, the mode tells you a lot: a public FFA lobby carries Overtime by default, and a public Special lobby that rolled the Doomsday Clock modifier shows it in the modifier list. If the lobby is public Team, neither breaker is on and this guide's schedules do not apply. Once the match is running, the in-game signal is unmistakable and distinct for each breaker. Overtime is a moving threshold: after the default thirty-minute start an Overtime panel appears showing the live percentage, and that number falls by two whole points every minute with no floor. The Doomsday Clock is a moving bar: after a ten-minute grace period the first ramp begins, a skull warning appears as the bar approaches a side, and the in-game readout shows the current required share of the map in percentage points. The two reads look different because they measure different things: Overtime reads your share of the map against a falling bar, and the Doomsday Clock reads your share of the map against a rising bar, with a drain or rot timer attached when you fall below. If the number in front of you is a percentage of the map that is getting smaller, you are in Overtime. If the number is a required percentage of the map that is getting larger and a skull or rot timer is visible, you are in the Doomsday. Make this read in the first minute of the late game and write down the breaker, because every decision on this page keys off that one label and the cost of guessing wrong is a full endgame plan built for the opposite mode. The confirmation rule is to use both signals together: the lobby mode and modifier tell you what is supposed to be on, and the in-game number confirms what is actually running, so if the two ever disagree you should trust the lobby as the committed rule and re-read the in-game number as the live consequence, because a lobby that rolled the Doomsday Clock will always show the rising bar and a public FFA lobby will always show the falling threshold once the start minute passes. One practical trap is the private-lobby exception: a private host can enable either breaker by hand, so the public defaults that this page states do not cover that case, and the lobby modifier list is the only authority when you are not in public play; if you are not sure you are in public play, re-read the lobby header for the mode and the modifier list before you commit to a posture, because the two signals are the cheapest deterministic read available to you and the cost of the wrong read is a plan built for the opposite machine.

## Overtime posture: the bar falls, so finish or defend the crossing

Overtime turns the endgame into a race against a clock that works in the leader's favor. The FFA win threshold is eighty percent, and after the default thirty-minute start it falls by two percentage points per minute, from eighty at thirty minutes to sixty at forty minutes and fifty at forty-five minutes, with no floor, so the bar keeps sinking until the end. The win check requires your usable-land share to be strictly greater than the live threshold, so sitting exactly on the bar is not a win and a one-point lead is a real lead that must be protected. That strictness is the whole strategic core: a player who holds eighty-one percent at the thirty-minute mark has not won yet, and each thirty-second step of the falling bar makes the win easier but also makes the challenger's path to the bar shorter. The Overtime posture has two branches. If you are the leader and above the bar, the correct move is to defend the lead and finish before a challenger converts the land that would put them above the now-lower bar; you do not need to take the whole map, you need to stay strictly above the number shown. The full threshold math and the hold-or-finish timing of that branch are the subject of the [Winning Overtime guide](/guides/winning-overtime/), and the land-share rules it builds on are in the [Team Victory Threshold guide](/guides/team-victory-threshold/); this guide only needs their conclusion, which is that the bar is a moving deadline and the margin is a one-point-kill zone. If you are the challenger and below the bar, you have two options and the decision is a cost calculation: push now to take the lead, or wait for the bar to fall below your share and then convert the cheapest safe land that crosses you. Waiting is real because the bar falls, so a challenger at seventy percent only needs the bar to reach sixty-nine, which at two points per minute is thirty seconds of waiting, and that wait can be cheaper than a full push. The Overtime endgame is therefore a hold-or-finish decision, and the rest of the page tells you when each branch is the cheaper one, with the concrete troop and economy costs of each branch in the scenario sections that follow. One thing to keep in mind is that the bar's fall is the only source of the challenger's option value in Overtime; if the bar were static the challenger would have no free path to the win and would have to push, but because the bar falls the challenger can sometimes buy the win with time and land conversion alone, and that asymmetry is exactly why the leader cannot treat Overtime as a passive hold.

## Doomsday posture: the bar rises, the challenger is drained, the leader is immune

The Doomsday Clock is the opposite machine: it does not lower a win line, it raises a survival line and then punishes anyone below it. After a ten-minute grace period, the required share of the map climbs in seven waves, with an FFA ladder of two, four, seven, eleven, seventeen, twenty-five, and thirty-five percent and a Team ladder of three, six, ten, fifteen, twenty-one, twenty-eight, and thirty-five percent. The pacing depends on the speed the lobby rolled: at normal the final bar lands at thirty-five minutes, at slow at forty-five, at fast at twenty-five, and at veryfast at fifteen. The bar is the same for every side regardless of headcount, so it shrinks the number of viable sides rather than scaling to the largest one. When a side falls below the bar, a skull appears and that side is marked: its troops drain down to a floor at five percent of max troops over about ninety seconds, and while at the floor its territory begins to rot and is eliminated at the one-fifty-second deadline after the skull. Critically, the drain applies only to challengers: the leading side is never doomed, so the clock culls the field toward the leader rather than the leader itself, and a doomed side that climbs back above the bar clears the mark and stops the drain, which is why a brief dip is recoverable. Warships behave differently from troops: they bleed on their own gentler start and a steep ramp to a much higher ceiling, so a ship caught when its side is first doomed lasts about as long as troops, but once the side has been under the clock for the full ramp the ship drops to the floor almost instantly, which means a doomed side's naval power collapses faster than its land army and a challenger that leans on a fleet is more exposed to the drain than one that leans on a standing army. The Doomsday posture is therefore to survive the waves: as the leader you hold and let the clock eliminate the field, and as a challenger you must either push to become the leader or convert enough land to stay above the next wave, because once you are below the bar you are on a visible countdown and the only two exits are to cross the bar or to be eliminated by the rot. The full wave table, the thirty-second warning window, and the recovery mechanics are worked out in the [Doomsday Clock guide](/guides/doomsday-clock/), and this page only needs the conclusion that the drain is a visible countdown and the leader is immune, which is the structural fact that separates the Doomsday posture from the Overtime posture and is the reason the two postures are the inverse of each other.

## The decision: the same position, the opposite move

The whole guide comes down to one table, because the position you are in means the opposite thing under the two breakers. Read your position, read the breaker, and take the move the table names. The failure the table exists to prevent is the conflation that players actually make: seeing a late-game countdown and assuming it is the breaker you know, then playing the wrong endgame for the last ten minutes of the match.

| Your position | Overtime active | Doomsday active |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leader, above the bar | Defend the lead, finish before the bar falls below your margin | Hold the core and let the clock cull the challengers |
| Challenger, below the bar | Push to take the lead, or wait for the bar to fall below your share then convert the cheapest safe land | Push to become the leader, or convert enough land to stay above the next wave |
| Leader, near the bar | A one-point lead is real; do not trade land you need | You are immune to the drain; the clock is on your side |
| Challenger, in the drain | No drain exists in Overtime; a losing share is a timing problem | You are on a countdown; the next wave is either a kill or a forced push |

The two columns are the inverse of each other by design. In Overtime the leader's problem is that the bar keeps falling and a challenger can cross it without winning the map, so the leader must finish; in the Doomsday the leader's problem does not exist because the leader is immune and the clock does the culling, so the leader must hold. In Overtime the challenger's problem is timing against a falling bar, which can be solved by waiting; in the Doomsday the challenger's problem is a rising survival line, which can only be solved by pushing or by converting land fast enough to stay above the next wave. The decision is therefore: identify the breaker, identify your position, and let the table pick the move. If you are unsure which breaker is active, default to the lobby read from the identification section rather than guessing from the in-game number, because the cost of the wrong guess is a plan built for the opposite machine. The table is the page's working artifact, and the sections that follow fill it with the numbers and the failure modes that make each cell real. One structural note about the table: the "Leader, near the bar" row is the row that most players get wrong, because a one-point lead in Overtime is a real lead that must be defended with a specific endgame plan, while a one-point lead in the Doomsday is the most comfortable position in the game because the leader is immune and the clock does the work of culling the challengers for free; treating that one-point lead as the same position in both games is exactly the conflation this page exists to prevent.

## Scenario A: Overtime, you lead at eighty-two, the bar is at seventy

Take a public FFA game that has Overtime on, with the default thirty-minute start. At thirty-five minutes the live bar is at seventy percent, and you hold eighty-two percent of usable land, so you lead the bar by twelve points and are comfortably above the threshold. The next step brings the bar to sixty-eight percent in a minute. Your position is the leader above the bar, so the Overtime column says: defend the lead and finish before a challenger crosses. The question is what that finish looks like. You do not need to take the last eighteen percent of the map; you need to make sure no challenger can take enough of your current land to put them above sixty-eight percent. So the endgame move is to pack your border on the direction a challenger is most likely to push from, hold your economy so you can re-grow any land that is taken, and convert only the specific pockets that keep your share safely above the bar. A challenger who is at sixty percent and pushes you has to take twelve percent of your land to reach seventy-two, which is a large push they must fund with troops they do not currently have. The scenario's assumption is that you are the clear leader with a twelve-point cushion and a challenger at or below seventy percent; if the cushion is smaller, the Overtime challenger branch of the table applies to the leader instead, and the leader must attack the challenger's growing share before the bar falls below it and the challenger can hold. The numbers in this scenario are the real FFA defaults: eighty percent at thirty minutes, two points per minute after, no floor. The economy math in this scenario is the point: a twelve-point cushion means the challenger would need to convert twelve percent of the map's usable land to reach your share, and at a typical late-game conversion rate that is a several-minute push they must fund with troops, so the correct leader move is not to spend troops on a preemptive strike but to hold the border and let the challenger's push fail on its own cost. If the challenger does take land, the correct leader response is to re-grow that land from your economy before the next bar step, because a bar step that falls below your current share while you are re-growing is a step that costs you the win.

## Scenario B: Doomsday, you are the challenger at fourteen, the bar is rising

Take a public Special FFA game that rolled the Doomsday Clock at normal speed. At the start of the third wave the bar has climbed to seven percent and the next wave is eleven percent in about five and a half minutes; you are the challenger with fourteen percent of the map, well above the current bar. The wave schedule means the next few waves are at eleven, seventeen, and twenty-five percent, and your map share is fixed by your economy and the land you can convert. If you do nothing, your share of the map will eventually fall as the bar rises, and the moment a wave lands above your share you enter the drain and the skull appears. So the challenger branch of the table applies: push to become the leader, or convert enough land to keep your share above the next wave. The push option is to attack the current leader's land and take enough to become the leader before the next wave, because the leader is immune and the instant you take the top share the clock stops applying to you. The convert option is to take enough cheap frontier land that your map share rises enough to keep you above the next wave without becoming the leader. The scenario's assumption is that you are the challenger with a map share above the current bar but below the next one, and that the leader's border is the cheapest path to the top share; if the leader's border is not cheap, the convert branch is the only safe move and a full push would land you below the next wave and into the drain. The numbers in this scenario are the real FFA normal-speed schedule: two, four, seven, eleven, seventeen, twenty-five, thirty-five percent, with the final bar at thirty-five minutes. The decision in this scenario is a cost comparison: the push to take the crown requires you to convert enough of the leader's land to cross their share, and that push must be funded with troops you can afford to lose without dropping below the next wave; if the cost of the push is higher than the cost of the convert, the convert is the correct move. If the cost of the convert is higher than the cost of the push, the push is the correct move. If both are cheaper than the cost of entering the drain, either is a viable endgame, and the tie-breaker is the direction the leader's border is weakest, because the weakest border is the cheapest path to the top share and the cheapest path to the top share is the cheapest path to winning the game.

## Failure modes and the counterplay that stops them

The failures are the mirror images of the two postures. In Overtime the failure is a leader who holds too passively: the bar keeps falling, a challenger converts a few pockets, and the leader's cushion shrinks to one point, and now the leader is the challenger in the table and must attack a growing share that was cheap to let grow. The counterplay is to treat the bar as a moving deadline: at each step, check whether your margin is still at least two points, and if it is not, convert the cheapest land that restores the margin before the next step. In the Doomsday the failure is a challenger who pushes into the drain: they commit troops to a push that drops their map share below the next wave, and now they are in the drain and on a visible countdown while the leader holds immune. The counterplay is to never push with a map share that will land below the next wave unless the push takes the top share; a push that does not become the leader and drops you below the next wave is a push that ends in elimination. The recoverable-dip rule is the one that saves the close cases: a doomed side that climbs back above the bar clears the mark and stops the drain, so a brief dip below the bar is survivable if you can convert enough land to cross it before the one-fifty-second elimination deadline. The shared failure across both breakers is the misread: playing the Overtime hold in a Doomsday game, where the hold means you sit still while the clock rots your territory, or playing the Doomsday push in an Overtime game, where the push spends troops the leader does not need to fund. The counterplay for the misread is the lobby read from the identification section: before the endgame, confirm the breaker from the lobby modifier and the in-game number, and if the two disagree, trust the lobby and re-read the in-game number, because the lobby modifier is the committed rule and the in-game number is the live consequence of that rule. One more failure mode is the one that players most often rationalize away: the leader who, in a Doomsday game, treats the leader immunity as a license to play passively and stop converting land, when in fact the leader's endgame in the Doomsday is still to convert enough land that the challengers' pushes do not succeed, because the clock culls the challengers only if the challengers are below the bar and the leader's share is high enough that the challengers' pushes cannot cross it; a leader who stops converting and lets their share fall is a leader who will eventually be the challenger in the table and will have to fight their way back up.

## Mode and map adjustments: Team, compact maps, and the speed roll

The two breakers behave differently by mode and map, and the schedules change with them. In Team, the Doomsday ladder is three, six, ten, fifteen, twenty-one, twenty-eight, and thirty-five percent, which is denser than the FFA ladder, so waves come more often and the challenger's countdown is shorter; the leader immunity is the same, but a Team side survives while either member does, so the field collapses more slowly. On a compact map, the total land is smaller, so the Overtime bar's fall in absolute land is smaller and the endgame is faster because there is less land to convert; the Doomsday rot is also faster in absolute terms because the territory that rots is a larger fraction of a smaller board. On a large map, the opposite holds: the Overtime endgame can drag because there is more land to convert and the bar's fall is a smaller slice of a bigger board, and the Doomsday rot is a smaller fraction of a larger board so the drain is slower to bite. The Doomsday speed roll is the map-independent variable that changes the pacing: a veryfast roll puts the final bar at fifteen minutes and compresses the whole clock, so a veryfast Doomsday game is a fast survive-or-die race, and a slow roll stretches the waves to forty-five minutes and gives the challenger more time to convert. The adjustment rule is the same as the rest of the guide: read the mode (FFA or Team) and the speed (for the Doomsday) first, because those two inputs fix the schedule, and then apply the position table from the decision section with those numbers filled in. One practical note about Team specifically: because a Team side survives while either member does, the Doomsday cull in Team is slower than in FFA at the same bar, because the leader's team does not die when one member drops below the bar and the other member holds above it; this means that in a Team Doomsday game the leader has more time to convert land and the challengers have more time to push, and the whole endgame is stretched relative to the FFA equivalent at the same speed. The map-size adjustment also interacts with the speed roll: a compact map with a veryfast roll is the most compressed endgame in the game, and a large map with a slow roll is the most stretched, so the two variables together define the pacing of the endgame and the correct posture depends on both of them.

## Checklist: read the breaker, read your position, take the move

Run this checklist in the first minute of the late game and again after every wave or every threshold step. First, read the lobby mode and the in-game number and write down the breaker: Overtime if the number is a falling share of the map, Doomsday if the number is a rising required share with a skull or rot timer. Second, read your position: are you the leader above the current line, or a challenger below it, and in the Doomsday, are you currently in the drain. Third, fill the schedule with your lobby's numbers: for Overtime, the start minute and the current bar; for the Doomsday, the speed, the current wave, and the next wave. Fourth, take the move the decision table names for your position and breaker: defend the lead or hold the core for a leader, push to take the lead or convert to stay above the bar for a challenger. Fifth, set the deadline: for Overtime, the next threshold step and whether your margin survives it; for the Doomsday, the next wave and whether your map share survives it, and the one-fifty-second elimination clock if you are in the drain. Sixth, name the counterplay in advance: the land you will convert if the margin or the share drops, and the push you will make if the leader's share is the cheapest path to the top. If any of the six is missing, you do not have an endgame plan yet, and the cost of finishing the match without one is the conflation this guide exists to remove: a player who sees a countdown and plays the breaker they know instead of the breaker that is actually in the lobby. The checklist is the page's working artifact, and running it in the first minute of the late game is the single highest-leverage move a player can make in an OpenFront match, because the two breakers are the inverse of each other and the cost of the wrong read is a full endgame plan built for the opposite machine. If you are in a private or singleplayer lobby, re-read the lobby modifier list before you run the checklist, because the public defaults that this page states do not cover that case and the modifier list is the only authority when you are not in public play. Two things to keep out of the checklist and out of the endgame: the temptation to re-derive the bar from memory under pressure, which is exactly the misread this page exists to prevent, and the temptation to treat the in-game number as the rule when the lobby modifier already committed to a breaker, because the lobby is the committed rule and the in-game number is the live consequence of that rule, and a number that does not match the lobby is a signal to re-read the lobby, not to abandon the lobby's plan.
