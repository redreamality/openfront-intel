## Direct answer: breaking the naval endgame lock

When a match has run to the point where land armies grind to a halt and both
sides have spent their nukes on each other's SAM walls, the game does not end —
it migrates to the sea. That is the naval stalemate, and it is the single most
common endgame in OpenFront because the Port economy keeps printing gold for
both players even after the land war stops. The question a player asks in that
position is always the same: how do I break a board where neither of us can win
by force on land? The answer this guide gives is a two-axis framework. The
**blockade side** wins by denying a *lane that moves gold* — one or two
Warships anchored on the opponent's main Trade and Transport route stall their
Port income and, because a captured Trade Ship redirects its route's gold to the
capturer, actively drain it. The **anti-blockade side** wins by *route
diversification and massed Transport* — two or more Ports on different coasts,
long trans-ocean trade routes that pay far more per ship and are harder to
fully screen, and a forward Port seeded behind the blockade by a successful
Transport landing so a second economy can run where the blocker cannot reach.

A blockade is leverage, not a win condition. A Warship sitting on a quiet bay
denies nothing and is a 250,000-gold decoration; a Warship on the opponent's
main trade lane is a money pump that only works while the blocker has the gold
to keep replacing the ships the opponent eventually breaks. The decisive
inflection in every naval stalemate is the same one a video of a collapsed
match shows: the moment the blocker's Warships stop existing, the Transport and
Trade economy runs unopposed and the gold race collapses in the defender's
favor. So the real decision is not "blockade or don't" but "which axis is
currently cheaper for me than for them." The rest of this guide builds the
decision framework around that question: the two gates for choosing a side, the
economics of the Port ladder and the trade-gold curve, the blockade playbook,
the anti-blockade playbook, the two worked scenarios, the failure modes, the
mode and map adjustments, and the point where the nuke becomes the axis that
finally decides it. The point of the framework is that the naval stalemate is
not a fight you win by out-fighting, it is an economics you win by out-denying
or out-diversifying, and the player who recognizes which side of the gate they
are on before the match reaches the lock is the player who is already deciding
the ending. The same board that looks unbreakable to the player who tries to
win it by force is breakable to the player who is already running the blockade
or the diversification that makes the gold race tip, which is the difference
this guide is trying to put in front of you.

## The two gates: choosing blockade or anti-blockade

You cannot know which side of the stalemate you should take until you check two
gates, because the right side is not the one that is "braver" — it is the one
whose next dollar buys more gold-denial or gold-capture than the opponent's
next dollar. Check both before you commit a single Warship or a single forward
Port. The gates are the **economics gate** and the **lane gate**.

The economics gate asks which player is currently converting gold into
sustainable income faster. A Port on the cost ladder is 125,000 for the first,
250,000 for the second, 500,000 for the third, and 1,000,000 for every one after
that, and it takes 50 units of time to build. A Factory or City on the same
ladder costs 125,000, 250,000, 500,000 and then caps at 1,000,000 but only takes
20 units to build. The player who already has a longer Port-and-Factory chain
has a larger gold faucet, and the player who does not is the one for whom the
blockade is the cheaper axis, because their problem is *stopping the other
faucet* rather than *out-pumping their own*. If you are behind on the Port
ladder and your opponent's main route is a single screenable strait, the
blockade side is yours: you are cheaper to defend a lane than to race them on
the ladder. If you are ahead on the ladder, the anti-blockade side is yours,
because you can out-produce the denial as long as your routes are diversified
enough that one Warship cannot cover them all. The economics gate is a ratio,
not a mood: the player with the bigger current gold delta wins the race if both
keep spending optimally, so the side that closes the delta fastest is the side
you should take.

The lane gate asks where the opponent's gold actually moves through the water.
A blockade only works on a *concentrated* lane — a strait, a gap between two
landmasses, or a single long route that has no cheap alternative. If the
opponent's trade is spread across three coastlines with three different route
geometries, a single Warship screens one of them and leaks the other two, so the
anti-blockade side is theirs by construction and you should be the one
diversifying instead. The rule is to count the lanes before you spend: one
screenable lane means the blockade side is available to whoever can hold it
longer; three or more lanes means the anti-blockade side is locked in and the
blockade is a losing spend. Check both gates in the same turn, and if they point
at the same side the answer is obvious; if they point at different sides, the
lane gate wins, because a blockade you cannot hold is a 250,000-gold mistake
regardless of what your economics say. The [warship fleet decisions
guide](/guides/warship-fleet-decisions/) covers the deeper placement logic once
you have chosen a side, but the gate decision here is the one that decides which
side to even try.

| Gate | Blockade side favored | Anti-blockade side favored |
|------|------------------------|------------------------------|
| Economics | You are behind on the Port/Factory ladder | You are ahead on the ladder |
| Lane | Opponent gold moves through one screenable strait | Opponent trade is spread over 3+ lanes |
| Fleet | You can replace the 1-2 ships they break | You can field 2+ ships that cannot be replaced |

## The economics: Port ladder, trade-gold curve, and what a blockade is worth

The whole naval stalemate is an economics problem with a naval skin, so the
numbers have to be concrete. The Port, Factory and City all share the same
exponential cost ladder: the first is 125,000 gold, the second 250,000, the
third 500,000, and every structure after that is 1,000,000. The Port is the
only one of the three that opens the naval economy — Trade Ships and Warships
both spawn from Ports — and it takes 50 units of time to build, double the 20
units a Factory or City takes. That means a Port is the slowest, most
expensive piece of the late-game income chain, and it is also the piece whose
income can be denied at sea, which is exactly why it is the chokepoint of the
stalemate. A Factory keeps printing troops and gold on land, but a Port's
income only flows if Trade Ships can actually make their round trip.

The trade-gold curve is the number that makes a blockade worthwhile at all. A
Trade Ship's arrival gold is `75,000 / (1 + exp(-0.03 x (dist - debuff))) + 50
x dist`, where `debuff` is the short-range penalty. This is a sigmoid: for long
routes the first term saturates near 75,000 and the second term adds 50 per
unit of distance, so a 2,000-unit trans-ocean route pays roughly 75,000 plus
100,000, about 175,000 gold per ship. For short routes the sigmoid collapses:
a 100-unit local loop pays far less than 5,000 from the base term, which is why
the game "heavily punishes" short routes and why a player who only has local
trade is economically weaker than one who has one long route. The strategic
consequence is that the *longest* route on the map is the most valuable lane to
denies, because it carries the most gold per ship, and the *shortest* route is
the cheapest to defend because it carries the least. A blockade should always
target the lane that carries the most gold per ship, not the lane that is
easiest to watch.

The value of a blockade is the difference between the gold the denied route
would have delivered and the gold the blocker instead captures. When a Warship
captures an enemy Trade Ship, the capturer's owner gains the route's gold as
piracy gold, so a single Warship on a 175,000-gold route does not merely
prevent the opponent from gaining 175,000 — it redirects that 175,000 to the
blocker. The effective swing of a single successful capture is therefore closer
to the full route value, not half of it. That swing is what funds the
blockade: it is the gold that buys the replacement Warships, the forward
Ports, and the nukes that the stalemate eventually demands. If a route only
carries 20,000 gold per ship, the capture swing is too small to fund the
replacement fleet and the blockade is not worth the 250,000 first ship; if the
route carries 150,000 or more, the capture swing funds the whole axis on its
own. The economics gate is ultimately this comparison: is the blocked lane
worth more per ship than the cost of holding it? If yes, blockade; if no, the
economics say diversify instead. The [embargo timing and trade control
guide](/guides/embargo-timing-and-trade-control/) covers how to time the trade
flow itself, which is the lever you pull once the lane decision is made.

## The blockade playbook: deny the lane, fund the follow-up

The blockade side has a strict sequence and skipping a step is the most common
way a blockade fails. The first step is to hold a single high-value lane with
one Warship, and the rule is to hold the lane that carries the most gold per
ship, not the lane that is nearest. A Warship costs 250,000 for the first,
500,000 for the second, 750,000 for the third and 1,000,000 for the fourth and
beyond, so a single-ship blockade is the cheapest credible denial and a
two-ship blockade is a real commitment. Place the first ship on the strait,
anchor it within its 150-tile passive-heal range of one of your own Ports so it
heals to full between engagements, and let it capture. The ship's patrol range
is 100 tiles and its targeting range is 130, so it does not need to be on top
of the route to screen it — it needs to be able to reach a Trade Ship within
130 tiles of its anchor. A Trade Ship that is 200 tiles from your anchor is
outside the screen; a Trade Ship that is 100 tiles in is inside it. That is why
a blockade works best on a narrow strait where the route is forced within
130 tiles of the anchor, and why it fails on a wide open ocean where the route
can simply detour around the 130-tile bubble.

The second step is the follow-up, and this is the step most players skip, which
is why a blockade usually produces a draw instead of a win. A blockade alone
does not win the game; it buys time and gold. The time it buys is the window in
which you spend the captured piracy gold on the axis that actually ends the
match. That axis is either a massed Transport landing on the opponent's coast
to seed a forward Port and start a second economy behind their line, or a
forced nuke escalation that the opponent's stalled income cannot absorb. Both
of those are described in the failure and nuke sections below. The rule is that
a blockade without a follow-up is a stalemate you are maintaining, not a win
you are producing, and the gold you are capturing is being spent on
replacement Warships instead of on the follow-up. The third step is to keep the
blockade sustainable: every Warship that the opponent eventually breaks costs
you the next tier of the 250k/500k/750k/1M ladder, so the capture swing has to
keep funding the replacement tier or the blockade collapses the moment the
opponent commits to breaking it. If the capture swing is only funding the
replacement Warships, you are spending your blockade budget on the blockade and
never on the follow-up, which is the definition of a draw. The blockade side
is a money-denial tool that only wins if the money it captures is redirected to
the follow-up, and the discipline of that redirect is the entire skill of the
axis.

## The anti-blockade playbook: diversify, mass, and seed forward

The anti-blockade side answers the denial with three moves, and the order
matters. The first move is route diversification, which is the structural
answer to a single-screen blockade. A single Port with a single long route is
exactly what a blockade is built to deny; two or three Ports on different
coasts with different route geometries is not, because the blocker's limited
Warships cannot cover every lane at once. Build the second and third Ports on
the cost ladder — 250,000 and 500,000 — and route them through water that is
not on the strait the blocker is holding. The economic payoff is double: the
long routes pay more per ship on the trade-gold curve, and the diversification
means the blocker's one or two ships can only screen one of the routes, so the
other routes keep flowing. The anti-blockade player who has three routes
running against a one-ship blockade is losing gold on the blocked route but
gaining gold on the two that are open, and the net is positive as long as the
open routes carry more than the blocked one, which is exactly the condition
the lane gate checks.

The second move is massed Transport, which is the kinetic answer once the
diversified economy is producing. A Transport Ship is free to build, and a
Warship gains one veterancy level per 10 Transport kills, which means the
blocker's ships get stronger the more Transports you throw at them. The answer
is to throw them in waves, not one at a time: a single Transport crossing a
blockaded lane is a guaranteed capture and a guaranteed piracy-gold transfer to
the blocker, but a wave of many Transports forces the blocker's limited
Warships to commit to the lane and either lets some through or trades the
blocker's ship for a wave. The discipline is to send the wave only when the
blocker's ships are at a disadvantage — retreating below the 75 percent
auto-retreat threshold, docked and healing, or simply outnumbered on the lane —
and to hold the wave back when the blocker's ships are fresh and at full
health. The third move is the forward Port, which is the win condition: once a
Transport wave successfully lands and holds a tile behind the blockade, you
build a Port there, and that Port starts a second economy on the far side of
the line where the blocker cannot reach. A forward Port is the anti-blockade
answer's endgame, because it converts a successful landing into permanent
income that the blockade cannot deny, and the moment the forward Port is
building and its Trade Ships are running, the stalemate is broken and the game
is a gold race in which the defender's economy has been copied behind their
line. The [transport landing guide](/guides/transport-landings/) covers the
specifics of the landing itself, but the forward-Port logic here is the one
that turns a landing from a raid into a win.

## Scenario A: the single-strait blockade with a nuke follow-up

This scenario is the canonical naval stalemate and it has concrete numbers so
you can check your own match against it. The setup: you and a Blue ally have
traded and nuked a stronger third player to survive, and now the Blue player's
navy and your navy are locked. Your opponent holds the only real strait that
connects their three main Ports to their trade, and your own trade is local and
short. The economics gate says the blockade side is yours, because your Port
ladder is behind theirs and your local trade is short-range and low-value. The
lane gate says the blockade side is yours, because their gold moves through one
screenable strait. The plan is to hold the strait with two Warships — the first
at 250,000, the second at 500,000 — anchored within 150 tiles of your own Port
so they heal, and to let them capture. Their main route is a long trans-ocean
route worth roughly 150,000 gold per ship, so each capture redirects that
150,000 to you as piracy gold and prevents them from gaining it. Over a
blocking window of, say, 30 Trade Ship crossings, a two-ship blockade that
captures 60 percent of them is worth roughly 270 captures times 150,000, or
about 40,000,000 gold of swing, which is the budget for the follow-up.

The follow-up is the nuke, because the opponent's income is stalled and they
cannot afford to absorb a MIRV. The MIRV costs 25,000,000 with an additional
15,000,000 for every MIRV you have already launched, and a Missile Silo costs
1,000,000. The point is that the blockade is what funds the MIRV: without the
270 captures of piracy gold, you do not have the 25,000,000 to launch, and the
stalemate continues. With the captures, the MIRV is affordable and the
opponent's stalled income means they cannot build the SAM Launcher (1,500,000
for the first, the ladder min(3,000,000, (n+1) x 1,500,000), 300-unit build)
fast enough to intercept it. The sequence is: hold the
strait, capture 25,000,000 of piracy gold, buy the Missile Silo, launch the
MIRV, and the opponent's SAM wall — which they can only extend if their trade
keeps flowing — is the thing that finally breaks. The failure mode of this
scenario is the one the Reddit thread describes: the opponent has 60 Factories
and can keep nuking your Transports even after you blockade their trade, so
the blockade stalls the Trade but not the nuke. The fix is to target the
Factories with the nuke follow-up, not the Trade, because the Factories are
what fund the opponent's nukes, and a blockade that only stops the Trade while
the Factories keep printing is a partial blockade that the opponent can
out-last. The nuke follow-up has to be aimed at the income source, not the
income route.

## Scenario B: the multi-route anti-blockade that masses through

This scenario is the mirror image and it is the answer when the lane gate says
the anti-blockade side is yours because the opponent's trade is already spread.
The setup: the opponent holds two Warships on the strait that connects your
main Port to your longest route, and your other two Ports are on different
coasts with routes that do not cross that strait. The lane gate says the
blockade is theirs but it only screens one of your three routes. The economics
gate says the anti-blockade side is yours, because your Port ladder is ahead
and you can out-produce the denial. The plan is to let the blocked route lose
gold for a window while the other two routes keep flowing, and to use the net
positive gold to build the forward Port.

The forward Port is the endgame. Your two open routes are long trans-ocean
routes worth roughly 150,000 gold per ship each, and together they carry more
gold per crossing than the single blocked route, so the net is positive. You
send a wave of Transports not through the strait the opponent is holding but
along the coast where their two Warships cannot reach, landing on a tile behind
their line and building the forward Port there at 125,000. The forward Port
starts its own Trade Ships on the far side, and now you have four routes
running against their two-ship blockade, which means their two ships can
screen two of the four and the other two keep flowing. The massed-Transport
rule applies here too: the wave only goes in when their ships are retreating or
outnumbered on that coastal lane, and the wave holds back when their ships are
fresh. The win condition is the forward Port being fully built and its Trade
Ships running, because at that point the stalemate is broken and the game is a
gold race in which your economy has been copied behind their line. The failure
mode is the wave being sent through the strait they are holding instead of
around it, which trades the wave for their captures and gives them the piracy
gold they need to extend the blockade. The anti-blockade side is only as good
as the discipline to go around the screen, not through it, and the forward Port
is only worth the 125,000 if it actually gets built on the far side. The
[nuclear stalemate breaker guide](/guides/nuclear-stalemate-breaker/) covers the
point where this gold race meets the nuke axis, which is where scenario B
usually ends.

## Failure modes and the counter to each

The most common failure on the blockade side is the draw, which is a blockade
that stalls the Trade but never funds the follow-up. The tell is that your
capture swing is entirely going to replacement Warships and not to the nuke or
the forward Port, which means the blockade is a maintenance cost, not a win.
The counter is to stop buying the fourth Warship once you have two and redirect
the next capture swing to the Missile Silo or the forward Port, because the
follow-up is what ends the match and the fourth ship is a 1,000,000-gold
commitment that only pays if the follow-up is already in motion. The second
failure is the short-route blockade, which is denying a lane that carries 20,000
gold per ship when the capture swing is too small to fund the replacement
fleet. The counter is to check the trade-gold curve before you spend the 250,000
first ship, and to skip the blockade if the lane is not worth at least the cost
of the ship it is meant to fund. The third failure is the wide-ocean blockade,
which is anchoring a ship where the route can detour around the 130-tile
targeting bubble. The counter is to only blockade a strait where the route is
forced within 130 tiles of the anchor, and to treat a wide open ocean as a
lane that cannot be held.

The most common failure on the anti-blockade side is the single-route economy,
which is building one Port with one long route and expecting it to out-produce
a blockade that is built specifically to deny that route. The counter is to
build the second and third Ports on different coasts before the blockade is
fully established, because once the strait is held the window to diversify is
narrow. The second failure is the single-Transport crossing, which is sending
one Transport through a blockaded lane and giving the blocker a guaranteed
capture and a guaranteed piracy-gold transfer. The counter is the wave, which
forces the blocker's limited ships to commit and trades their ship for the wave
rather than capturing every Transport one at a time. The third failure is the
wave sent at full health, which is throwing the wave while the blocker's ships
are fresh and at full 1,000 health and their veterancy bonus at maximum. The
counter is to time the wave to the blocker's retreat window, when their ships
are below the 75 percent auto-retreat threshold and docked and healing, because
a Warship at 700 health kills fewer Transports than a Warship at 1,000. The
fourth failure is the wave sent through the held strait instead of around it,
which is the anti-blockade equivalent of the wide-ocean blockade, and the
counter is the coastal lane where the blocker's ships cannot reach. Every one
of these failures is a discipline failure, not a mechanics failure, which is
why the gate framework above is the thing that prevents them.

## Mode and map adjustments

The framework above is written for the standard FFA map, but the lane gate and
the follow-up change by mode and by map shape. On a map with a single dominant
strait — the classic chokepoint geography — the blockade side is almost always
available, because the lane gate is easily met and the strait is narrow enough
that the route is forced within 130 tiles of an anchor. On that map, the player
who reaches the strait first with the first 250,000 Warship has the
blockade-side option open to them, and the player who is behind on the Port
ladder should take it. On a map with open water and no dominant strait, the
blockade side is rarely available, because the route can detour around any
anchor, and the anti-blockade side is the default for both players, which means
the game is a pure Port-ladder race with no naval denial. The rule is to check
the map's water geometry before the match: a dominant strait means the blockade
axis is in play, and open water means it is not.

In Team mode the adjustment is coordination, because the blockade and the
follow-up can be split between two players. One player holds the strait with
the Warships and the capture swing, and the other player builds the forward
Port and the nuke with the captured gold, so the blockade and the follow-up
run in parallel instead of in sequence. The [team naval control
guide](/strategies/team-naval-control/) covers the specific roles, but the
principle is that the naval stalemate in Team mode is a two-player problem and
the player who is holding the lane should not also be the one building the
forward Port, because the two jobs compete for the same attention. In
Asymmetric mode the adjustment is the ladder gap: if one player starts with a
Port and the other does not, the economics gate is already decided and the
player behind on the ladder should take the blockade side, because their
problem is stopping the other faucet, not racing their own. The mode and map
do not change the mechanics — the cost ladder, the trade-gold curve, the
capture redirect, and the 130-tile screen are the same in every mode — but they
change which side of the gate is available and who should be doing the
follow-up, which is the only thing that matters for the decision. The practical
takeaway is to run the two gates at the start of the endgame on whatever map
and mode you are in: count the lanes, check the ladder gap, and name the side
you are taking before the first Warship is built, because the player who names
the side first has the first Warship, the first capture, and the first follow-up
funded, and the naval stalemate is won by the player who decides it a turn
before the other player does.

## The nuke axis: where the stalemate finally ends

The naval stalemate does not end on the sea; it ends where the economy the
blockade is buying meets the nuke, and the nuke is the axis that finally decides
it. The Atom Bomb costs 750,000, the Hydrogen Bomb 5,000,000, and the MIRV
25,000,000 with an additional 15,000,000 for every MIRV you have already
launched, and a Missile Silo costs 1,000,000 with a 100-unit build. The
SAM Launcher costs 1,500,000 with a 300-unit build and is the only structure
that can intercept the MIRV. The point is that the blockade's entire purpose is
to change the economics of this escalation: a player whose Trade is denied
cannot build the SAM Launchers fast enough to intercept a MIRV, because the
SAM Launchers are funded by the same Port income the blockade is stalling. So
the nuke follow-up is not an alternative to the blockade — it is the blockade's
reason for existing. Without the blockade, both players have the gold to build
SAM Launchers and the MIRV is intercepted; with the blockade, the opponent's
SAM budget is stalled and the MIRV gets through. The [which nuke to commit
guide](/guides/which-nuke-commit/) covers the specific choice between the Atom,
Hydrogen and MIRV, but the point here is that the naval stalemate is the
decision that makes the MIRV affordable for one side and unaffordable for the
other, and the side that can afford it wins the game.

The final inflection is the same one in every naval stalemate: the moment the
blocker's Warships stop existing, the Transport and Trade economy runs
unopposed and the gold race collapses in the defender's favor, which is the
exact moment the nuke axis becomes the deciding one. If you are the blocker and
your ships are gone, the nuke follow-up has to be ready before the ships go,
because once the ships are gone the follow-up has to come from a gold pool you
have already built, not from captures you are no longer making. If you are the
defender and the blocker's ships are going, the nuke follow-up is yours,
because the blocker's stalled income means they cannot afford the SAM Launcher
to intercept you, and the MIRV you can afford now is the one that ends the
stalemate. The naval axis is the lever, the nuke axis is the blade, and the
player who coordinates the two — who holds the lane long enough to fund the
MIRV and launches the MIRV at the moment the opponent's SAM budget is stalled —
is the player who breaks the endgame lock. That coordination, not the
individual ship or the individual Port, is the skill this whole guide is
pointing at, and it is the thing that separates the players who break the
stalemate from the players who maintain it.
