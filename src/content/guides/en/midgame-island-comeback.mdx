## Direct answer: retreat when the mainland math stops favoring you

Retreat to an island mid-game when your mainland position has lost one of two things: cheap land access to keep growing, or the safe distance to keep your army from being bled. Once you commit, do not build a normal economy. Build a fortress in this order: one Port for income, your first completed City to push the troop cap, a Warship screen to deny the rival's navy, a spread of SAMs as a deterrent, and a Silo for a MIRV when you can afford the price. The single decision that wins the comeback is not the retreat itself. It is refusing to keep spending on warships after your rival starts outbuying you at sea, and refusing to leave the game after a MIRV lands on you. The build order, the retreat thresholds, and the version boundary all come from the v0.34.22 tagged source. The [island defense guide](/guides/island-defense/) covers the reverse case where you are the coast player holding a landing; the [post-MIRV recovery guide](/guides/post-mirv-recovery/) covers the minutes after a warhead has already landed on your core.

A comeback has three prerequisites, and none of them is optional. The first is a reachable island: you need an island large enough to hold a Port, a completed City, a warship screen, and a SAM distribution, and it has to sit in open water the opponent cannot rush over land, because a "fortress" one tile from the enemy's border is just a mainland position with a different name. The second is a gold reserve: the build order below spends well over a million Gold before it starts producing, and if you retreat with no reserve you will be out of money the moment the enemy screens up. The third is a correct read of the match: the comeback only works if the mainland position is genuinely lost, because a successful retreat on a still-winning position is just a self-inflicted slowdown that hands the lead back to the opponent.

The timing is the part that is hardest to read in real time. A retreat that is too early costs you the map, and a retreat that is too late costs you the match, so the signal to watch is not a clock but the gap between your troop cap and your income. When that gap is narrowing, the mainland still works and you should stay. When the gap is widening, the mainland is already lost and the island is the only position left that can still win. That gap is the whole decision, and the rest of this guide is the checklist for confirming it before you commit.

## When the mainland is already lost

Most losing mid-games are not lost by a single bad fight. They are lost by a player who kept running the same expansion loop that worked in the first twenty minutes, after the loop stopped being profitable. The mainland expansion loop only works while two conditions hold. The first is land access: you need cheap, reachable tiles on the open border so that pushing forward does not cost a full army to take one tile at a time. The second is safe distance: you need the rival far enough away that your army can recover between contacts instead of being forced into a fight while below cap. When the rival consolidates, builds a Port on the only coast you can reach, or simply becomes large enough that every border you push is a full-frontal war, one or both of those conditions has already broken.

The retreat trigger is a ratio, not a timer. Watch your army cap against your income. If you are sitting below a large fraction of `maxTroops` while spending Gold on units that immediately die at a border, you are not spending on an army. You are spending on a funeral. The cap formula is `2 × (tilesOwned ^ 0.6 × 1000 + 50,000) + (sum of completed City levels × 250,000)`. On a small mainland position with no completed Cities, the tile term is doing almost none of the work, and a rival with the same tile count but one extra completed City has 250,000 more cap than you. That is not a small gap. It is a gap that means your army regenerates to a ceiling that is structurally below theirs, so every fight you lose leaves you a permanently smaller army. The moment the cap gap widens faster than your tile growth can close it, the mainland is a dead position and the island becomes the correct next move.

A useful way to confirm the read is to project the border one step forward. If pushing the border one tile would cost you the full army and leave you at the enemy's border with nothing behind you, the border is not a position, it is a liability. The mainland has a second, less visible cost: every tile you own is a tile that can be taken back. The larger your position, the longer the border, and the more of your cap the rival can threaten at once. On an island, the border is the coastline, and the coastline is the shortest border on the map. That is the geometric argument for the retreat, and it is the reason the fortress works even when the mainland does not.

## What an island actually buys you

An island changes the geometry of the game in three specific ways. First, it gives you a natural border. Every tile of coast on the island is a wall that the rival must cross by sea, which means the rival cannot simply push over the border the way they can on land. The [transport landings guide](/guides/transport-landings/) covers the mechanics of how a rival gets troops across water. From your side, the point is that any amphibious attack is slower, more visible, and more expensive than a land border, so you get warning time that the mainland never gave you.

Second, it makes your cap independent of the rival's pressure. On the mainland, the rival can shrink your cap by shrinking your tiles. On an island, your tiles are the island. If the island is large enough, your tile term in the cap formula is a fixed, defensible number, and the only way the rival lowers it is by actually landing and taking tiles. That changes the pressure model from a slow bleed to a binary event: either the rival lands or they do not. You can plan for a binary event. You cannot plan for a bleed you cannot see.

Third, it turns your navy into a force multiplier instead of a liability. On the mainland, a Warship is a patrol unit that the rival can ignore or trade against one-for-one. Around an island, a Warship is the gate. Any rival unit that reaches your coast is a target for your screen. The Warship costs `min(1,000,000, (n+1) × 250,000)` per ship, so the first few ships are cheap, and the retreat logic is to buy exactly enough to make the rival's navy more expensive than their landing, not enough to win a war at sea. Once the rival's warship spend is higher than the value of the troops they are protecting, they stop the amphibious attempt. That is the whole point of the screen.

A fourth, quieter benefit is that the island isolates your income from the front. On the mainland, every Port and Factory sits within reach of the border, so a rival who closes the distance can strike the income that funds the army. On the island, the only income you can lose is the income the rival physically lands and takes, which forces them to commit troops before they can touch your economy. That is the difference between a front that bleeds and a position that can be held: the island makes every attack on your income a landing first, and a landing is exactly what the warship screen and the SAM spread are built to deny. Together the four benefits give you something the mainland never does, which is a position where the only thing that can end your game is a visible, committed, and expensive enemy decision.

## The fortress build order, and why

The build order is not aesthetic. It is the sequence that keeps each decision reversible while the previous one is still paying for itself. Start with one Port. The Port and Factory share a cost ladder of `min(1,000,000, 2^n × 125,000)`, so the first Port is 125,000 Gold and the second is 250,000. The first Port is the cheapest income you will ever buy on the island, and it is also the cheapest thing you will ever build, so there is no reason to delay it. You need Gold to fund everything else, and the Port is the only building that generates Gold rather than consuming it.

Next, build your first completed City. A City costs `min(1,000,000, 2^n × 125,000)` and takes 20 ticks to complete, and each completed City adds 250,000 to your maxTroops. The first City is the single most important building on the island. It is not an income building. It is a cap building. Without it, your army cap is whatever your tiles give you, which on an island is a fixed, modest number. With it, your cap jumps by 250,000, which is the difference between an army that can defend the coast and an army that is structurally too small to matter. The City is also the anchor for your fallback. If the rival lands and takes your Warship, your SAM, your Port, your City is still standing, and the 250,000 cap it gives you is still paying for your army.

After the first City, build a Warship screen. One or two Warships are enough to deny a small rival's navy. Buy exactly enough to make the rival's warship spend more expensive than the troops they are protecting. Do not spam Warships. The warship spend cap is the most common comeback killer. A community tutorial notes that losing five Warships is a loss of five million Gold, and that if they are getting shot down you want to stop spending. The screen is a deterrent, not a war. Once the rival's navy is outspent, the screen has done its job and the Gold you were going to spend on a sixth Warship belongs on the next Port or the next City.

Then, and only then, build a spread of SAMs. A SAM Launcher costs `min(3,000,000, (n+1) × 1,500,000)` and takes 300 ticks, or 30 seconds, to build. The SAM cooldown is 90 ticks. The community read is that a SAM is a deterrent, not absolute protection, and that the only time to stack them is a stalemate where you have a stockpiled bank. The comeback position is exactly a stalemate. You are the smaller player, the rival is the larger player, and the game is stalled until one of you makes a mistake. Spread a handful of SAMs across the island so that a MIRV has to be thrown more carefully and less destructively. One or two SAMs on the coast is not enough. You need the spread so that the rival has to decide which part of the island to MIRV, and the wrong answer costs them the war.

Finally, build a Silo for the MIRV. A Missile Silo costs a fixed 1,000,000 and takes 100 ticks. The MIRV itself is `25,000,000 + 15,000,000 × game-wide MIRVs launched`. The first MIRV of the game is 25 million Gold. That is a lot. But a comeback that stalls into a stalemate is a comeback that has time to save. The MIRV is not the comeback. It is the closing. You build the Silo early so that the cooldown and the price ladder are already in play when the stalemate ends, and you fire the MIRV when the rival's army is at its largest and the stalemate is about to break. The [MIRV mechanics guide](/guides/mirv/) covers the 350-warhead envelope and the SAM interception math. The point here is that the Silo is the building that turns the island from a slow death into a winnable game.

## Two scenarios with numbers

**Scenario A: the clean retreat.** You are mid-game, behind, and the rival has built a Port on the only coast you can reach. Your cap is 350,000, the rival's is 600,000. You have 1.2 million Gold. The correct move is to retreat to an island of roughly 400 tiles. You build one Port for 125,000. Your income comes online. You build the first City for 250,000. Your cap jumps to 600,000, matching the rival. You build two Warships for 250,000 and 500,000. Your screen is up. The rival's navy is now more expensive than their landing. You build one Port and one SAM over the next few minutes. The rival's amphibious attempt fails. You have 2.4 million Gold and a cap that matches the rival's. The game is no longer lost. It is a stalemate, and the stalemate is the correct position for the smaller player.

**Scenario B: the failed screen.** You retreat to the island, build the Port, build the first City, but you also build four Warships because the rival's navy is pressing hard. Your Gold is now 400,000 and your cap is still below the rival's because you did not build a second City. The rival's navy is outspent but the rival does not care, because the rival is large enough to ignore your screen and push the land border you never closed. You are now in a land war on the island with a smaller army and no Gold to rebuild. The failure mode is the warship spam. The counter is the second City. Had you built the second City instead of the fourth Warship, your cap would have been 850,000 and the rival's amphibious attempt would have been a fight you could win. The lesson is that the fortress is a cap game, not a warship game, and the moment you start spending more on Warships than on Cities, the comeback has already lost.

**Scenario C: the MIRV closing.** You are in the clean stalemate from Scenario A. The rival has a Silo, you have a Silo, and neither side will blink first because both sides can afford the price. You have 4.2 million Gold saved. The rival fires the first MIRV at your tile share, which costs them 25,000,000 and leaves them at 1.8 million Gold and a 15,000,000 price on the next one. You read the reload window: the rival's Silo enters its cooldown after that strike, and their army is at cap on your coast because the landing they tried to fund is now unfunded. You fire your MIRV into the rival's main base during the reload window. The rival has 15,000,000 Gold and a 15,000,000 price on the next MIRV, which means they cannot afford a second one. You have 15,000,000 and a 30,000,000 price on yours, which you also cannot afford. But you have a larger army at the coast and the rival's base is damaged. The comeback is won not by the MIRV but by the reload window it creates, and the only reason the window exists is the SAM spread that made the rival's first strike a visible, aimed decision rather than a free hit.

## Failure modes and how to counter them

The first failure mode is the warship spam. You keep buying Warships because the rival's navy is visible and threatening, and you never build the second City. The counter is the two-City rule: you need two completed Cities before you buy a fourth Warship. The second failure mode is the slow death. You retreat, build the fortress, and the rival simply waits you out, hoping the overtime bar will sink below your tile share. The counter is the MIRV. A stalemate is not a loss. It is the correct position. You build the Silo and you fire the MIRV when the rival's army is at its largest.

The reason the slow death is the most common way the comeback actually fails is that the player who has retreated stops being aggressive and starts being passive, and a passive island is exactly the kind of island a rival can plan a landing against. The fortress only holds while you are still the one making the decision; the moment you stop deciding, the rival takes it. So the slow-death counter is not just the MIRV, it is the refusal to let the stalemate become a waiting room. You keep the screen turning, you keep the SAM spread aimed, and you keep the Silo ready, so that when the rival finally commits to the landing, they are committing into a position where every part of it has already been paid for. The slow death and the MIRV are the same decision twice: you either hold the island as an active position, or you hold it as a corpse, and only the first one wins. The third failure mode is the rival's counter-landing. The rival builds their own Port and Warship screen and tries to land on your island. The counter is your SAM spread. A spread of SAMs makes the rival's MIRV more expensive and less certain, and the rival has to decide which part of the island to attack. The wrong decision costs them the game.

The fourth failure mode is the retreat to a too-small island. You pick an island because it is reachable, not because it is large enough to hold the fortress. The island is 150 tiles, the build order fits, but the tile term in the cap formula is so small that your cap never recovers, and the rival only needs a small landing to drop your tile count below your army's needs. The counter is the 300-tile rule: an island under roughly 300 tiles is a staging position, not a fortress, and you should keep the mainland or pick a larger island. The fifth failure mode is the late retreat. You retreat after the rival has already built a Port and a Warship screen of their own, so the island you are retreating to is now contested water. The counter is to retreat before the rival screens the coast, or to skip the comeback and play the [midgame survival guide](/guides/midgame-survival-low-threat/) instead. A comeback that arrives after the rival has committed their navy is not a comeback, it is a second, worse position.

## Mode and map adjustments

The island comeback works best on maps with a single large island and a clear open ocean, like Pharaoh Islands or any map where the islands are separated by deep water. On a map with many small islands, the comeback is harder because the rival can split their navy and land on multiple islands at once, and your screen has to cover more coast. The counter is to pick the largest island and to build the fortress there, and to ignore the smaller islands. On a map where the rival is already on an island, the comeback is a different question entirely, and the [island defense guide](/guides/island-defense/) is the correct reference. In 40-player FFA, the comeback is easier because there are more potential rivals and the rival is less likely to commit to a single amphibious attempt. In 2-player, the comeback is harder because the rival has no reason to ignore your island and will commit to the landing. The [FFA strategy guide](/guides/ffa-midgame-strategy/) covers the 40-player decision tree.

The mode also changes the value of the SAM spread. In a low-player-count game, the rival has time to pick the soft part of your island and commit to it, so the spread has to be dense enough that every landing zone is covered. In a high-player-count FFA, the rival is more likely to be fighting someone else on the coast, which means the spread can be sparser and the screen can do more of the work. The map changes the cost of the landing itself: a map where the water between the mainland and the island is shallow and short makes the rival's transport cheap, and the island stops being a fortress because the enemy reaches your coast before your screen can turn. On those maps the comeback is only viable if you pick the island that is farthest from the rival's nearest Port, and that distance is the thing you are actually defending.

The number of players also changes which of the five decision-table rows you should weight most. In a 40-player FFA, the fourth row, the screen, is the row that decides the game, because the rival is more likely to be split across several fronts and the screen only has to beat one of them. In a 2-player game, the first row, the cap, is the row that decides the game, because the rival has no reason to split and will commit to the landing the moment the screen is down. In every mode, the third row, the Gold, is the row you should re-check the moment the rival fires the first MIRV, because that strike is the one event in the game that changes the Gold math on both sides at the same time. The mode changes the weight of the rows; it does not change the order, and it does not change the fact that the fifth row, the two Cities, is the closing condition in every mode and on every map.

## A quick decision table

The table below is the quick reference for the comeback decision. Read it in order. If you answer yes to the first row, you are in a lost mainland position and the island is the correct next move. If you answer yes to the second row, the island is large enough to be a fortress. If you answer yes to the third row, you have the Gold to fund the build order. If you answer no to any row, the comeback is not viable and you should play the [midgame survival guide](/guides/midgame-survival-low-threat/) instead.

| Question | Yes means | No means |
|---|---|---|
| Is your cap below the rival's and your army bleeding at the border? | The mainland is lost; retreat to the island. | Stay and fight the border. |
| Is there an island of at least 300 tiles you can reach? | The island is large enough to be a fortress. | The comeback is not viable. |
| Do you have at least 1.5 million Gold? | You can fund the build order. | Save for the second City first. |
| Is the rival's navy outspent by your screen? | The screen is working; stop buying Warships. | Build one more Warship or stop the screen. |
| Have you built two completed Cities? | You can afford the MIRV closing. | Build the second City before the MIRV. |

The order of the rows matters because each row is a precondition for the next. The first row answers whether you are even in a comeback position, and answering yes here is a cost: you are admitting the mainland is gone. The second row answers whether the destination can actually hold the fortress, and an island that is reachable but too small will let you retreat into a losing land war rather than a held position. The third row answers whether you have the Gold to pay for the build order at all, and a no here does not mean the comeback is impossible, it means you should stop spending on the front and save for the second City first. The fourth row is the one that most players get wrong, because it is the moment the comeback is supposed to start working and instead it keeps spending on Warships; if the answer is no, you have not finished building the screen and you should build one more Warship before touching the Cities. The fifth row is the closing condition: once the two Cities are complete and the cap has recovered, the MIRV is the closing move, and building a fourth Warship at that point is the failure mode that loses the game.

If you answer yes to all five rows, the comeback is fully funded and you play the build order and the MIRV closing in the order given above. If you answer yes to the first four rows but not the fifth, you are one City away from the closing and the correct play is to save Gold and refuse further Warships. If you answer yes only to the first row, you have a lost mainland but no funding, and the correct play is to retreat to the largest reachable island and start the build order while you save, accepting that the comeback will take longer than the rival expects. The table is a check, not a timer: run it at the moment you are considering the retreat, and run it again after the rival fires the first MIRV, because the answer to the fourth and fifth rows changes the moment the rival's Silo goes on cooldown.


## What to do after the MIRV lands on you

If the rival MIRVs your island, the [post-MIRV recovery guide](/guides/post-mirv-recovery/) is the correct reference. But the comeback framing changes the recovery decision. On the mainland, a MIRV is a slow death because your tiles are shrinking and your cap is falling. On the island, a MIRV is a binary event because your tiles are the island and the only way the rival lowers your cap is by landing. The recovery decision is not "how do I rebuild my tiles." It is "does the rival have the army to land and take my island, or are they just trying to break my morale?" If the rival's army is at its cap and the landing is in progress, the island is still a fortress because the SAM spread and the Warship screen are still standing. If the rival's army is below cap, the MIRV was a bluff and the stalemate continues. The [winning overtime guide](/guides/winning-overtime/) covers the sinking overtime bar. The comeback is a game that you win by not losing, and the MIRV is the moment the rival has to decide whether they have the army to take the island or whether they have to keep waiting.

The practical recovery sequence after a landing attempt has failed is the same as the build order, just re-run. The SAM spread that took the hit has units down, and those units need to be rebuilt before the next attempt; the screen that turned the transport has taken damage and needs the Warship slot to fill in again; and the Port that funded the army is the first thing the rival will try to break in the next attempt, so the Port's tile needs protecting. In other words, the recovery is not a rebuild, it is a re-fortification. The rival's failed landing has cost them the Gold they spent on the transport and the Warships that came with it, and that is the second MIRV's window, because a rival who just failed a landing is now below the Gold line for a second attempt.

The decision that the post-MIRV moment forces is the same decision the whole comeback was about, only now it is about the rival's island. You have just spent your last MIRV on the rival, the rival's Silo is on cooldown, and the rival's army is at its cap on your coast. The question is not whether you can take the rival's island, it is whether you can hold your own. If the answer is yes, you hold the island and you let the overtime bar do the rest. If the answer is no, you have spent your closing move and the comeback has run out of fuel, and the correct play is the [stack vs spread guide](/guides/stack-vs-spread/), which covers the land war that replaces the sea war when both sides have spent their navy. The comeback wins when the last MIRV lands and the rival still has the Gold to fire one back. It loses when the last MIRV lands and the rival has spent the Gold that would have paid for it, and the only reason that Gold is still there is the SAM spread that made the rival's first strike a visible, aimed decision rather than a free hit.
