## Direct answer: what is the v0.35 new maps overview?

OpenFront v0.35 added twelve maps to the live pool, all carrying the official "new" rotation category in the v0.35.2 tag. They shipped across the v0.35.0, v0.35.1, and v0.35.2 releases, so the v0.35.2 tag source and its release notes are the only complete, verifiable list of the full set. Five are African regional maps: Bab el-Mandeb Strait, Canary Islands, Cape of Good Hope, Horn of Africa, and Madagascar. The other seven span Europe, Asia, South America, North America, Oceania, and two arcade themes: Benelux, Pulicat Lake, Rio de Janeiro, Vancouver Island, New Zealand, Brain Rot, and Ommatrypta.

Each map also carries its own `multiplayer_frequency` value, which controls how often it is drawn in public matchmaking, so the twelve maps are not all equally likely to appear in a given lobby.

For a player, the decision that matters is which of these you want to actually queue into, and which lobby settings will get you there, because the v0.35 lobby rework ties lobby size to the map and changes how often any given map comes up. On top of the new terrain, v0.35 also introduced a per-player starting-economy recovery that speeds up the first trade ship for the most-lagged player, both of which change how a new map feels in the first minutes. This guide walks through every map, how rotation works, what changes per map, and how to choose.

The overview matters because the twelve maps split into three distinct bands that play very differently. The arcade band, Brain Rot and Ommatrypta, carries by far the highest rotation weight and the densest nation fields, so it dominates public queue volume and rewards tempo and layered defense. The African regional band, five maps in all, is the team-oriented and modifier-heavy middle, where three maps carry a team frequency of 10 and one, Madagascar, forces 50 water nukes onto the board. The single-region band, the remaining five maps from Europe, Asia, South America, North America, and Oceania, is the quieter, rarer set that you are more likely to reach through a custom lobby than through the public rotation. If you only take one thing from this page, take that split: queue public lobbies to play the arcade maps, queue with a squad to reach the team-weighted African maps, and open a custom lobby when a specific single-region map is the goal. The sections below break each band into per-map detail, lay out the lobby and spawn changes that apply to all of them, and end with a decision framework and a list of the failure modes to watch for.

## The twelve maps at a glance: rotation weight and region

The single most useful fact about the new maps is how often each one is likely to appear. Every map's `multiplayer_frequency` value is its rotation weight for public lobbies; a higher number means the map is drawn more often. Brain Rot is the runaway leader at a frequency of 50, which is five times the next highest new map, Ommatrypta at 10, and ten times most of the regional maps, so Brain Rot is by a wide margin the map you will land on most in a public queue. Ommatrypta sits at 10, making the second arcade map a reliable pick too. The regional maps cluster in a much lower band from 3 to 5: Bab el-Mandeb Strait, Benelux, Horn of Africa, Madagascar, and New Zealand each sit at 5, Cape of Good Hope, Rio de Janeiro, and Vancouver Island at 4, and Canary Islands and Pulicat Lake at 3. None of the regional maps individually rivals the arcade pair, but as a group the five African maps occupy the middle of the new set and get steady public play without dominating the rotation. Three of those maps, Bab el-Mandeb Strait, Canary Islands, and Madagascar, also carry a `team_frequency` of 10, meaning they are additionally weighted toward team-oriented lobbies. The practical read is that the two arcade maps dominate casual queue volume, the African cluster is your middle ground, and the single-region maps like Vancouver Island and Pulicat Lake are rarer draws you should plan a custom or private lobby for if you want them specifically.

Benelux at 8 nations is by far the most compact, which suits shorter, tighter games. Pulicat Lake at 12 and Canary Islands at 14 are the next most contained. If you are coordinating a team and want a smaller, more controllable set of opponents, the low-nation maps give you more predictable matchups; if you want a wide field and longer strategic depth, the 26-to-43 nation maps reward patience and macro planning.

## How the v0.35 lobby rework changes map selection

The new maps are not just new terrain; they arrive alongside a rework of how lobbies are built and sized. In the v0.35 beta the maximum public lobby size became map-driven, and the old 125-player cap was removed, so the size of the room you queue into now reflects the map that will host the match rather than being fixed to a single global number. The delivered [lobby size, map driven guide](/guides/lobby-size-map-driven/) carries the per-map size data and the beta-only boundary, so this page keeps the map list and rotation logic as its focus. The immediate effect is that the maps with the highest nation counts, like Brain Rot at 43 and Ommatrypta at 34, can now fill more of their available nation slots before the lobby is full, which raises early-match tempo and the number of simultaneous frontlines. For the new maps this matters in two concrete ways. First, a high-frequency map like Brain Rot is more likely to reach a larger, more full population, which increases the tempo and the number of simultaneous frontlines. Second, the maps with a `team_frequency` value, specifically Bab el-Mandeb Strait, Canary Islands, and Madagascar, are additionally surfaced in team-oriented routing, so if you are queuing with a squad you are more likely to land on one of those three than on a solo-weighted map. You can still steer the outcome by choosing a custom or private lobby and naming the exact map, which bypasses the public rotation entirely and guarantees the layout you want. The tradeoff is that custom lobbies depend on other players filling in, so the public rotation is where the twelve new maps will most reliably appear for you in normal play.

The spawn system was also changed around these maps. The [v0.35 small-economy spawn boost guide](/guides/v035-small-economy-spawn-boost/) covers the exact mechanism, but the short version is that v0.35 added a per-player starting-economy recovery: when the most-lagged player has fewer open trade ships than the best-placed player, the most-lagged player's next trade ship timer is shortened for a fixed window, so an unlucky spawn no longer locks in a flat early deficit. The broader implication is that the "where you spawn" question, which has always been central to OpenFront, now interacts with "which map" as a second axis of early positioning, and the recovery narrows the gap between a good corner and a bad one on any of the twelve layouts. A spawn on a low-frequency, compact map like Benelux behaves very differently from the same spawn on a high-frequency, large map like Brain Rot, even if the local terrain looks similar, because the density of neighboring nations changes how fast the gap closes or widens. The net effect is that map choice is now a real early-game decision rather than a fixed variable you adapt to, and the rest of this guide is about reading the twelve options well enough to make that decision deliberately instead of by accident.

## Reading the regions: Africa, Europe, Asia, and the single-region maps

Five of the twelve maps are tagged to the African continent, which makes the region the most represented block of the new set. Bab el-Mandeb Strait sits at the strait between Africa and Asia and is the only new map with a dual Africa and Asia tag, reflecting its strait geography; it also carries two special-team nations and a team frequency of 10, which together make it the most team-friendly of the new regional maps. Canary Islands is a 14-nation island cluster in the Atlantic, with a low multiplayer frequency of 3 and a team frequency of 10, so it is a deliberate, smaller, team-oriented draw rather than a casual default. Cape of Good Hope is a 26-nation map around the southern African tip at a frequency of 4. Horn of Africa is a 29-nation map at a frequency of 5, one of the higher-weighted African draws. Madagascar is a 34-nation island map that pairs a frequency of 5 with a team frequency of 10, two special-team nations, and a forced modifier of 50 water nukes, which is the single most distinctive mechanic on any new map.

The remaining seven maps spread across the other regions and the arcade category. Benelux is an 8-nation European map, the most compact of the set and a good fit for short, high-attention games. Pulicat Lake is a 12-nation Asian map with two special-team nations and a low frequency of 3. Rio de Janeiro is a 30-nation South American map at a frequency of 4. Vancouver Island is a 32-nation North American map at a frequency of 4. New Zealand is a 20-nation Oceanian map at a frequency of 5 and is the only new map tagged to the Oceania region. Brain Rot and Ommatrypta are the two arcade maps: Brain Rot is a 43-nation space and war themed map with the highest rotation weight in the pool, and Ommatrypta is a 34-nation scary and fantasy themed arcade map at a frequency of 10. The practical split for a player is that the African block is where you will find the most team-oriented and modifier-heavy new terrain, the single-region maps are your quieter, rarer draws, and the two arcade maps are where the volume of public lobbies actually concentrates. Treat the region tag as a rough guide to tone: the real-world maps ask you to think about coastlines, island groups, and straits, while the arcade maps are built around theme and tempo rather than geography.

## The two arcade maps: Brain Rot and Ommatrypta

Brain Rot is the standout of the entire new set, and not just because of its name. It is an arcade-category map with a space and war theme, 43 nations, and a multiplayer frequency of 50. That frequency is five times the next highest new map and ten times the regional band that runs from 3 to 5, so Brain Rot is by a wide margin the most likely of the twelve to be the map you land on when you queue for a public lobby. The density of its 43-nation field compounds that: more nations means more simultaneous fronts, more early-trade and early-pressure decisions, and a faster climb toward the late-game nuke threshold. The map also uses a multi-layer structure in its data, which in practice reads as a busier, more layered terrain than the single-layer regional maps, and that layered layout rewards players who can hold depth and reposition rather than those who commit to a single coastline. Community reaction to Brain Rot has been consistently high-energy; the map's title appears verbatim in dedicated commentary, and the map is treated as the headline casual experience of the update. If you want the newest map to simply be the easiest to find and play, Brain Rot is the answer.

Ommatrypta is the second arcade map and sits in a clearly different niche. It is a scary and fantasy themed arcade map with 34 nations and a frequency of 10, which is the second-highest weight among the twelve but well below Brain Rot's 50. The lower frequency means Ommatrypta is a rarer public draw than Brain Rot, so if it is the one you want you are more likely to queue through it than to land on it by accident. Its fantasy and scary theme makes it the natural pick for players who want a non-realistic, high-flavor board rather than a recognizable real-world coastline, and its 34-nation field is dense enough to keep the match competitive without reaching Brain Rot's full crowding. Ommatrypta is also the map that community creators built content around for the Halloween window, so it carries the update's seasonal flavor even though it is a permanent addition to the arcade pool rather than a temporary event board. The two arcade maps together cover the casual end of the new set: Brain Rot for maximum volume and tempo, Ommatrypta for a distinct themed board with a steadier, slightly smaller field.

## Decision framework: which new map should you queue into?

The right map depends on what you want the match to be, and the v0.35 data lets you pick deliberately. If your goal is simply to play as much new content as possible with the least friction, queue public lobbies and lean on Brain Rot: its 50 frequency makes it the most probable draw, so you will hit new-map content more often than any other option. If you are playing with a squad and want a map that is both team-weighted and team-sized, the three maps with a `team_frequency` of 10 are your priority: Bab el-Mandeb Strait, Canary Islands, and Madagascar, because team-oriented routing will surface them more often when you queue with allies, and their nation counts (26, 14, and 34 respectively) give you a controllable opponent set. If you want a short, sharp, high-attention game, Benelux at 8 nations is the tightest new map and the fastest to reach a decisive end state. If you want a large, slow, macro-heavy game with a lot of room to maneuver, the 30-to-43 nation maps, Rio de Janeiro, Vancouver Island, Ommatrypta, and Brain Rot, reward long planning horizons and layered defense. If you want to test a specific mechanic, Madagascar is the one to pick because its forced 50 water nukes and special-team nations make it the most mechanically distinct board in the set.

A second axis is how you reach the map. The public rotation will favor the high-frequency maps, so Brain Rot and Ommatrypta will appear without any effort on your part, while the lower-frequency regional maps like Vancouver Island, Rio de Janeiro, Pulicat Lake, and Cape of Good Hope are rarer draws that you should not count on in a single queue. When a specific map matters, for a team practice session, a replay of a layout you already understand, or a test of a spawn strategy, build a custom or private lobby and set the map directly; that bypasses the rotation and guarantees the board. The spawn recovery introduced in v0.35 is worth factoring in on any new map before you commit to a spawn, because how far behind the leader your spawn sits on open trade ships determines how much the recovery shortens your next trade-ship timer, and that can meaningfully change the value of a coastline or a corner. The cleanest rule of thumb is: play public lobbies to discover the new maps and let the 50-frequency Brain Rot do most of the discovery for you, and switch to custom lobbies once you have a specific map and a specific plan you want to execute.

## Failure modes and how to counter them on the new maps

The most common failure on the new maps is queueing for a high-volume arcade map and then misreading the tempo. Brain Rot's 50 frequency and 43-nation field produce more early pressure and more frontlines than a typical regional map, so a player who opens with a slow, low-risk economic build is at a structural disadvantage: the larger field reaches the nuke and MIRV thresholds sooner, and the layered terrain means your coast is not as protected as it would be on a single-layer regional board. The counter is to front-load at least one port or factory in the first minutes and to lean on the v0.35 spawn recovery if you find yourself behind on open trade ships, so your economy can keep pace with a denser field rather than chasing it from behind. A second failure is picking a team-weighted map, Bab el-Mandeb Strait, Canary Islands, or Madagascar, without an actual team present; the `team_frequency` routing surfaces these maps to squads, so a solo player who lands on one is more likely to be outnumbered by organized groups. The counter is either to queue with allies or to treat those maps as higher-risk solo picks and play a more defensive, trade-focused line.

A third failure is underestimating Madagascar's forced modifiers. The map's 50 water nukes convert terrain in a way that changes naval and amphibious options, and its two special-team nations give specific positions a structural advantage that a generic opening ignores. If you queue Madagascar expecting a standard island map, you will be caught off guard by the water-nuke conversion and the special-team positions; the counter is to plan around the nuke modifier from the start, holding water access and treating the special-team positions as high-value objectives. A fourth failure is assuming a rare regional map will show up in a short session; Vancouver Island, Rio de Janeiro, Pulicat Lake, and Cape of Good Hope all sit at frequencies of 3 to 4, so waiting for them in public lobbies can waste a large fraction of a session. The counter is to use a custom or private lobby when a specific map is the goal. The final failure is ignoring the spawn axis entirely: the v0.35 per-player spawn recovery means the best spawn on a new map can be the one that pairs a strong local position with a small trade-ship deficit, and that combination changes the early-game value of a coastline in a way that older maps did not have.

## Mode and map adjustments, plus what to verify in the client

The v0.35 cycle pairs the new maps with two system changes you should account for before you judge any map. The first is the small-economy spawn recovery, which the [v0.35 small-economy spawn boost guide](/guides/v035-small-economy-spawn-boost/) breaks down: v0.35 shortens the next trade-ship timer for the most-lagged player for a fixed window whenever they have fewer open trade ships than the best-placed player, so an unlucky starting position no longer locks in a flat early deficit. When you open a new map, check how far behind the leader your spawn sits on open trade ships, because that single recovery can turn a marginal coastline into a viable opening or confirm that a corner is genuinely weak even with the recovery applied. The second is the map-driven lobby size, which the [lobby-size, map-driven guide](/guides/lobby-size-map-driven/) documents: in the v0.35 beta the old 125-player cap was removed and lobby size became tied to the map rather than a single global number. The map-driven size matters most on the high-nation maps, where Brain Rot at 43 and Ommatrypta at 34 can now fill more of their nation slots before the lobby caps, which raises the number of concurrent frontlines and the tempo of the opening. On the low-nation maps like Benelux at 8 and Pulicat Lake at 12, the map-driven size has less direct effect because the map fills its nation slots well before a large lobby cap is reached, so those games keep their tighter, more deliberate pacing. The honest boundary is that both changes were introduced and measured in the v0.35 beta, so treat the exact per-map numbers as beta-observed and verify the live client values before you plan a squad around them.
