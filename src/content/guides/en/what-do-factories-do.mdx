## Direct answer: what a factory actually does

A factory in OpenFront does **not** produce gold on its own — it **spawns trains**, and those trains are what collect gold, by stopping at a controlled City or Port within 110 tiles of rail. Build a factory with no connected, in-range paying station and it earns nothing: the Factory's own stop handler is a no-op in the v0.35.2 engine. The income is the network — factories as departure sources, rail as the road, and a City or Port as the paying stop. Spend 125,000 gold and about 20 ticks to build the first factory, wire it to a station you control, and each train that completes a paying stop returns 10,000 gold (your own station), 35,000 (an ally's), or 25,000 (a teammate's or another nation's), before a distance penalty. If that loop clears the shared 125k-to-1M cost ladder faster than the alternative, the factory engine is worth it; if you cannot commit a rail line to a paying station, put the gold into a Port instead, which pays on a schedule and never needs a rail at all.

The way to hold the whole guide in your head is the loop, not the building. A factory is the **source** of trains, rail is the **road**, and a city or port is the **paying stop**; the gold lives on the destination side of that loop, never on the factory tile itself. So the question you ask before spending the 125k is never "does this factory pay?" — it does not, by design — but "how many stations that actually pay sit inside this factory's 110-tile rail radius, and can I control them?" Count those stations, multiply by the per-stop payout, and compare the result against the next ladder rung you are about to spend. If the in-range, controlled, paying stations are few or out of reach, the factory is a furnace with no chimney and the Port is the safer income; if they are plentiful and in range, the factory is the multiplier that turns that rail network into compounding gold. This guide walks the spawn math, the payout math, the 110-tile range rule, and the decision in that order, and ends with a factory-versus-port-versus-city comparison, so you can see exactly where the gold comes from and where it does not before you spend your first 125k on a building that, on its own, will never hand you a single gold.

## The factory is a train factory, not a gold printer

The single most important thing to internalize: **the factory building itself pays nothing.** When you place a Factory, the engine attaches a `FactoryExecution` to it, and that execution creates a `TrainStationExecution` with the `spawnTrains` flag set — the Factory is the **only** unit in the game that does this. A City or a Port also becomes a train station (the engine builds the same `TrainStationExecution` for them), but it is constructed with `spawnTrains` off, so a City and a Port *receive* trains rather than *send* them. This is why the factory feels like a "departure" structure and the city or port feels like a "destination": one side of the rail loop is the engine room, the other side is the register.

Now look at what happens when a train actually arrives at a building. Every train station has a *stop handler* that decides whether the arrival produces a payout. In the v0.35.2 engine the factory's stop handler is a **no-op** — the code block that runs when a train settles at a factory is empty, so the train collects zero gold from the factory tile itself. The city's and the port's stop handler, by contrast, is the *trade* stop handler, and that handler is the one that calls into the `trainGold` payout function. So a train that round-trips factory-to-factory earns nothing, ever; the moment it touches a city or port that pays, it collects. This is the whole engine in one sentence: **factories launch the trains, cities and ports collect the money, and rail is the road that connects the two.** If a new player ever wonders "why isn't my factory making gold?", the answer is almost always that the train has nowhere to *pay* — it has a departure source but no destination that hands out gold. The fix is never "build more factories"; it is "give the trains a station that pays." That reframe — from *the building* to *the loop* — is the difference between a dead factory and a working economy, and every section that follows is just the math of that loop made explicit, so you can predict how much a particular layout will pay before you commit gold to it.

There is a second consequence of the split that new players miss until their first bad economy: because the factory is the *only* departure source, a factory that loses its rail link to every paying station does not become a slower printer, it becomes a literal zero. The spawn engine will keep launching trains into a cluster that leads nowhere, and every one of those trains burns gold you will never collect. That is why "I built a factory and it did nothing" is not a bug in the game — it is the exact prediction the loop model gives you when the departure side exists and the destination side does not. The practical test before you buy a factory is not "can I afford 125,000 gold" but "is there already a City or Port within 110 rail tiles that I can defend long enough for the trains to round-trip?" If that answer is no, the factory is capital you are paying to launch into the void, and the correct first purchase is the rail or the destination, not the departure.

## How the spawn engine actually works

A factory does not sit idle until you click "send train". It spawns trains on a continuous, probabilistic loop that runs every engine tick. Two gates control every spawn. The first is a **cooldown**: each train-station node carries a `ticksCooldown` of 10 ticks, and one engine tick is 100 milliseconds, so a single factory cannot launch two trains in under 1 second. This is the hard physical limit on how fast one node can fire. The second gate is the **spawn rate**, computed by the `trainSpawnRate` function as `(your factory count + 10) × 15`, then divided by two dampening terms — global train saturation and the small-economy boost — and finally floored at 1. The result is a probability *denominator*, not a timer. Each tick, the engine makes **one independent check per factory level you own**, where each check succeeds with probability 1 over that denominator, and the node fires the moment any one check passes. A 1-level factory therefore gets exactly one roll per tick at 1 in `(1+10)*15`, while a 10-level factory gets 10 rolls per tick at 1 in `(10+10)*15`, so higher-level factories both improve the odds of each roll *and* take more rolls, which is why upgrading a factory makes its trains arrive noticeably more often.

Put the numbers together and the pace becomes concrete. With no global damping in play, a 10-level factory sits at `(10+10)*15 = 300` before the boost is applied; the small-economy boost is 1.0 at 10 levels, so the effective denominator is 300, meaning roughly one successful roll per 300 ticks, or one spawn per 30 seconds *per level* — and because a 10-level factory takes 10 rolls per tick, the expected wait drops to about 3 seconds at a single node. Spread that across, say, five connected factory nodes each running the same loop, and the combined network is launching a train every fraction of a second. Two subtleties matter here. First, the "factories" term in the formula is the **total factory levels you own across all your factory buildings**, not the number of factory buildings, so stacking levels on one node and spreading levels across many nodes both feed the same denominator, but only nodes that have rail to a paying station actually convert the spawn into gold. Second, the spawn check happens *before* the cooldown is consulted in the code, so a node that has no destination in its rail cluster simply never spends a roll — it waits, for free, until a paying station appears in range. That is why an early factory on a fresh map can feel "on" (it rolls constantly) yet still produce zero gold: the rolls are happening, but there is no cluster destination to send the train to, so `spawnTrain` bails out before a train ever materializes. The spawn engine is always warm; it is the destination that decides whether it pays.

## Why the factory pays nothing and the station does

Let me separate the two payout paths cleanly, because the confusion here is the number-one beginner trap. A train has exactly two things that can make it earn gold during its life: the **station it visits**, and the **relationship between you and the station's owner**. The factory is a train station, but its stop handler does nothing, so the factory contributes zero to either input. The city and the port use the trade stop handler, and that handler is where the `trainGold` function is called, passing in the relationship and how many stops the train has already made. So the factory is *structurally* a station (it connects to rail, it can host a train), but it is *economically* invisible — it is the engine room, not the till.

The `trainGold` function is short and worth memorizing. It takes the relationship — your own station, a teammate's station, an ally's station, or some other nation's station — and assigns a base payout: **10,000 gold for your own station, 35,000 for an ally's station, and 25,000 for a teammate's or another nation's station.** Then it applies a distance penalty: the first 10 stops on the train's route cost nothing, but every stop *beyond* the 10th subtracts 5,000 gold, and the payout is clamped at a floor of 5,000 so a very long route never goes negative. Finally the result is multiplied by a global gold multiplier. Read that back and the strategy is obvious. Your own stations are the *cheapest* paying stop (10k) but they are also the ones you control and can defend; an ally's station pays the most (35k) because the alliance bonus is real, which is why a friendly station on a shared rail line is worth far more than a lone station of your own. And the distance penalty quietly tells you that a long, winding train loop is not automatically better than a short, tight one — past 10 stops you start losing 5k per stop, so a 20-stop tour is paying only 5k on the last stop. The practical read: **the factory is the source, the station is the destination, and the money is on the destination's side of the rail.** A factory with no rail to a station is a furnace with no chimney; a station with no rail from a factory is a till with no customers. The factory *looks* like the money structure because it is the one you actively build and upgrade, but the engine is the pair, and the payout lives in the city or port, not in the factory. If you are deciding whether a new factory is worth its 125k, the question is never "does this factory pay?" — it does not — but "does this factory reach a station that pays, often enough, to cover the cost?"

## The 110-tile rail requirement

A factory only matters if a train can reach a paying station, and reach is measured in rail tiles, not straight-line distance. The engine caps the distance at which one train-station node can search for another at **110 tiles** — that is the `trainStationMaxRange` in the v0.35.2 config — and the diagonal rail length a single line can span is **155.56 tiles** (110 × √2). A minimum of 15 tiles is the floor below which two nodes are considered the same point and are not a meaningful route. These are not soft suggestions; a train physically cannot be routed beyond them, so a factory 130 tiles of rail from your only paying city is simply *not connected*, and its spawn rolls never produce a gold-earning train no matter how high you level the factory. This is the most common silent failure mode: a player builds the factory, levels it, watches trains spawn in their head, and gets nothing, because the rail line to the nearest paying station exceeds the 110-tile search radius and the cluster never forms.

The number that actually drives layout is the 110-tile search radius, because that is the radius within which the engine looks for a destination before a node can even consider spawning. Think of it as the "service area" of a factory: any paying city or port inside that circle, linked by rail under the 155.56 diagonal cap, is reachable; anything outside it is out of range. Two practical consequences follow. First, **placement matters more than level.** A single factory placed at the center of a cluster of four in-range cities will out-earn a stack of max-level factories placed 120 tiles from any station, because the central factory's trains actually complete paying stops while the distant ones spawn into empty rail. Second, **you can extend reach by building intermediate nodes**, but only up to the cap: if your paying city is 140 tiles of rail from the factory, you are already past the 110 search radius, so no amount of extra factory levels fixes it — you need to move the factory closer or build a paying station closer to the factory. The 155.56 diagonal cap is the ceiling on a single straight rail segment; beyond it you need an intermediate node to hop the connection. So when you are deciding where to drop a new factory, do not look at "how much gold do I have to level it?" — look at "how many paying stations sit within 110 tiles of rail of this tile?" That count, times the per-stop payout, is the real value of the factory; the level only scales how often the train arrives. A factory with three in-range paying stations and a level-2 upgrade is a far better investment than a max-level factory with zero in-range stations, and the 110-tile rule is the test that tells you which one you are building.

## The cost ladder and build time

Factories, ports, and cities all share **one** cost ladder, and understanding the sharing is half the decision. The level-1 cost for each is **125,000 gold**, and the ladder climbs as `min(1,000,000, 2^n × 125,000)` where `n` is the shared level index — the port's `n` and the factory's `n` are the same counter in the engine, so a port at level 2 and a factory at level 2 both cost 250,000, level 3 both cost 500,000, and the ladder reaches its 1,000,000 cap at level 4 and stays flat from level 5 on. The build *time* differs by structure: a factory and a city each take **20 ticks** (2 seconds of build) to complete, while a port takes **50 ticks** (5 seconds). That 30-tick gap is real and strategic. Because the factory is cheap to build *fast* and the port is cheap to build *slow*, a port commit is a slower burn of gold but does not depend on any rail at all, whereas a factory commit is a faster burn that only pays if the rail is already in place. You are not buying "a factory" or "a port" in isolation; you are buying a *slot* on the shared ladder, and the slot's price is the same whether you spend it on the rail-dependent factory or the schedule-based port.

The cap at 1,000,000 is the second thing to notice. Because the ladder tops out, a level-4 (1M) factory is not 16× the price of a level-1 (125k) factory; it is the final rung. That flattening is what makes late-game factory investment affordable at all — the marginal cost of the highest levels is bounded, so a committed factory engine has a *known* total cost you can plan against, rather than a cost that doubles without limit. Concretely, going from a level-1 factory to the level-4 cap costs 125k + 250k + 500k + 1M = 1,875,000 gold of cumulative ladder spend for the factory side (the port side, if you run both, spends the same rungs). That is a meaningful but *finite* commitment, and it is the number you should compare against the per-stop payout of your rail loop to decide whether the engine clears the ladder in the time the game allows. The build-time asymmetry adds a timing layer: because the factory builds in 20 ticks versus the port's 50, you can drop a factory mid-fight and have it spawning almost immediately, while a port commit takes longer to come online. For a player deciding under pressure, the factory is the fast option and the port is the slow option, and the 110-tile rail requirement is the condition that makes the fast option *work*. The cost ladder tells you *how much*; the build time tells you *how soon*; the rail range tells you *whether it pays at all*. Stack those three and you have the full investment picture before you spend a single gold, which is exactly the decision framework the next two sections turn into concrete numbers.

## Decision framework and two worked scenarios

Turn the three inputs — cost, build time, rail range — into a rule, then stress it with two concrete layouts. The rule: **a factory is worth its 125k when the number of paying stations inside its 110-tile rail radius, times the per-stop payout, times the spawn frequency at your factory level, covers the next ladder rung before the route becomes contested.** Everything else is either a port (if the rail is not there yet) or a pass (if the stations are not in range or not controlled).

**Scenario A — the central cluster (build the factory).** You have four level-1 cities inside 90 tiles of rail of a fresh factory tile, all yours, and your factory is level 1. The per-stop payout to your own station is 10,000 gold, and the first 10 stops on any route carry no penalty, so a train that loops through all four cities is earning 40,000 per full loop before the distance penalty even touches it. At level 1 the spawn denominator is `(1+10)*15 = 165` before boost; with the small-economy boost at level 1 (1.5×) the effective denominator is 110, so the factory rolls at roughly 1 in 110 per tick, one roll per tick (level 1 = one level). That is a train every ~11 seconds at this single node, each worth up to 40k, which is 400k of potential per minute — enough to recover the 125k factory cost in under 20 seconds of clean loops and then keep compounding. The answer here is unambiguous: **build the factory, and upgrade the cities, not just the factory**, because the cities are the paying stations and the factory is only the launcher.

**Scenario B — the orphan factory (build the port instead).** You have a factory level 1 sitting 130 tiles of rail from your only city, and no other station in range. The factory rolls constantly (the spawn engine is always warm) but the cluster has no in-range destination, so `spawnTrain` bails before a single train exists; the factory's own stop handler is a no-op, so even the hypothetical train that spawned and settled on the factory pays zero. The 130-tile gap is past the 110 search radius, so no factory level fixes it — you would have to move the factory or build a station closer. In this layout the correct move is not to level the orphan; it is to spend the 125k on a **port** at the city, which pays on a schedule, needs no rail, and comes online in 50 ticks, or to build a *new* paying station inside 110 tiles of the existing factory so the orphan finally has a destination. The contrast is the whole framework in one breath: scenario A has in-range, controlled, paying stations, so the factory is the engine; scenario B has none, so the factory is a dead furnace and the port (or a relocated station) is the actual income. Decide by the 110-tile count, not by the factory icon — the icon is the cheap, fast launcher, and it only becomes an economy when the rail puts a paying station in its service area.

## Failure modes and how to counter them

Most factory failures are not "the factory is broken"; they are a missing or misplaced *link* in the loop. The first failure mode is the **orphan factory**: a factory with no paying station inside its 110-tile rail radius. Symptom is "trains should be spawning but I see no gold." Counter: do not level the orphan; either move the factory within 110 tiles of a station you control, or build a new station inside the factory's service area. A port at the nearest city is the fastest fix if the city is reachable, because the port pays without any rail. The second failure mode is the **long-route penalty**: a train loop that visits more than 10 cities before returning, so every stop past the 10th loses 5,000 gold and the final stops pay only the 5k floor. Symptom is "my loop visits a lot of cities but the gold per train feels low." Counter: tighten the loop so the high-payout stations (your own 10k and ally 35k) come *before* the 10-stop mark, and drop low-value stops from the middle of the route; the penalty is linear past 10, so a 15-stop loop is not worth 50% more than a 10-stop loop — it is worth less per stop.

The third failure mode is the **uncontrolled destination**: the in-range station belongs to an enemy, so the train either does not reach it or, if the engine routes to an ally's or other nation's station, the payout relationship changes. Symptom is "the math works but the station keeps getting captured." Counter: the 35k ally payout is the highest rate in the game, so an *allied* station on your rail line is worth protecting and prioritizing over your own 10k station when you can only defend one; a 25k "other" station is a fallback, not a goal. The fourth failure mode is **leveling before the rail is done**: spending the 250k/500k/1M ladder rungs on a factory that still has no in-range destination, which locks gold into a dead node while the game moves on. Counter: level the factory *after* the rail and at least one controlled station are in place; the levels multiply the spawn rate, but a spawn with no destination is worth zero, so the levels only become gold once the cluster exists. The fifth failure mode is **ignoring the global saturation**: in a large lobby the world train fleet grows, and the `trainSaturation` term raises the spawn denominator for *everyone*, so your per-train frequency drops as the map fills with trains. Counter: saturation is a slow, global effect, not a per-node bug — if your factory "slowed down" in the late game with no local change, the likely cause is the world fleet passing the damping midpoint, and the fix is to add *more paying stations* (more gold per train) rather than more factory levels (more trains, each paying less as saturation climbs). Every one of these failures is the same underlying loop missing one link, and every counter is "put the missing link back" — the destination, the short route, the controlled station, the finished rail, or the added station — rather than "build a bigger factory."

## Factory vs Port vs City, and when to reach for each

The cleanest way to end the confusion is to compare the three stations that make up the train economy side by side, because they share the cost ladder but differ in what they do to gold. The table below captures the v0.35.2 numbers and the decision rule that follows from them.

| Structure | Level-1 cost | Build time | Role in the train economy | Payout role | Needs rail to pay? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Factory | 125,000 | 20 ticks | **Spawns** the trains (only unit that does) | Its own stop handler is a **no-op** — pays 0 | Yes — needs a paying station within 110 tiles |
| Port | 125,000 | 50 ticks | Receives trains; also runs the trade-ship economy | **Pays** via the trade stop handler | No — pays on a schedule, rail optional |
| City | 125,000 | 20 ticks | Receives trains; holds troops and territory | **Pays** via the trade stop handler; +250k troop cap per level | No — it is the destination, not the source |

Read the table and the decision logic writes itself. The **factory** is the only *source*: it is where the trains come from, so it is the structure you build to *start* an engine, but it is also the one structure that pays nothing on its own tile, so it is useless without a destination. The **port** and the **city** are the *destinations*: both run the trade stop handler that actually calls `trainGold`, so both turn a passing train into gold, and neither requires rail to pay its base income — a port pays on its trade-ship schedule and a city earns from its territory and troop presence even before any train arrives. That asymmetry is the entire choice. If you have rail to a controlled station and want to scale train income, the factory is the lever, and you level it to raise spawn frequency; if you do not have rail yet, or the stations are out of range, the port and the city are the safe income, because they pay without depending on a connected loop. The shared cost ladder means you are never forced to pick one over the other on price — they cost the same at every rung — so the pick is purely on *mechanics*: factory for the fast, rail-dependent engine, port for the slow, rail-independent schedule, city for the destination that also holds the front line. A mature economy runs all three in a loop: cities and ports as the paying stations, a factory in the center of them as the launcher, and rail as the road, with the 110-tile radius as the test that the loop actually closes. When in doubt, the port is the fallback that never goes wrong, and the factory is the multiplier that pays off once the loop is in place.

## Version notes and where to go next

Everything in this guide is bound to the **formal v0.35.2 tag** of the OpenFrontIO engine, and the numbers above were re-derived from the tag source rather than from community write-ups, which is important because the train economy is the part of the game that changes most between releases. The spawn formula `(factories+10)×15`, the 10-tick (1 second) cooldown, the `trainGold` mapping of 10k/25k/35k with the 5k-per-stop penalty after 10 stops and the 5k floor, the 110-tile search radius and 155.56 diagonal rail cap, the 125k shared cost ladder capped at 1,000,000, and the 20-tick factory build against the 50-tick port build are all the v0.35.2 values, and any of them can shift in a later patch. A few community sources describe an older or simplified version of the spawn — for example a phrasing that treats the denominator as a literal timer, or a payout table with the team and ally rates swapped — and those should be treated as folklore until re-checked against the tag, which is exactly the re-derivation this guide did. If you move to a newer release, re-read `EngineConfig.ts` (the `trainSpawnRate`, `trainSaturation`, `smallEconomySpawnBoost`, and `trainGold` functions) and `Config.ts` (the `trainStationMaxRange`) before reusing any number here, because the damping constants in particular are tuned per release and are the ones most likely to move.

The single most useful mental model to keep from this guide is that **a factory is a launcher, not a cash register**: it earns zero on its own tile, it needs a paying station within 110 tiles of rail, and its value is the per-stop payout of the stations it can reach, multiplied by how often its trains arrive at your factory level. Once that model is locked, the rest of the train economy is arithmetic you can do on a map: count the in-range stations, multiply by 10k/25k/35k, factor the 10-stop penalty, and compare against the 125k-to-1M ladder you are about to spend. For the deeper optimization of an already-working engine — routing, cluster layout, and how to maximize the per-train payout once the loop closes — read the [train network guide](/guides/train-network/), which covers the advanced layout math. If your real question is "factory or port, given my gold and my map," the [port vs factory guide](/guides/port-vs-factory/) walks the payback comparison directly, and the [in-game gold rates guide](/guides/in-game-gold-rates/) lists the exact payout tables the engine uses so you can verify any number in this guide against the live data. Start with the 110-tile test, build the station before you build the factory that serves it, and let the loop — not the icon — be the reason you spend the gold.
