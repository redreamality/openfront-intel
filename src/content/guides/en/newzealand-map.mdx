**Direct answer.** New Zealand is the v0.35 mid-tier Oceania board: 20 Māori iwi nations plus 63 region entities on 772,373 land tiles, a 1,888 by 3,448 grid. It is a sparse-rotation (frequency 5) map you reach in a custom lobby, a 40-player free-for-all base where expansion room beats a coastline, and — since the v0.35.5 two-team split (2026-10-26) — a frontline map whose two teams open at opposite edges and fight for the center.

## What New Zealand actually is on the board

The clearest way to describe New Zealand is as a medium regional map, not a world map and not an arcade map, and that distinction drives every choice that follows. The grid is 1,888 tiles wide and 3,448 tiles tall, a portrait rectangle, and the playable land is 772,373 land tiles. That land figure puts it squarely in the middle of the v0.35 set: it is far larger than the tiny single-layer regional maps and far smaller than the world map, so a full game covers a board you can hold in your head without losing track of where your neighbors are. The theme is Oceania, and the map is the only new v0.35 map that carries the Oceania region tag, which is why it is the natural home for players who specifically want to play from the New Zealand starting geography rather than from a generic coastline or a continental landmass. The map is single-layer in its tile data, meaning the terrain does not use the stacked multi-layer structure that the arcade maps use, so the board reads as open terrain with a coastline rather than a busy layered maze. That single-layer, open-terrain layout is the reason the map rewards players who expand and screen rather than players who rely on dense layered defense, and it is also the reason the spawn decisions in the next few sections matter more here than they do on a multi-layer map, where a single layer of walls can absorb an early push that New Zealand has no equivalent of. If you have played the world map and want a smaller, themed slice of the same idea, New Zealand is that slice: same ocean-and-land logic, compressed into a board you can actually track, and wrapped around a Māori tribal identity that none of the other new maps use, so the flavor of the board is doing real work on top of the raw geometry. The portrait shape also matters more than it first appears. Because the board is nearly twice as tall as it is wide, the two long sides run along the 3,448-tile direction and the two short sides run along the 1,888-tile direction, and that asymmetry is what the two-team split exploits later, so you should keep the orientation in your head from the start. There is also a third consequence of the portrait shape that is easy to miss: because the board is tall, a team that commits to one short side of the grid has a shorter front to defend than a team that is spread along a long side, which is part of why the two-team split puts the spawn areas at the long edges rather than the short ones, and it is also part of why a squad that bunches on one corner is easier to break than a squad that spreads its front across the long side.

## The nation layer: twenty iwi and sixty-three regions

The defining mechanic of New Zealand is the way the engine populates the nation field, and it works in two layers that you need to keep separate. The manifest carries two distinct arrays. The first is a list of exactly twenty named nations, and in the delivered map those twenty are the traditional Māori tribes, the iwi. This is a deliberate design choice and it is the single most important thing to understand before you play the map, because it changes what a "nation" feels like to you on the board. On most maps a nation is a generic territory you conquer, a shape on the map that has no identity beyond its borders; on New Zealand the named national layer is a cultural layer of recognizable tribal names, so the early-game read of who is your neighbor is different from a standard board, and you are not just counting territories, you are reading a field of named entities that carry meaning. The second array is a list of sixty-three additional region and island entities. These are not full nations in the same sense; they are the engine's overflow population that fills the rest of the field so the board is not twenty isolated points sitting on a mostly empty map. Together the two layers name eighty-three places, and the engine draws from them to seed the match. The practical consequence is that a full New Zealand game is denser in named places than a twenty-nation arcade map, even though the core national layer is small. You will see iwi names and regional names mixed across the board, and the way they seed determines your opening neighbors. Treat the twenty iwi as the named anchors, the places that define the national layer, and the sixty-three regions as the connective tissue that makes the board feel populated, and your map read will match what is actually on the screen instead of the smaller number you would expect from a twenty-nation count. The reason the distinction matters is that a single number, eighty-three, hides two different roles. The iwi are the national anchors with a defined identity and a defined expansion, while the regions are the filler that keeps the board from feeling empty, so when you are deciding how to read your neighbors you are working with two different kinds of entity at once, and the density of the field is higher than the national count alone would suggest.

## Where to spawn on New Zealand

Spawn choice is where the community discussion is most consistent, and the advice transfers directly to New Zealand rather than being something you have to re-derive from scratch. The dominant read across the spawn threads is that you should not hug the coastline. A coastline spawn looks safe because the water blocks one side, but it traps you against that water and gives you only one direction to expand into, which is exactly the opposite of what an early game needs, because the early game is about opening up room, not about protecting a single edge. The advice that repeats across the threads is that the most important property of a spawn is ample room to expand: you want open land in front of you so your first few trade ships and your first expansion are not immediately contested by a neighbor who has already seen the same lane. The second consistent read is that spawning near nations is a real advantage, not a liability. Being adjacent to another player's starting position gives you immediate trade and pressure options and shortens the distance to your first meaningful decision, so the threads describe it almost as a built-in advantage, and a spawn that feels isolated is usually a spawn that is costing you more than it is protecting you. The third read is about alliances: expand aggressively at the start and take strategic alliances, but do not ally with every neighbor, because a player who allies too wide early hands their neighbors a coalition they cannot fight, and the board then has no direction left to press. On New Zealand specifically, the open single-layer terrain means expansion room is the scarcest resource on the board, so a spawn with a clear lane of land to the interior beats a spawn with a pretty coastline every time, and the heat-map analysis of thousands of free-for-all games supports the same conclusion: win rate tracks expansion room and nearby nations more than it tracks coastline proximity. So spend your spawn selection on the direction you will grow into, not on the water you will be pressed against, and the coastline becomes a fallback you retreat to rather than a home you commit to. There is a further reason the interior beats the coast on this specific board: because the land is single-layer and open, a coastline spawn on New Zealand has the water on one side and often a neighbor on the other, which means your only open direction is the interior, and if that interior lane is already contested, a coastline spawn has effectively no expansion left, so the water you were hiding behind has cost you the whole game, which is why the threads are so consistent that the coast is a fallback, not a home.

## The 40-player free-for-all base

The base population a New Zealand game tends to hold is a 40-player free-for-all, and that number is the frame for most of the map's balance, so it is worth understanding why it lands where it does. The manifest's nation math works out to a sparse field: with the twenty iwi as the national layer and a multiplayer frequency of 5, the board supports a modest population relative to its land, so a 40-player match is a natural, well-populated size for this board rather than an overfull one, and the board is never crowded the way the arcade maps get. A community playthrough that is specifically a 40-player free-for-all on New Zealand is the most direct evidence of how the map is actually played, and the player's first observation is that the map is unfamiliar enough that the early spawn and the early neighbors decide a lot of the outcome, which is the opposite of a board where the opening is a formality and the game really begins after the first expansion wave. At 40 players the board is dense enough that you have several live neighbors in every direction, but sparse enough that no single frontline is a permanent wall, so the game is about picking your expansion lane and holding it rather than about surviving a crush of attacks from every side. The practical read for a 40-player New Zealand lobby is that you should be making an expansion decision in the first minutes and committing to it, because with forty live players the window where your opening position is recoverable is short, and by the time you realize a lane is closed, the neighbor who took it has already started to build on it. If your spawn has a clear interior lane, take it and grow into it before a neighbor closes the gap; if your spawn is pinched between two neighbors, the 40-player pressure means you should be planning a trade or an early alliance to buy room, not waiting for the board to open up on its own, because at this population density the board does not open up, it only rearranges. The reason 40 is the number to plan around is that it is the size where the map's own land-to-population ratio produces a board that is neither overpopulated nor hollow, and the density is high enough that every one of your neighbors is a live threat or a live trade partner.

## The two-team split added in v0.35.5

The most important version boundary on New Zealand is the two-team spawn configuration, and it landed in v0.35.5 on 2026-10-26, which is after the map first shipped in v0.35.0. That timing matters because it means the map's solo free-for-all behavior and its team behavior are not identical: the team split reshapes where the two sides begin and how the front between them forms, so you are reading two different versions of the same map depending on the mode you queue. The v0.35.5 release notes name New Zealand explicitly as one of the maps that received two-team spawns, alongside Madagascar, Rio de Janeiro, and Bab el-Mandeb Strait, so this is a deliberate, announced change rather than a side effect of a shared code path, and the fact that it is called out by name in the release is the signal that the change is real and intended rather than an accident you have to work around. On the board, the two-team configuration places the two spawn areas at opposite long edges of the portrait grid, so a team game on New Zealand opens with the two sides separated by the full width of the land and the frontline developing down the middle of the board, with both teams pushing toward the center from their respective edges. For a squad, that separation is the feature: you get a defined front, you get a shared expansion direction, and you get a cleaner read on where the enemy team will press than you get in a chaotic free-for-all, because the enemy is not a hundred independent players, it is a team with a front. The boundary you should hold in your head is the version date. If you are watching a playthrough from before 2026-10-26, the team behavior it shows is not the delivered behavior, and the two-team split you are reading about here is what is in the game now, and it is the version you should plan a squad around rather than the solo behavior you would see in older footage. If a lobby is set to two teams, the front is the unit of analysis for the whole game, and the rest of this guide assumes you are reading the team version whenever it is a team lobby. The practical upshot is that a New Zealand team lobby is a different game from a New Zealand solo lobby even though the tiles are the same: the same board, the same spawn edges, and the same nation field are all present in both, but the mode changes which of them is the object of play, and a player who reads the board correctly will always know which mode they are in before the first trade, because the mode is visible in the lobby setup and the front that forms in the first minute is the visible consequence of that choice.

## Team play: reading the front and holding the middle

When you queue New Zealand as part of a squad, the two-team split turns the map into a frontline map rather than a free-for-all map, and the play that wins a team game is the play that holds the middle of the board, because the middle is where the two edges meet and where the outcome of the game is decided. Because the two spawn areas sit at opposite long edges of the portrait grid, the opening is a race to establish the first contested line down the center, and the team that anchors the middle first controls where the rest of the game is fought, which means the team that wins the center is the team that gets to fight on its own terms for the rest of the match. That makes the middle the most valuable real estate on the map in a team lobby, and it changes the spawn advice for a squad: you are not optimizing for the widest personal expansion lane the way you are in a free-for-all, you are optimizing for a spawn that lets your team form a coherent front quickly, and a personal spawn that is spacious but isolated from your team's development is a worse pick than a personal spawn that is close to your teammates even if it is narrower. A spawn near your teammates with a clear lane toward the center is worth more than a spawn that is personally spacious but isolated from your team's development, because the team's front is the thing you are actually protecting, not your own personal border. The second team read is about trade and pressure. In a two-team game your neighbors on your own side are your teammates, so the early trade lines that a free-for-all treats as risky become free information and support, and the pressure you apply should be aimed at the enemy front rather than at your own flank, because pressuring your own flank is pressuring your own team. The failure mode to avoid is the squad that treats a team lobby like a free-for-all and starts ganking its own side or expanding selfishly away from the center, because on a board where the two teams are separated by the full width of the land, internal fighting gives the enemy team the open middle for free, and the enemy team will take it the moment it appears. The deeper point is that the middle on New Zealand is not a line you defend with a single player, it is the shared asset of the whole squad, and the squad that treats it as shared, with every member understanding the front is theirs and not just theirs, is the squad that holds it, while the squad that treats it as someone else's problem loses it the first time the enemy team tests it.

## Failure modes and the counters that work

The most common way players lose on New Zealand is the same as the most common way they lose on any open-terrain map: they spend the first ten minutes defending a coastline instead of expanding, and by the time they turn to grow, a neighbor has already closed their only good lane, and the game is now a defensive war on a board that was designed for expansion. The counter is the spawn discipline from the community threads, which is to choose the interior lane first and treat the coastline as a fallback, not a home, and to commit to the expansion direction in the first minutes rather than the first ten. The second failure mode is over-alliancing in a free-for-all, where a player allies with every neighbor and then cannot find a direction to press because every direction is a treaty, and the board has no purpose for the alliances and no direction for the pressure. The counter is to take a small number of strategic alliances, enough to protect your expansion lane, and keep at least one direction open for pressure so the alliances have a purpose, because an alliance with no direction behind it is just a border with a friendly face on it. The third failure mode is specific to team play and comes from the two-team split: a squad that lets its front fragment, with individual players pushing deep while the middle goes soft, and the enemy team threads the gap through the center of the board, and the game is over before anyone realizes the center was abandoned. The counter is to hold the middle as a team decision, not as an individual one, and to trade the center deliberately so the enemy cannot pick where the front breaks, because a center that trades on your terms is a center you keep. The fourth failure mode is misreading the version: a player who plans their squad on the solo free-for-all behavior and walks into a two-team board, and then plays the team game with free-for-all instincts, expanding selfishly and ignoring the front. The counter is the version boundary, which is to confirm the lobby is a two-team lobby and read the board as a frontline map before you commit to a play, because the two behaviors reward different decisions in the first five minutes of the game, and the wrong instinct in those five minutes sets the tone for the whole match. The fifth failure mode is the population trap: a player who expects a 40-player board to be crowded and plays for a survival game it does not produce, when the map's own density means the game is about the lane, not about the crush, and the counter is to read the density correctly and play the lane.

## How New Zealand fits into the v0.35 map set

New Zealand occupies a specific slot in the v0.35 map set, and understanding that slot tells you when to pick it and when to pick something else, because the map is not the right tool for every kind of player or every kind of lobby. The twelve new maps split into three bands: the arcade band with the highest rotation weight and the densest nation fields, the African regional band that is team-oriented and modifier-heavy, and the single-region band that runs from Europe, Asia, South America, North America, and Oceania. New Zealand is in that third, quieter band, and it is the only map in the whole set that carries the Oceania region tag, which makes it the map you reach when you specifically want a medium-sized, single-region, culturally themed board, and it is the map you are least likely to land on by accident in a public queue because its multiplayer frequency of 5 puts it in the sparse rotation alongside the other single-region maps. The practical implication is that New Zealand is a custom-lobby map more than a queue map: if you want to play it, open a custom or private lobby and name it directly, which bypasses the public rotation and guarantees the layout, and the fact that it is sparse in the queue is not a flaw of the map, it is a consequence of the rotation weight the map was given. Within its own slot, New Zealand is the mid-tier option: the world map is the giant thematic board for players who want the full scope, the arcade maps are the fast dense boards for players who want the rotation weight, and New Zealand is the middle where the board is big enough to matter, small enough to track, and themed enough to feel different from a standard regional map, so it is the choice for the player who wants substance without the full scope of the world map. The map also sits between the arcade band and the African band in terms of team support, because the two-team split gives it a team identity it would not otherwise have, and the frequency keeps it out of the arcade crowd. If you are choosing between New Zealand and the other mid-tier single-region maps, the deciding factor is the theme and the team split, because the raw geometry is comparable to the other single-region boards and the iwi layer and the two-team split are what make New Zealand its own board rather than a generic medium map. The decision that New Zealand is asking you to make is simple: do you want a medium, themed, Oceania board you can actually track, and are you willing to reach for it in a custom lobby rather than wait for the public rotation? If the answer to both is yes, New Zealand is the map for you, and the rest of this guide is the play. If you want the highest-probability map in a public queue, the arcade band has the rotation weight and New Zealand will not be it, so you are waiting for a map that is not the one you are here to read about. If you want the biggest board, the world map is the choice, and if you want the densest nation field, the arcade maps are the choice, and New Zealand is neither, so it is the choice for the player in the middle who wants a board with an identity and a team mode.
