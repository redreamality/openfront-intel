**Direct answer.** Your stats live in three places: your in-game account (top-right, then "games"), the official public ranked leaderboard, and the third-party sites OFstats, Trackerfront, and OFratings. The numbers that change a decision are the ranked Elo and its per-game swing, the FFA tier rating with its placement, and your raw wins/losses/total normalized by mode and lobby size. Ignore level, prestige, and legend for skill; they are cosmetic progression. Read the swing, not the win/loss line, and log into a third-party site so it is your stats and not a player with your name.

## Where your stats actually live: the three surfaces that hold your numbers

Before you can read any number, you need to know where it lives, because OpenFront spreads your record across three different surfaces that each answer a different question. The first is your in-game account. When you are signed in, the account control sits in the top-right of the screen; open it and you reach your games list, which is the raw per-match record. Every match you played appears there with its mode, your name and clan tag, and whether you won, lost, or left it incomplete. Two controls sit at the top-right of each match entry: one opens that match's leaderboard, the other opens the replay. This surface is the only place that tells you what happened in a specific game, so it is where you go when a number looked wrong and you need the match context behind it.

The second surface is the official public ranked leaderboard, which is the canonical home for the top ranked numbers. It is a public endpoint that the community points at when they ask how to see top-50 ranked stats, and it is the same source that the site itself documents in its API reference. Unlike your games list, it does not hold your full history; it holds a ranked snapshot of the strongest players per ladder, refreshed roughly every fifteen minutes. That refresh cadence is the single most important fact to internalize, because it means a match you just finished will not appear here for a short while, and a number that "should have moved" is often simply not there yet.

The third surface is the third-party stat layer, and in the OpenFront community this means OFstats, Trackerfront, and OFratings. These are independent sites that index matches and give you richer views than the in-game list: match history, conquests, nukes, gold earned, and per-player profiles. OFstats is the one OpenFront itself links from its FFA rating UI, so it is the most tightly coupled of the three, but all three serve the same job and the community treats them as interchangeable depending on taste. The one rule that applies to all of them is attribution: you must log in so the site ties the stats to your account. Logged out, a site that shows "your" stats by name is really showing whoever shares that name, which is the most common reason a player's third-party numbers look wrong even when nothing is actually broken.

## What the official ranked leaderboard actually tells you

The official ranked leaderboard is a public endpoint that returns the top players on each ranked ladder, and understanding its shape is the difference between skimming it and reading it. It is organized per ladder — 1v1, 2v2, and free-for-all — so the "top 50" you see is the top of a specific queue, not a global all-purpose ranking. Each entry is a flat set of fields rather than a page, and the live response returns a consistent schema you can read at a glance. The fields that matter are: `rank` (your position in that ladder), `elo` (your current rating), `peakElo` (the highest you have reached, a lifetime best), `wins`, `losses`, and `total` (the raw match counts that the win rate divides), plus `level`, `prestige`, and `legend`, which are progression badges rather than skill measures. The entry also carries your username and a public identifier, which is what the third-party sites use to link your public record to your profile.

The table below maps each field to what it actually answers, because reading the leaderboard is mostly knowing which column is a skill signal and which is decoration. The two columns you should treat as the skill core are `elo` and `peakElo`; the columns you should treat as raw context are `wins`, `losses`, and `total`; and the columns you should mostly ignore for the question "am I getting better" are `level`, `prestige`, and `legend`. `rank` is only meaningful inside its own ladder and its own refresh window, so a rank of 40 in 1v1 says nothing about your 2v2 standing.

| Field | What it is | What it tells you about skill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| rank | Position in this ladder | Relative only, within this ladder and refresh |
| elo | Current rating | The core skill number; read its swing per game |
| peakElo | Lifetime highest rating | Your best demonstrated level |
| wins / losses / total | Raw match counts | The base that win rate divides; normalize by mode and lobby |
| level | Player level | Progression, not skill |
| prestige | Prestige count | Progression, not skill |
| legend | Legend badge | Cosmetic recognition |

The refresh cadence and the per-ladder split are the two properties that most often confuse a first-time reader. The endpoint refreshes roughly every fifteen minutes, so treat any number you pull as a snapshot that is at most about a quarter hour stale, not a live read of the match you just played. And because each ladder is independent, your 1v1 `elo` and your 2v2 `elo` are separate numbers with separate leaderboards; a player who is strong in one and unknown in the other will show up near the top of one list and not on the other at all. When you open the leaderboard, the first move is to pick the ladder you actually play, then read `elo` and `peakElo` before anything else, and only then look at `total` to see how much evidence sits behind the number.

## Reading the ranked Elo: why the same win pays out differently

The single biggest mistake a new reader makes is treating the Elo change after a match as a fixed reward, as if a win is always worth the same number of points. It is not, and the swing is determined by who you played, not by whether you won. The community explains it plainly in the threads: if you are around 1700 and you beat a player near 1500, you gain a little, because the model already expected you to win; if you beat a player near 2000, you gain a lot, because you produced evidence the model said was unlikely. The mirror image is true too: losing to someone far below you costs more than losing to someone far above you, because the former is the result the model priced against you. This is the whole "read the swing, not the win/loss line" rule, and it is why two players with an identical win/loss record can be climbing at very different speeds.

Consider a concrete scenario. You are at 1700 and you queue three matches. Match one you win against an opponent rated around 1500: the swing is small, a modest gain, because beating a weaker player is the expected outcome. Match two you win against an opponent rated around 2000: the swing is large, because you beat a stronger field and the model updates its estimate of you sharply upward. Match three you lose to an opponent rated around 1400: the swing is a large negative, because you lost to a field the model thought was clearly weaker. After three matches your net win/loss is 2-1, a better-looking record than it should be, but the honest read of your growth is that your two wins included one genuinely strong one and one weak one, and your one loss was the expensive kind. A player who only looks at 2-1 sees a "good block"; a player who looks at the swings sees that the strong win did most of the climbing and the weak win and the costly loss roughly cancelled each other.

This also answers the question players ask about whether match length or the amount they built affects the points: the dominant input is the strength of the field relative to your rating, not a per-minute rate or a build score. If you are tempted to "wait until the last minute" to farm points, that does not work, because the model is not paying you for time on the map; it is updating from the outcome against an expected field. The practical habit that follows is to look at the rating change and the expected percentage on the result screen, not the win/loss line, and to treat a large positive swing against a hard field as the most valuable result of the block and a large negative against a weak field as the one to fix. For the deeper mechanics of how the FFA rating specifically computes its gain, the [FFA rating climb guide](/guides/ffa-rating-climb/) covers the placement adjustment and the high-uncertainty window in detail.

## Wins, losses, and total: why win rate alone misleads

Win rate is the number players reach for because it is a single, satisfying percentage, and that is exactly the problem: it is mode-blind and lobby-blind, so the same number can mean opposite things in different queues. The community puts it directly in the threads: an 8 percent win rate means different things depending on what you play. If those games are mostly team games, 8 percent is extremely low, because team games have a stable, roughly even split and a weak player does not hide behind team luck. If the 8 percent comes from free-for-all in small lobbies, it is bad in a different way, because small FFA is high-variance but a single player can still consistently place. If it comes from 100-plus-player lobbies, 8 percent is entirely reasonable, because in a very large FFA most players simply cannot win and finishing placement is the realistic goal. The number is only meaningful once you know the mode and the lobby size it came from, which is why you must read `wins`, `losses`, and `total` together with the context of which ladder produced them.

Work through a concrete scenario. Two players both show 30 percent win rate. Player A played mostly 1v1 ranked against a steady field: 30 percent in 1v1 means they are losing two of every three direct duels, which is a clear signal to review their own play, because 1v1 removes the noise of a large lobby. Player B played mostly 100-player FFA: 30 percent wins there is strong, because in a huge free-for-all a single player rarely wins and a third of the time is a top-tier result. If you read only "30 percent" for both, you wrongly tell Player A they are fine and Player B they are not. The raw counts do the discriminating: Player A's `total` is small and the mode is 1v1, so the percentage is a high-skill signal; Player B's `total` is large and the mode is big-lobby FFA, so the same percentage is a low bar cleared.

The habit that follows is to never report or act on a win rate without the mode and the sample size behind it. When you look at your own `total`, a very small one means the percentage is unstable and should not drive a decision yet; a very large one means it is a stable signal you can act on. And when you compare yourself to a leaderboard player, compare within the same ladder, because their 2v2 win rate says nothing about your 1v1. If you want a cleaner read of what your games actually look like from the inside rather than from an aggregate, the [replay-review guide](/guides/replay-review/) walks through how to watch your own matches and connect the numbers to specific decisions.

## FFA rating and placement: the number that separates winners from finishers

Free-for-all has a property that team modes do not: only one player can win, so a match produces a winner and a long tail of finishers. That makes the raw win count a blunt instrument, because a player who finishes in the top five in half of their games but rarely takes the final structure is playing differently — and often better than the raw win rate suggests — than a player who rarely places but flukes a win when the top contenders cancel each other out. The community points exactly at this: it is quite possible to have a lower win rate than someone else and still be the better player, because placing consistently against a strong field is the evidence that matters. This is why the FFA layer adds a tiered rating rather than relying on win count, and why reading your FFA rating is about placement and field strength, not just the win line.

The FFA rating is organized as a tier ladder that runs from Iron up through Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum, Diamond, Master, and finally Grandmaster. Every player begins with a short calibration stretch of about ten games that establishes their starting rating, and after that the ladder has divisions: the first seven tiers are each split into divisions I through III, which is why you will see a label like Silver II rather than just Silver. The rating display itself carries three things: the tier badge, the rating value with an uncertainty band around it (the ± you see on the result screen), and your peak. That uncertainty band is not noise to ignore; it is the model saying how confident it is in your number, and early on, while the band is wide, your rating moves a lot per game because every match carries a lot of new information. As the band tightens, the same games move the number less, which is normal and means you are converging on your true level.

The concrete scenario is this. You place third in a 20-player FFA. Your raw win/loss says a loss, because you did not take the final structure. But the FFA rating does not read it as a flat loss the way a team mode would: it looks at your placement, the strength of the field, and how much information this match added given your current uncertainty, and it applies a placement adjustment that costs you less than a last-place finish would have. That is the mechanic that turns "I finished third against a strong lobby" into a small cost or even a gain rather than a full loss, and it is the whole reason placement matters in FFA the way it does not in 1v1. If you want the exact gain-per-game model, the placement adjustment rules, and how the high-uncertainty window hands out large median swings, the [FFA rating climb guide](/guides/ffa-rating-climb/) covers all of it; here the point is simply that your FFA number is a placement-and-field rating, and the number you should read for "am I placing well" is the tier plus the finish line, not the win count.

## Peak Elo, level, prestige, and legend: the numbers that do not change your decision

A leaderboard entry carries more numbers than a player can usefully act on, and the discipline is to know which ones are decoration. Four fields stand out as progression or recognition rather than skill: `level`, `prestige`, `legend`, and the `peakElo` when you misread it as current form. Your `level` goes up with play and experience over a long time; it measures how much you have played, not how good you are at any moment, so two players at the same level can be years apart in skill. `prestige` is the count of times you have reset or climbed through your progression, and again it is a badge of longevity and commitment, not a live skill signal. The `legend` flag is a cosmetic recognition marker attached to certain accounts; it tells you a player is notable, not what their rating is. And `peakElo`, while genuinely useful, answers a different question than `elo`: it is your best demonstrated level, a lifetime best, so a player with a high peak and a lower current number has been better in the past than they are right now, which is the opposite of the signal you want if you are asking "where am I today."

The reason this matters is that players repeatedly conflate these with skill. A new player who sees a high-level, high-prestige account on the leaderboard assumes it is a strong account and reads its recent results as a benchmark for themselves, when the high level and prestige only say that person has played a lot. A player comparing their own `peakElo` to a leader's current `elo` mixes a lifetime best with a present rating and draws a false conclusion about the gap. The clean rule is to keep the skill core — `elo`, `peakElo` as a "where I have been," and the raw `total` for sample size — in one mental bucket, and the progression badges — `level`, `prestige`, `legend` — in another, and never let the second bucket answer the question "am I improving?" The one exception is that `peakElo` is worth tracking as a personal ceiling, because watching it rise over weeks is the most honest long-term proof of real improvement, as long as you read it as "my best so far" and not as "my current form."

The practical habit is to stop reading these four fields at all when you are making a decision, and to reserve them for the question "how long have I played and how notable am I," which is a different, softer question. If you are trying to understand what level and prestige actually reward and whether they are worth chasing for their own sake, the [player-levels guide](/guides/player-levels-xp-prestige/) covers the XP and prestige system directly. The point here is simply that they are not on the decision path: when you open your stats to decide what to fix, the numbers that change your mind are the rating, its swing, your placement, and your normalized win rate, and the badges can wait until you are done.

## When the numbers lag, freeze, or look wrong: refresh, attribution, and calibration

The most common complaint in the community threads is not that the stats are wrong but that they feel wrong: "my stats don't update there," "it won't move," "why did I lose points I was not expecting." Almost every one of these has a mundane cause once you know the three failure modes. The first is refresh lag. The official ranked leaderboard refreshes on roughly a fifteen-minute cadence, so a match you just finished is not on it yet, and if you pull the leaderboard twice in a row a few minutes apart you can get identical snapshots that look frozen. The fix is patience and cadence: do not treat a single pull as ground truth, and if a number "should have moved," wait out the refresh window and pull again before concluding anything is broken.

The second failure mode is attribution, and it is specific to the third-party sites. If you are logged out, OFstats, Trackerfront, and OFratings identify you by name, and names are not unique, so the site may be showing a different player who happens to share your name. The threads are explicit about the fix: log in so the site ties the stats to your account rather than to a name match. This is the most common reason a player's win rate, match count, or placement on a third-party site looks wildly wrong even though their in-game account is fine. The in-game account list does not have this problem, because it is scoped to your signed-in account, so if your third-party numbers disagree with your in-game history, the first thing to check is whether you were logged in.

The third failure mode is the calibration and uncertainty window, which is a real mechanic rather than a bug. New players and freshly placed FFA ratings sit in a wide-uncertainty stretch where the rating moves a lot per game because the model is still establishing you; the same thing happens when a rating has gone stale after a break. A player who plays one match after two weeks and sees a large swing has not found a bug; they are inside the wide-band window where a single match carries a lot of information. The counter for all three is a short diagnostic routine: first confirm the refresh window has passed, second confirm you are logged in on any third-party site, and third check whether you are in a calibration or high-uncertainty stretch. If the number still looks wrong after those three checks, the right move is to open the specific match in your in-game games list, look at its leaderboard and replay, and verify what the match actually recorded, because the per-match record is the ground truth that every aggregate is built from.

## A reading routine: which numbers to check and when, and what to do with them

Putting the pieces together, the goal is a short routine you can run before, during, and after a session so the numbers steer your play instead of just decorating it. Before you queue, spend two minutes on your current ladder's `elo` and `peakElo` and your recent `total`, so you know where you stand and how much evidence is behind the number. If your `total` is small, your percentages are unstable and you should be gathering matches, not analyzing; if your `total` is large, your numbers are stable signals you can act on. Decide which ladder you are going to focus this session, because mixing ladders in one block makes every aggregate you read afterward harder to interpret.

During the session, the number to watch is the per-game rating swing on the result screen, not the win/loss line. Treat a large positive swing against a hard field as the best result of the block and a large negative against a weak field as the one to fix, and note which situations produced the costly losses. For FFA, also note your placement, because finishing consistently near the top is the signal the win count hides, and a block where you place third and fourth but do not win can still be a climbing block once the placement adjustment is accounted for. If you are adjusting for mode or map, read the numbers within the mode you are actually playing: a 1v1 map change changes your 1v1 `elo` signal, and a FFA lobby-size change changes your FFA placement signal, so do not judge a 2v2 experiment by your 1v1 trend.

After the session, run the short audit: did my `total` grow, did my `peakElo` move, and did the swings from the block point at a specific fixable situation? If the answer points at a situation — an overextension, a late-game structure collapse, a bad early placement — then the numbers have earned their keep and the next step is to watch the specific matches where it happened, which is exactly what replay review is for. The whole point of the routine is that the stats are a steering input, not a scoreboard: `elo` and its swing tell you what to train, placement tells you how FFA is going, your normalized win rate tells you whether a mode is working, and the badges and the raw level are the things you ignore while you are actually deciding. If the routine ever produces a number you do not understand, loop back to the failure checks in the previous section before you act on it.
